Chapter 6, Carbon Policy History, 1988-2015: From Science to Paris.

Why this chapter exists.

The international climate-policy regime didn't spring fully-formed from the Paris Agreement in 2015. It evolved over 27 years through repeated negotiations, failures, compromises, and reinventions. The institutional architecture we have today, U.N.F.C.C.C., COPs, NDCs, carbon markets, MRV requirements, climate finance, all of it is layered on decades of prior work and was shaped by specific historical contingencies. Understanding why the system is the way it is requires understanding the path that produced it.

This chapter is unusual in this curriculum because it's primarily historical rather than analytical. The point isn't to memorize every COP decision but to internalize the patterns: how the regime started with science-driven optimism, encountered the limits of binding international law, fragmented in the 2000s, came close to collapse in 2009, and rebuilt itself on a different foundation in 2015. Each of those phases produced institutions and norms that still shape what's politically possible today.

I'll cover this in seven phases: the formative period (1979-1992) culminating in the U.N.F.C.C.C.; the Kyoto era (1992-2001); the Bush years and the Kyoto Protocol's troubled entry into force (2001-2007); the road to Copenhagen and its collapse (2007-2009); the rebuilding period (2010-2014); and the Paris breakthrough (2015). I'll end with a brief look at what the regime accomplished and didn't accomplish, which sets up the policy chapters that follow.

Part 1: The formative period (1979-1992).

Climate science arrives at policy.

Climate science is much older than climate policy. The greenhouse effect was identified by Joseph Fourier in 1824. CO2's infrared absorption was measured by John Tyndall in the 1860s. Svante Arrhenius calculated the temperature impact of doubled CO2 in 1896, within rough range of modern estimates. Charles Keeling began continuous measurement of atmospheric CO2 at Mauna Loa in 1958, producing the iconic "Keeling Curve" that documents the relentless rise from ~315 ppm in the late 1950s to ~431 ppm (as of April 2026; NOAA GML).

But climate science as a policy concern is much more recent. Through most of the 20th century, climate change was a curiosity studied by a small community of atmospheric scientists. The political question of whether to do anything about it didn't seriously arise until the late 1970s.

Three things changed in the late 1970s and 1980s:

1. Scientific consensus solidified. The 1979 World Climate Conference in Geneva, organized by the World Meteorological Organization, brought together climate scientists from across the world. The conference concluded that continued CO2 emissions risked "long-term climate change which is likely to be significant." The 1985 Villach Conference (Austria), jointly organized by WMO, UNEP, and ICSU, produced a stronger statement: that warming "may be expected to be of a magnitude greater than any in [recent geological time]."

2. Computer models became plausible. The 1980s saw rapid improvements in general circulation models, early climate models that could simulate the atmosphere's response to greenhouse gas forcing on supercomputers. Models from NCAR, GFDL, and other groups began producing increasingly consistent projections.

3. Public attention spiked, especially in 1988. That summer was extraordinarily hot in North America (the hottest U.S. summer on record at the time) and brought severe drought to large parts of the country. On June 23, 1988, NASA climate scientist James Hansen testified before the U.S. Senate Committee on Energy and Natural Resources that he was "99 percent confident" that warming was underway and that it was caused by human activity. The testimony made the front pages and is widely cited as the moment climate change entered the American political mainstream.

The I.P.C.C. and the path to Rio.

The institutional response came fast. In late 1988, the WMO and UNEP jointly established the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change to assess the scientific evidence on climate change and inform international negotiation. The UN General Assembly endorsed the I.P.C.C. in Resolution 43/53 (December 1988), with U.S. support, under the Reagan administration, which saw a science-only panel as a low-commitment way to address growing public concern.

The I.P.C.C.'s first task was to produce a comprehensive scientific assessment. The First Assessment Report, published in 1990, concluded that "emissions resulting from human activities are substantially increasing the atmospheric concentrations of the greenhouse gases" and that this "will enhance the greenhouse effect, resulting on average in an additional warming of the Earth's surface." FAR became the scientific basis for the negotiation of an international climate convention.

The negotiation took two years. The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change was opened for signature at the United Nations Conference on Environment and Development (the "Earth Summit") in Rio de Janeiro, June 3-14, 1992. It was signed by 154 countries and the EEC. The Convention entered into force on March 21, 1994 (after ratification by 50 countries) and now has near-universal membership.

What the U.N.F.C.C.C. actually says.

The U.N.F.C.C.C. is a framework convention, it sets general principles and creates institutional machinery, but doesn't impose specific emissions targets. Its key provisions:

Article 2 (Objective):
"The ultimate objective of this Convention... is to achieve... stabilization of greenhouse gas concentrations in the atmosphere at a level that would prevent dangerous anthropogenic interference with the climate system."

This is the founding objective of the entire international climate regime: avoiding dangerous anthropogenic interference. The Convention doesn't define what level is "dangerous", that became the central political question that ultimately led to the 2 degrees Celsius and 1.5 degrees Celsius targets.

Article 3 (Principles): Introduced the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities (CBDR-RC). Developed and developing countries have different obligations because (a) developed countries are responsible for most historical emissions, (b) developed countries have greater financial and technical capacity, and (c) developing countries' priorities are economic development and poverty eradication.

Annexes I and II: The Convention divided countries into two main groups:

Annex I countries: 36 original entries (35 states plus the EEC/E.U.), comprising both OECD members as of 1992 and economies in transition (post-Soviet states). The list has since grown to 43 parties through subsequent amendments. Annex I countries took on specific obligations to "[aim] to return... emissions of CO2 and other greenhouse gases not controlled by the Montreal Protocol to their 1990 levels" (Article 4.2).
Annex II countries: a subset of Annex I (the OECD members, excluding the post-Soviet transition economies). Took on additional obligations to provide financial resources and technology to developing countries.
Non-Annex I countries: developing countries. Had general obligations to report emissions and develop national programs, but no quantified emission targets.

The Annex I / non-Annex I divide is sometimes called the "firewall", and it has been the most contested structural feature of the international regime ever since. It assumed that developed countries would lead, with developing countries following only after their economic conditions caught up. By the 2000s, when China and India became major emitters, the firewall became politically unsustainable but legally entrenched.

Conference of the Parties: The U.N.F.C.C.C.'s supreme decision-making body. Meets annually starting with COP1 in Berlin (1995). Decisions are made by consensus, which means in practice that any single country can block. This consensus-rule has been one of the regime's recurring frustrations, single objectors (Saudi Arabia, Russia, U.S. at various times) have stalled major decisions repeatedly.

What the U.N.F.C.C.C. didn't say.

The U.N.F.C.C.C. famously avoided quantified emission targets. Article 4.2's "aim to return... to 1990 levels" was non-binding and ambiguous. This was a deliberate choice: the U.S. (under George H.W. Bush) had made clear that it would not sign a treaty with mandatory targets, and other countries decided that universal participation under a framework was more valuable than binding commitments by a smaller group.

This choice, framework first, specific commitments later, set the pattern for everything that followed. The Kyoto Protocol would add quantitative targets to the framework. The Paris Agreement would replace the targets with self-determined commitments. But the framework convention itself has remained the legal backbone of international climate policy for 30+ years.

Answers

Three things converged. Scientific consensus solidified through the 1979 World Climate Conference and the 1985 Villach statement; general circulation models became credible enough on 1980s supercomputers to project greenhouse gas-forced warming; and public attention spiked, crystallized by James Hansen's June 1988 Senate testimony during a record-hot U.S. summer. Science alone doesn't move politics, it took models that could project consequences plus a salient public moment to convert a century-old physics result into an agenda item, which is why the institutional response (I.P.C.C. 1988, U.N.F.C.C.C. 1992) came so fast afterward.

It was a deliberate feature, not an oversight. Defining "dangerous" quantitatively in 1992 would have required agreeing on a temperature or concentration threshold, and the science wasn't settled and the politics were impossible. Leaving it open let 154 countries sign a framework they could all accept, deferring the contested threshold question to later science and negotiation (it eventually became the 2 degrees Celsius and 1.5 degrees Celsius goals). The cost is that an undefined objective provides no enforceable standard; the benefit is that it got the regime built. It's the same "framework first, specifics later" logic that runs through the whole period.

The equity logic was CBDR-RC: developed countries had caused most accumulated emissions and had the most capacity to act, so they should lead while developing countries prioritized growth and poverty reduction. The problem baked in was that the Annex I / non-Annex I line was static, fixed to 1992 circumstances, while the emissions map was dynamic. Once China passed the U.S. as the largest emitter and India grew rapidly, a regime that permanently exempted all developing countries from quantified targets lost credibility with developed-country publics yet remained legally entrenched, consuming two decades of negotiating energy until Durban/Paris finally dismantled it.

This is the foundational trade-off of the regime, and it doesn't resolve cleanly. The case that it was right: universality kept everyone, including the U.S., China, and India, inside one framework with shared reporting norms and an institutional home (the COP), and arguably no binding 1992 treaty could have included the U.S. Senate anyway, so a smaller binding club would have excluded the largest economy and emitter. The case that it was wrong: three decades of framework-building coincided with emissions rising ~50 percent, so universality may have bought participation at the cost of urgency. The counterfactual binding treaty would likely have resembled Kyoto, real targets for a shrinking minority of emissions, U.S. absent, suggesting the binding-vs-broad tension was never escapable, only relocatable.

Part 2: The Kyoto Protocol (1992-1997).

The road to Kyoto.

After U.N.F.C.C.C. entered into force in 1994, attention turned immediately to negotiating a protocol with quantitative emission targets. The first COP (Berlin, 1995) produced the Berlin Mandate: an agreement that Annex I countries should take on legally-binding emission limits, while non-Annex I countries would not.

This was politically important and analytically contested. From a developing-country perspective, the Berlin Mandate codified the principle that the rich countries that had caused the problem should solve it first. From a developed-country perspective (especially the U.S. Senate), it created a structural problem: any treaty would impose costs on rich-country economies without imposing equivalent costs on rapidly-developing competitors like China and India.

In July 1997, the U.S. Senate passed the Byrd-Hagel Resolution by 95-0 (an extraordinary level of bipartisan agreement). The resolution stated that the U.S. should not sign any treaty that would (a) "result in serious harm to the economy of the United States" or (b) "mandate new commitments to limit or reduce greenhouse gas emissions for the Annex I Parties, unless the protocol or other agreement also mandates new specific scheduled commitments to limit or reduce greenhouse gas emissions for Developing Country Parties within the same compliance period."

Byrd-Hagel was a warning shot. The U.S. administration (Clinton-Gore) was negotiating in Kyoto with one eye on a Senate that had told them, by unanimous vote, that any developing-country-free agreement would not be ratified. That tension hung over the negotiations and was never resolved.

Kyoto adopted, December 1997.

The Kyoto Protocol was adopted at COP3 in December 1997. It established legally binding emission targets for Annex I countries for the period 2008-2012 (the "first commitment period"). The aggregate target: a 5.2 percent reduction below 1990 levels by 2008-2012.

Individual country targets:
E.U.-15 (as a bubble): -8 percent
U.S.: -7 percent
Japan: -6 percent
Canada: -6 percent
Russia, Ukraine: 0 percent (post-Soviet economic collapse meant their 1990 baseline was much higher than current emissions; "hot air")
Iceland, Australia, Norway: small increases allowed

The targets were binding under international law, a country that failed to meet its target would face consequences (later defined as needing to make up the shortfall in the second commitment period at a 30 percent penalty).

The Protocol covered six gases (the "Kyoto basket"): CO2, methane, nitrous oxide, HFCs, PFCs, sulfur hexafluoride. Compliance was measured against 1990 baselines (or 1995 for synthetic gases, where 1990 data was poor). global warming potential-100 was used to aggregate gases, initially with AR2 values, later updated to AR4 values for the second commitment period.

The flexible mechanisms.

Kyoto's most lasting innovation was its flexible mechanisms, market-based instruments to allow cost-efficient compliance. There were three:

1. International Emissions Trading (IET, Article 17). Annex I countries could trade emission allowances among themselves. A country with surplus allowances (Russia, with its post-Soviet economic collapse) could sell them to a country that exceeded its target (Japan). This was the antecedent of every cap-and-trade system in the world.

2. Joint Implementation (JI, Article 6). Annex I countries could earn emission reduction credits by financing emission reduction projects in other Annex I countries. JI was specifically designed for projects in Eastern European economies in transition, where reduction costs were lower than in Western Europe.

3. Clean Development Mechanism (CDM, Article 12). Annex I countries could earn credits ("Certified Emission Reductions," CERs) by financing emission reduction projects in non-Annex I countries. CDM was the most innovative and consequential mechanism. It allowed Annex I countries to meet part of their obligation through projects in developing countries, methane capture at a Chinese landfill, hydropower in Brazil, fuel-switching at an Indian cement plant, at lower cost than domestic reductions. CDM was the world's first global carbon offset market, and its rules, methodologies, and integrity controversies established the template that voluntary markets (Chapter 8) would later inherit, for better and for worse.

The flexible mechanisms were controversial from the start. Critics argued they let rich countries buy their way out of domestic action while developing countries took on the harder physical changes. Defenders argued they reduced compliance costs and channeled climate finance to developing countries. The compromise: a supplementarity principle stating that flexible-mechanism use must be "supplemental to domestic action." How much was "supplemental" was never quantified.

The Marrakech Accords (2001).

Kyoto needed extensive implementation rules before it could function. Negotiating those rules took four years and was nearly derailed by the U.S. withdrawal from the Protocol (next section). The Marrakech Accords (COP7, 2001) finalized the rules for:
Measuring and reporting emissions and removals
The flexible mechanisms (eligibility, project cycle, registry systems)
Compliance regime (the consequences for missing targets)
Reporting and review procedures

The Marrakech Accords became the operational backbone of the Kyoto regime. They were also remarkable for being negotiated without the U.S., which had withdrawn from the Protocol earlier that year.

Socratic prompt: The U.S. Senate passed Byrd-Hagel 95-0 in July 1997, five months before Kyoto was adopted. Knowing that the U.S. Senate had effectively pre-rejected any developing-country-free agreement, why did the Clinton administration sign Kyoto in 1997? What's the strategic logic, and what's the cost?

Answers

A framework convention sets out general principles, objectives, and institutional machinery (the COP, reporting duties) without imposing specific quantified obligations; a protocol is a subsequent instrument that adds concrete commitments to that framework. The U.N.F.C.C.C. was deliberately structured this way because the U.S. under George H.W. Bush refused to sign a treaty with mandatory targets, and negotiators judged that near-universal participation under a flexible framework was more valuable than binding commitments from a smaller group. The "framework first, commitments later" sequence let the world establish the legal backbone in 1992 and defer the contested target-setting to Kyoto.

CBDR-RC holds that all countries share responsibility for the climate but that obligations differ according to historical emissions, financial and technical capacity, and development needs, so developed countries, having caused most accumulated emissions and possessing greater capacity, should act first. The Annex I / non-Annex I "firewall" operationalized this. It became unsustainable by the 2000s because the emissions map changed: China passed the U.S. as the largest emitter and India grew rapidly, so a regime that exempted all developing countries from quantified targets no longer matched reality and lost credibility with developed-country publics, even as it remained legally entrenched.

Market mechanisms were included to lower the cost of compliance: if a ton of CO2 can be cut more cheaply in one place than another, trading and crediting let countries achieve the same environmental outcome for less money, which in principle raises the ambition that is politically affordable. They solve the cost-efficiency problem and channel finance to developing countries (via CDM). But they create an integrity problem, credits may not represent real, additional reductions, and rich countries can substitute cheap foreign credits for domestic action. The unresolved "supplementarity" principle, which said offsets must be supplemental but never quantified how much, captures exactly this tension.

The strategic logic was that signing kept the U.S. at the table and preserved diplomatic leadership: walking away from Kyoto entirely would have collapsed the negotiation and ceded influence, whereas signing let the administration shape the rules (especially the flexible mechanisms it favored) and bet on building domestic and international momentum that might later make ratification or a renegotiated deal possible. The cost was a credibility gap, committing internationally to a treaty the Senate had unanimously pre-rejected risked exactly the outcome that followed: a signed-but-unratified treaty, U.S. withdrawal in 2001, and lasting doubt about whether U.S. negotiators could deliver what they promised.

Part 3: The Bush years and Kyoto's troubled entry into force (2001-2007).

U.S. withdrawal.

In March 2001, two months after taking office, President George W. Bush announced that the United States would not seek ratification of the Kyoto Protocol. The administration's stated reasons: (a) Kyoto's failure to require commitments from developing countries (Byrd-Hagel-consistent), (b) the economic cost to the U.S., and (c) scientific uncertainty about the magnitude of climate change.

This was a watershed moment. The U.S. was the world's largest emitter and the largest economy. Without U.S. participation, Kyoto's coverage of global emissions dropped from ~60 percent to ~30 percent. The regime was thrown into crisis.

The Bush administration's alternative was the Asia-Pacific Partnership on Clean Development and Climate (2005), a voluntary technology-cooperation framework with Australia, China, India, Japan, and South Korea. The partnership was widely viewed as an alternative track meant to weaken Kyoto's centrality. It produced few concrete outcomes and was wound down by 2011.

Russia ratifies, Kyoto enters into force.

The Kyoto Protocol required ratification by 55 countries representing at least 55 percent of Annex I emissions to enter into force. With the U.S. (responsible for ~36 percent of Annex I emissions) out, this required ratification by virtually every other major Annex I country plus Russia.

Russia hesitated for three years. Russian emissions had collapsed after the Soviet dissolution; it had massive "hot air" allowances it could sell, but ratification was politically contested at home. The breakthrough came in 2004, reportedly tied to the E.U.'s support for Russian WTO accession. Russia ratified Kyoto in November 2004, and the Protocol entered into force on February 16, 2005.

The CDM boom and bust.

With Kyoto in force, the Clean Development Mechanism became operational at scale. The first CER (Certified Emission Reduction) credits were issued in 2005. Demand came primarily from European countries needing to meet their Kyoto obligations and from the newly-launched E.U. Emissions Trading System, which began operations in January 2005 and allowed E.U. companies to use CERs for partial compliance.

CDM grew explosively. By 2012, over 7,500 projects had been registered, with hundreds of millions of CERs issued. The biggest project types:
HFC-23 destruction at refrigerant manufacturing plants in China and India. HFC-23 is a byproduct of HCFC-22 production with global warming potential of ~14,800, extremely cheap to destroy, very high credit yield.
nitrous oxide abatement at adipic acid and nitric acid plants in developing countries.
Coal-mine methane capture in China.
Renewable energy (small hydro, wind) in China and India.
Landfill gas capture in various countries.

The HFC-23 and nitrous oxide projects together accounted for roughly half of issued CERs in the early years. They were also the most controversial. Because the credits were so cheap to generate, there were credible accusations that some plants had been built to produce HFC-23 specifically in order to earn CDM credits for destroying it. The integrity of the credit system was challenged. The CDM Executive Board eventually capped these project types and (in 2013) developed new methodologies to address the perverse incentive, but by then a substantial volume of credits had been issued.

CDM also distributed unevenly. China hosted roughly half of all CDM projects; India another 20 percent; Brazil ~10 percent. Sub-Saharan Africa, despite being most climate-vulnerable, hosted less than 3 percent, because the projects there were typically smaller, harder to verify, and faced higher transaction costs. The geographic skew became a major equity criticism.

By 2012, CER prices collapsed from 20 dollars+ per tonne of CO2-equivalent in the mid-2000s to under 1 dollars per tonne of CO2-equivalent, as supply outpaced demand and the E.U. E.T.S. phased out the use of CERs from new HFC-23 and adipic acid projects. The CDM essentially shut down as a market mechanism, though it formally continues to issue credits for legacy projects.

The lessons from CDM, what works, what doesn't, what creates integrity problems, what creates the wrong incentives, are foundational for understanding the voluntary carbon market controversies we'll cover in Chapter 8.

E.U. E.T.S. launch (January 2005).

The European Union Emissions Trading System launched January 2005. It was the world's first major cap-and-trade system at scale and remains the largest. Phase 1 (2005-2007) covered approximately 12,000 large emitting facilities in 25 E.U. member states, representing ~45 percent of E.U. CO2 emissions. Allowances were free-allocated based on historical emissions ("grandfathering").

Phase 1 was a learning phase and had visible problems: allowances were over-allocated, prices collapsed near zero by 2007 when it became clear caps wouldn't bind, and several integrity issues (large windfall profits to grandfathered allocations, exemptions for certain sectors). But Phase 1 established the institutional infrastructure, registries, MRV requirements, allowance tracking, compliance procedures, that all subsequent emissions trading systems have copied. We'll dive into the E.U. E.T.S. in detail in Chapter 7.

Bali Action Plan (2007).

The Bali Action Plan at COP13 (December 2007) launched the negotiation of a post-2012 climate agreement. It set up a two-track negotiation process:
One track under the Kyoto Protocol (AWG-KP), to define second-commitment-period targets for Annex I countries.
Another track under the U.N.F.C.C.C. (AWG-LCA), to develop a broader agreement covering all countries.

The Bali Action Plan called for the negotiation to conclude in two years, with a target date of COP15 in Copenhagen (December 2009). Setting that deadline turned out to be a serious mistake.

The CDM let Annex I countries earn credits by funding emission-reduction projects in developing countries. What problem was it designed to solve, and why was that attractive to both sides?
HFC-23 destruction projects earned roughly half of early CER volume, and there were credible claims that plants were built specifically to produce HFC-23 so they could be paid to destroy it. Name the precise design flaw, and why it's a perverse incentive rather than just fraud.

Socratic prompt: The CDM's four signature problems, additionality (was the reduction real?), perverse incentives (HFC-23), geographic concentration (half in China, <3 percent in Africa), and supplementarity that was never quantified, all recur in the voluntary carbon market (Chapter 8) and Article 6. If these flaws were visible by 2012, why do they keep reappearing? Which are fixable with better rules, and which are inherent to paying for a counterfactual?

Answers

The CDM solved a cost-efficiency problem: a ton of CO2 avoided is worth the same to the atmosphere wherever it happens, and reductions were far cheaper in developing countries than in Western economies. So it let Annex I countries meet part of their Kyoto obligations more cheaply (attractive to them) while channeling climate finance and technology to developing countries that had no targets of their own (attractive to them). It was the first global offset market, and that buyer-and-host alignment is exactly what made it grow explosively, and what made its integrity so hard to police.

The flaw is crediting a counterfactual destruction of a super-pollutant whose abatement cost is tiny relative to the credit it earns. HFC-23 has a global warming potential near 14,800, so destroying one ton yields ~14,800 credits, while destruction costs pennies, meaning the credit revenue could exceed the value of the underlying HCFC-22 product itself. That inverts the incentive: a plant can be worth more for the waste gas it can be paid to destroy than for its actual product, so rational operators expand HCFC-22 production to generate destroyable HFC-23. It's a perverse incentive, not mere fraud, because the operators are following the rules exactly as written, the rules themselves reward the wrong behavior.

Demand for CERs came almost entirely from policy: Annex I Kyoto obligations and E.U. E.T.S. compliance buying. Supply, meanwhile, ballooned as thousands of projects registered. When the E.U. E.T.S. restricted the use of CERs from HFC-23 and adipic-acid projects, and Kyoto's first commitment period wound down with no robust successor demand, the demand side collapsed while supply kept arriving, so prices fell from 20 dollars+ to under 1 dollars. The lesson: a credit's price is only as durable as the policy that creates demand for it. A crediting market with no underlying compliance obligation (or a wavering one) has no price floor, which is exactly the fragility the voluntary market inherited.

They keep reappearing because three of the four are not bugs but structural features of paying for an avoided outcome. Additionality requires proving a counterfactual, that the reduction wouldn't have happened anyway, which is fundamentally unobservable and judgment-laden, so it can be tightened but never solved. Supplementarity (how much offsetting is "too much") is a political line that no technical rule settles. Perverse incentives are the most fixable: once diagnosed, you cap or re-baseline the specific project type (as the CDM eventually did). Geographic concentration is partly fixable with targeted support but sticky because transaction costs favor large, easy projects. They recur because each new market (voluntary carbon market, Article 6) re-creates the same buyer-host cost-efficiency logic and rediscovers that the counterfactual at its core can't be made fully rigorous, the reason Chapter 8's integrity crises rhyme so closely with the CDM's.

Part 4: The road to Copenhagen and its collapse (2007-2009).

The expectations.

The two years between Bali and Copenhagen saw enormous build-up of expectation. The new Obama administration (inaugurated January 2009) was clearly more committed to climate action than its predecessor. The I.P.C.C.'s Fourth Assessment Report (2007) had strengthened the scientific case. The Stern Review (U.K., 2006) had argued that aggressive mitigation was economically sensible.

The expectation was that Copenhagen would produce a comprehensive successor to Kyoto, covering all countries (developed and developing), with binding emission targets, an aggressive financial-assistance package, and a unified governance framework. NGOs and many governments treated it as humanity's last chance to stay within 2 degrees Celsius of warming.

The collapse.

What actually happened at COP15 (Copenhagen, December 2009) was a near-total breakdown. The two-week conference produced no comprehensive agreement. Instead, in the final hours, a small group of heads of state (Obama, Wen Jiabao, Manmohan Singh, Lula, and Jacob Zuma) negotiated a brief political document, the Copenhagen Accord, directly, then asked the broader COP plenary to adopt it. The plenary "took note of" the Accord rather than formally adopting it, after objections from a handful of Latin American countries (Bolivia, Venezuela, Cuba, Nicaragua).

The Copenhagen Accord was three pages. It:
Recognized the goal of limiting warming to 2 degrees Celsius.
Asked Annex I countries to submit national emission reduction targets by January 31, 2010.
Asked non-Annex I countries to submit national mitigation actions.
Promised 30 billion dollars in "fast-start" climate finance for 2010-2012 and 100 billion dollars/year by 2020.
Created two new institutions: the Green Climate Fund and the Technology Mechanism.

What it didn't do: produce a binding successor to Kyoto. The Accord was politically negotiated, but legally it was just a "noted" document with no formal status.

Why Copenhagen failed.

In hindsight, Copenhagen's failure had several causes:

1. The deadline was unrealistic. Two years (Bali to Copenhagen) wasn't enough time to negotiate a binding global agreement on a topic with this much complexity, especially with the post-2008 financial crisis disrupting government attention.

2. The Annex I / non-Annex I firewall was hardening. By 2009, China had passed the U.S. as the largest emitter. India was growing rapidly. The premise that only developed countries should take quantified targets was no longer credible to developed-country publics, but was constitutionally sacred to developing countries. The negotiations couldn't bridge this.

3. The negotiating structure was broken. The two-track Bali architecture (AWG-KP for Kyoto, AWG-LCA for everyone) meant that any agreement required reconciliation across tracks. The reconciliation never happened.

4. The "Climategate" controversy in November 2009 released stolen emails from East Anglia's Climatic Research Unit. The release was a politically-motivated effort to discredit climate science weeks before Copenhagen. It didn't actually contain anything damning, but it dominated media coverage in the lead-up and gave skeptics political ammunition.

5. The high stakes created brittleness. The narrative that Copenhagen was "humanity's last chance" meant that anything less than complete success was framed as catastrophic failure. There was no room for partial progress.

The collapse was traumatic for the climate-policy community. NGOs and many participants treated Copenhagen as a fundamental failure of the multilateral process. The mood through 2010 was bleak.

What survived.

Looking back, Copenhagen accomplished more than it seemed in the moment:

The 2 degrees Celsius goal was politically endorsed at the highest level. Even non-binding endorsement reframed the global conversation.
The pledges system, countries submitting their own targets rather than negotiating them top-down, was a structural innovation that would become the foundation of Paris.
The 100B dollars/yr climate finance commitment became a touchstone that has driven subsequent finance negotiations.
The Green Climate Fund and Technology Mechanism were created and operational.

In effect, Copenhagen failed to produce the kind of agreement it was aiming for (Kyoto 2.0) but inadvertently produced the architecture for a different kind of agreement (Paris). At the time, this wasn't visible.

The Kyoto Protocol entered into force in February 2005, eight years after its adoption. What does this delay tell you about the limits of international law as a climate policy instrument?
Copenhagen failed to produce a binding agreement. With hindsight, was the failure a setback or a necessary clearing-of-the-deck for a different kind of agreement? Make the case both ways.
Socratic prompt: If you had been a senior climate negotiator in 2008, what would you have argued for between the two tracks (a Kyoto-style binding regime with U.S. opt-out, or a comprehensive pledge-based regime)? What would you have given up to get the other?

Answers

The eight-year gap (1997 adoption to 2005 entry into force) shows that binding international law is slow, fragile, and hostage to domestic ratification politics. Kyoto needed 55 parties representing 55 percent of Annex I emissions; with the U.S. (~36 percent of Annex I emissions) refusing to ratify, the treaty's fate hinged on Russia, which held out for three years until ratification became tied to its WTO accession. The lesson is that a treaty can be legally binding and still take most of a decade to take effect, and even then cover a shrinking share of global emissions, because international law depends on voluntary national ratification it cannot compel.

As a setback: Copenhagen squandered two years of momentum, traumatized the policy community, hardened the firewall dispute, and produced only a "noted" three-page document with no binding successor to Kyoto, a real failure against its stated goal. As necessary clearing-of-the-deck: it killed the unworkable attempt at a top-down "Kyoto 2.0," and in doing so it surfaced the pledge-based architecture (countries submitting their own targets), endorsed the 2 degrees Celsius goal at head-of-state level, and created the Green Climate Fund, exactly the components Paris later built on. The honest reading is that it failed at the kind of agreement it sought while inadvertently inventing the kind that succeeded.

This is a genuine trade-off rather than a question with a settled answer. Arguing for a Kyoto-style binding regime preserves legal enforceability and a clear standard of accountability, but you would have to accept U.S. absence (and likely Canadian, Japanese, Russian disengagement), shrinking emissions coverage, and a hardening firewall, buying rigor at the cost of reach. Arguing for a comprehensive pledge-based regime buys universal participation, including the U.S., China, and India, and a ratchet that can raise ambition over time, but you give up binding achievement and any enforcement against weak or unmet targets. The decisive variables are whether you believe enforceability or participation produces more real-world abatement, and how much you weight near-term legal certainty against long-term inclusiveness.

Part 5: Rebuilding (2010-2014).

Cancún and the building blocks.

COP16 in Cancún (December 2010) was a deliberately low-expectation conference. After Copenhagen, the goal was to demonstrate that the UN process could still function and to formalize the elements of the Copenhagen Accord into U.N.F.C.C.C. decisions.

The Cancún Agreements accomplished this:
Formally adopted the 2 degrees Celsius goal (with a review of whether 1.5 degrees Celsius should be considered).
Inscribed Annex I and non-Annex I pledges from the Copenhagen Accord into the U.N.F.C.C.C. system.
Formally established the Green Climate Fund.
Created the Cancún Adaptation Framework.
Established the Technology Executive Committee and Climate Technology Centre and Network.

Cancún was modest by design. Its success, demonstrating that the UN process could produce useful outcomes, was as much about restoring confidence as about substantive progress.

Durban Platform (2011).

COP17 in Durban (December 2011) made a more consequential move. The Durban Platform for Enhanced Action committed parties to negotiate, by 2015, a new climate agreement that would:
"Be applicable to all parties", explicitly closing the Annex I / non-Annex I firewall.
"Have legal force", though the specific legal form was left undefined.
"Come into effect from 2020", providing a clear timeline.

The "applicable to all parties" language was the breakthrough. Developing countries (especially China, India, Brazil) accepted that the next agreement would apply to them in some form. This was a substantive shift from the Berlin Mandate (1995), which had been the legal basis for the developing-country exemption from quantified targets.

The Durban Platform also created the Ad Hoc Working Group on the Durban Platform for Enhanced Action, the negotiating body that would produce the Paris Agreement.

Doha Amendment and the second commitment period.

COP18 in Doha (December 2012) adopted the Doha Amendment to the Kyoto Protocol, creating a second commitment period (2013-2020) for Annex I countries that chose to participate. Some major countries did not, Canada had withdrawn from Kyoto in 2011, Japan and Russia announced they would not take on second-period commitments, and Australia signed up only to a weak target.

The Doha Amendment covered only about 14 percent of global emissions in its second commitment period. It was effectively a transitional arrangement, with the main negotiating effort focused on the post-2020 Paris Agreement.

The Warsaw decisions (2013).

COP19 in Warsaw (November 2013) introduced the concept of "intended nationally determined contributions", the structural form that would become the basis for Paris. Each country would prepare and publish its own contribution, ahead of COP21 in Paris. The contributions would be self-determined, not negotiated.

Warsaw also established the Warsaw International Mechanism for Loss and Damage, addressing climate impacts that exceed what countries can adapt to. This was deeply contested, developed countries resisted any framing that implied legal liability for historical emissions, while vulnerable developing countries insisted on a structured mechanism. The compromise was procedurally creative but substantively limited.

Lima Call for Climate Action (2014).

COP20 in Lima (December 2014) produced the Lima Call for Climate Action, which:
Confirmed the INDC process and provided guidance on what each country's submission should include.
Reflected the principle of "common but differentiated responsibilities, in light of different national circumstances", a wording that preserved CBDR but added the "national circumstances" qualifier that softened the bifurcated regime.
Set up the structure for Paris.

By the end of 2014, the architecture of the Paris Agreement was largely in place: bottom-up pledges (INDCs/NDCs), universal coverage, "applicable to all parties," anchored to the 2 degrees Celsius goal. The negotiation in 2015 was about finalizing the details and producing a legal text that could be adopted.

Stop-and-check 6.E.

Cancún (2010) was "deliberately low-expectation." After the trauma of Copenhagen, why might lowering expectations have been the strategically correct move for the UN process?
The Durban Platform (2011) committed to an agreement "applicable to all parties." Trace what had to change between the 1995 Berlin Mandate and 2011 for that phrase to become acceptable to China, India, and Brazil.
Warsaw (2013) introduced "intended nationally determined contributions". Why is the shift from negotiated targets (Kyoto) to self-determined contributions the single most important structural innovation of this period?
Socratic prompt: The Warsaw International Mechanism for Loss and Damage was created in 2013, but developed countries insisted it imply no legal liability for historical emissions. Why is "loss and damage" the hardest equity question in climate policy, harder than mitigation finance, and what makes the liability framing so radioactive for developed countries?

Answers

Because Copenhagen's failure was partly a failure of expectations: framing it as "humanity's last chance" meant anything short of a comprehensive binding treaty registered as catastrophe, which damaged confidence in the entire multilateral process. Cancún's modest, deliverable agenda, formalizing the 2 degrees Celsius goal, inscribing the Copenhagen pledges, standing up the Green Climate Fund, let the UN process demonstrate it could still produce useful outcomes. Restoring institutional credibility was itself the substantive goal; you cannot negotiate an ambitious agreement on top of a process everyone has written off, so rebuilding confidence with achievable wins was the necessary precondition for Durban and Paris.

The Berlin Mandate (1995) had hard-coded the firewall: binding targets for Annex I, none for developing countries. By 2011 the factual premise had eroded, China had passed the U.S. as the largest emitter and India was growing fast, so developed-country publics would no longer accept a regime that exempted the major emerging emitters. At the same time, the bottom-up pledge architecture surfaced at Copenhagen offered emerging economies a face-saving path: "applicable to all parties" without top-down imposed targets, since each country would set its own. The combination, eroded factual basis for the firewall plus a self-determination mechanism that protected sovereignty, is what let China, India, and Brazil accept universal coverage they had treated as non-negotiable.

Because it changed the theory of change of the entire regime. Kyoto's negotiated, binding targets could only ever apply to countries willing to accept them, a shrinking minority of emissions, and the negotiation was zero-sum (every country wanted others to do more). Self-determined contributions flipped this: every country can join because every country sets its own level, making universal participation possible, and the difficulty moves from "agreeing on targets" to "ratcheting up self-set ones over time." It trades enforceability for breadth, and it's the innovation that made a genuinely global agreement achievable, every feature of Paris (the ratchet, the stocktake, the transparency framework) exists to make self-determined pledges add up to something over time.

Loss and damage is the hardest equity question because it concerns harm that has already occurred or is unavoidable, impacts beyond what adaptation can prevent, so unlike mitigation finance (which funds future action) it points backward toward responsibility for emissions already emitted. That is precisely what makes liability framing radioactive: a formal acknowledgment of legal liability for historical emissions could expose the largest historical emitters to potentially unbounded compensation claims, litigated indefinitely, with no natural ceiling. Developed countries will fund loss and damage as an act of solidarity (the eventual COP27/28 compromise) but fight any wording, "compensation," "liability", that converts a voluntary contribution into an enforceable debt. The radioactivity is the open-endedness: mitigation finance can be pledged in bounded sums, but liability for past harm has no defined limit.

Part 6: Paris (2015).

What Paris does.

The Paris Agreement was adopted at COP21 on December 12, 2015, after two weeks of intensive negotiation. It was opened for signature in April 2016 and entered into force on November 4, 2016, extraordinarily fast for an international treaty, achieved because it had been designed for broad and rapid ratification.

The Paris Agreement contains several core elements:

Article 2 (Purpose): Limit warming to "well below 2 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels and pursuing efforts to limit the temperature increase to 1.5 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels." This is the first binding international agreement to reference 1.5 degrees Celsius, included largely at the insistence of small island developing states and other climate-vulnerable countries.

Article 4: Each party "shall prepare, communicate and maintain successive nationally determined contributions." NDCs are self-determined, each country decides its own target. NDCs must be updated every five years (the "ratchet mechanism") and each successive NDC must represent a progression beyond the previous one. There's no requirement that NDCs be sufficient to meet the temperature goal, only that they progress.

Article 6 (Cooperative approaches): Introduces three new market mechanisms (Article 6.2 cooperative approaches, Article 6.4 sustainable development mechanism, Article 6.8 non-market approaches). These are the successors to Kyoto's flexible mechanisms, redesigned for a world of universal participation. Article 6 rules were finalized at COP26 (Glasgow, 2021), six years after the Paris Agreement itself.

Article 7 (Adaptation): Creates a global goal on adaptation. Recognizes adaptation as a global responsibility, not just a national one.

Article 8 (Loss and damage): References loss and damage, but explicitly does not provide a basis for liability or compensation. This compromise was bitterly fought.

Article 9 (Finance): Developed countries "shall provide financial resources to assist developing country Parties." The 100 billion dollars/year goal from Copenhagen was referenced in the accompanying COP decision but not in the binding treaty text.

Article 13 (Transparency): Creates an Enhanced Transparency Framework, common rules for reporting and reviewing emissions and progress on NDCs, applicable to all parties (with flexibility for capacity-constrained developing countries).

Article 14 (Global Stocktake): Every five years (starting in 2023), parties take stock of collective progress toward the Agreement's goals. The first Global Stocktake concluded at COP28 in Dubai (December 2023).

What's binding and what's not.

The Paris Agreement is binding under international law, but its binding character is procedural rather than substantive:

Binding:
The obligation to prepare and communicate NDCs.
The obligation to pursue domestic mitigation measures to achieve the NDCs.
The obligation to participate in the transparency framework.
The obligation to participate in the global stocktake.

Not binding:
The content of NDCs (countries set their own targets).
Achievement of NDCs (no legal consequence for missing targets).
The specific level of financial support from developed to developing countries.
The 1.5 degrees Celsius and 2 degrees Celsius temperature goals (aspirational, not legally enforceable obligations on parties).

This was the trade-off Paris made: universal coverage and rapid ratification in exchange for non-binding substantive commitments. Critics argued (and still argue) that this trade-off was too weak, there's no enforcement mechanism for inadequate NDCs. Defenders argued that universal participation with a ratchet mechanism would produce more aggregate ambition over time than a smaller treaty with binding targets.

The empirical question of which side was right is still being adjudicated. The Paris Agreement has produced universal participation and a five-year ratchet cycle. Whether the aggregate ambition has been sufficient is the topic of every successive COP and the subject of substantial controversy.

What Paris replaced.

Comparing Paris to Kyoto:

The structural shift is enormous: from a small-club approach with binding obligations to a universal-participation approach with procedural obligations. The reason for the shift was the lesson of the 1997-2015 period: binding obligations on a subset of countries had not produced sufficient action and could not be politically extended. A different theory of change, universal participation with a ratchet, was the negotiating space that remained.

Stop-and-check 6.F.

The Durban Platform (2011) committed parties to negotiate an agreement "applicable to all parties." Why was this a breakthrough, and what political shift had to occur for it to be possible?
The Paris Agreement is binding on procedure but not on substance, countries must submit NDCs, but they choose what to put in them. Is this stronger or weaker than Kyoto? Make the case both ways.
Article 6 of Paris addresses market mechanisms but its rules were only finalized six years later, at COP26. Why does it take so long to work out rules for international carbon markets?
Socratic prompt: If Paris allows countries to set their own targets, what prevents them from setting trivially easy ones? What stops a country from announcing "we'll reduce emissions 5 percent by 2050" and then meeting that target effortlessly?

Answers

The breakthrough was the phrase "applicable to all parties," which committed the next agreement to cover developing countries as well as developed ones, explicitly dismantling the Annex I / non-Annex I firewall that had structured the regime since the 1995 Berlin Mandate. The political shift that made it possible was the major emerging economies, especially China, India, and Brazil, accepting that the future agreement would apply to them in some form. Given that these countries had treated their exemption from quantified targets as near-sacred, their willingness to negotiate universal coverage was the concession that unlocked Paris.

Stronger case: Kyoto's binding targets covered only Annex I countries and ultimately a shrinking share of global emissions, with the U.S. never ratifying; Paris is weaker on paper per country but achieves universal participation plus a five-year ratchet requiring each NDC to progress, so aggregate ambition can rise over time across all major emitters. Weaker case: Kyoto imposed legally binding achievement with penalties, whereas Paris has no enforcement for missing or setting feeble targets, only transparency and review. The honest answer is that Paris trades substantive enforceability for breadth of participation and a mechanism for escalating ambition, and whether that trade produces more real abatement is still being adjudicated.

International carbon-market rules are slow because they must prevent double counting across sovereign accounting systems, define what counts as a real and additional reduction, establish how credits transfer between countries' NDCs (corresponding adjustments), and reconcile the interests of buyers, sellers, and host countries, all under the U.N.F.C.C.C.'s consensus rule, where any single party can block. Article 6 also had to absorb the hard lessons of the CDM's integrity failures. Designing a system robust enough to avoid those problems, acceptable to nearly 200 parties, and technically workable took until COP26 in 2021, six years after Paris itself.

Several features constrain trivial targets, though none is a hard legal cap. The ratchet mechanism requires each successive NDC to progress beyond the last, so a country cannot keep resubmitting the same weak pledge indefinitely. The Enhanced Transparency Framework and Global Stocktake expose each country's target and performance to international scrutiny, creating reputational and diplomatic pressure ("naming and shaming"). Peer expectation, trade and finance linkages, and domestic political accountability add further pressure. But because achievement is not legally enforced, the real discipline is political and reputational rather than juridical, which is precisely the criticism leveled at Paris and the reason its adequacy remains contested.

Part 7: What the 1988-2015 period accomplished, and didn't.

The accomplishments.

The 1988-2015 period created the institutional architecture of international climate policy from scratch. Specific accomplishments:

Scientific consensus institutionalized. The I.P.C.C. has produced six full assessment cycles. Climate science is now a globally-organized field with strong peer review, broad participation, and policy-relevant outputs.
Universal political framework. U.N.F.C.C.C. has 198 parties, essentially every country in the world. The Conference of the Parties has met annually for 30 years. Climate is permanently on the global political agenda.
Binding international law. Both Kyoto and Paris are binding treaties under international law. The legal structure has been tested and held.
Carbon markets institutionalized. The CDM created the first global carbon market with rigorous (if imperfect) verification standards. The E.U. E.T.S. demonstrated cap-and-trade at scale. Both established methodological and institutional templates copied worldwide.
Climate finance infrastructure. Green Climate Fund, Adaptation Fund, Climate Investment Funds, Loss and Damage Fund (later). The plumbing for transfers of climate finance from developed to developing countries exists.
MRV standards. Measurement, reporting, and verification standards for emissions are globally harmonized, primarily through I.P.C.C. inventory guidelines (originally 1996, updated 2006, refined 2019).

The shortfalls.

The same period also has serious shortfalls:

Emissions kept growing. Despite 27 years of international climate policy, global total greenhouse gas emissions in 2015 were roughly 50 percent higher than in 1990 ( approximately 32.8 leads to approximately 49 gigatonnes of CO2-equivalent, EDGAR). The regime's stated objective, preventing dangerous anthropogenic interference, has not been met.
The Annex I / non-Annex I firewall fractured the regime. Between Kyoto and Paris, the regime spent most of its energy trying to handle the political problem of differentiated obligations. By the time Paris resolved it, two decades had passed.
Climate finance fell short. The 100B dollars/yr Copenhagen pledge was met for the first time in 2022 (per OECD accounting, released May 2024: 115.9B dollars); the 2021 outturn was 89.6B dollars, still below the target. The substantive impact has been contested, much of the "finance" has been counted from loans, market-rate investments, and other instruments that critics argue shouldn't count.
Market mechanisms have had integrity problems. The CDM had serious issues (HFC-23 perverse incentives, geographic concentration, additionality concerns). The voluntary market that grew alongside it (Chapter 8) has had even worse problems. Article 6 has been slow to operationalize.
The architectural choice for non-binding NDCs traded enforcement for participation. Whether the trade-off was wise is being adjudicated by the empirical record.

What came next.

The post-Paris period (2016-present) is the subject of subsequent chapters. The very brief summary: NDCs were submitted, the U.S. withdrew (2017) and rejoined (2021) and withdrew again (2025), Article 6 rules were negotiated (Glasgow 2021), loss and damage funding was operationalized (Sharm el-Sheikh 2022; Dubai 2023), the first Global Stocktake concluded with calls for "transitioning away from fossil fuels" (Dubai 2023), and COP30 (Belém, 2025) navigated rising political opposition to climate action in major economies. The regime persists; whether it's adequate is the central debate.

What this chapter simplified.

1. The I.P.C.C.'s institutional politics. I described the I.P.C.C. as primarily a scientific body. It is, but it's also intensely political, the Summary for Policymakers of each report is negotiated line-by-line by governments. There's a regular tension between the underlying scientific consensus and what governments will allow to appear in the policymakers' summary. The 2021-2022 AR6 cycle had several documented cases of language being weakened during plenary review.

2. The CDM integrity story. I described the HFC-23 and nitrous oxide problems as the main integrity issues. The full story is more nuanced, the CDM had a robust verification system (the Designated Operational Entities), substantial methodology development, and many genuinely additional projects. The integrity problems were real and concentrated in specific project types, but the framing "CDM was broken" oversimplifies. The CDM was both genuinely useful for some applications and seriously flawed for others, and the policy community took a decade to develop the diagnostic tools to tell the difference.

3. The Copenhagen "failure" framing. I described Copenhagen as a near-total failure that nevertheless laid the groundwork for Paris. This is a defensible reading, but some negotiating-history scholars (Bodansky, Yamin, others) argue Copenhagen accomplished more than is often credited, the 2 degrees Celsius goal, the pledges architecture, the institutional creation of GCF. The "failure" framing partly reflects NGO expectations management, not just the substantive outcome.

4. The role of non-state actors. I focused on intergovernmental negotiation. The actual climate regime is much broader, cities (C40), states and provinces (Under2 Coalition), companies (We Mean Business, RE100, S.B.T.i.), investors (Climate Action 100+), faith communities, and civil society all play substantive roles. The international U.N.F.C.C.C. track is one of several, and not always the most consequential. I'll cover this more in Chapter 14 (Geopolitics).

5. The U.S. specifically. The U.S. has been a uniquely consequential actor in this history (largest historical emitter, largest economy, sometimes a leader and sometimes a blocker). I gave the basic story but couldn't cover every twist. The U.S. story alone deserves its own treatment, the Senate's structural role via the 2/3 ratification rule, the executive/legislative friction, the difference between treaty law and political commitments, the Clean Power Plan and its judicial fate. This is a separate analytical thread that runs through the period and will reappear in Chapter 14.
