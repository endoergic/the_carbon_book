# Carbon: A Personal Curriculum

**Student profile:** Professional in adjacent field (climate/energy/finance/policy)
**Scope:** Markets AND physical/industrial carbon, equally weighted
**Pedagogy:** Socratic, conversational, frequent comprehension checks
**Chapter length target:** ~40 pages (~12,000–15,000 words) of structured content per chapter, plus exercises and a "what you should now be able to do" checklist
**Order:** Designed to be modular. Each chapter has stated prerequisites; you can take them in any order as long as prerequisites are met (or we patch gaps inline).

---

## How the curriculum is structured

Three tracks, woven together:

- **Track A — Foundations & Physical Carbon** (the molecule, the material, the industrial flows)
- **Track B — Carbon as Climate Liability** (emissions, accounting, policy, markets)
- **Track C — The Forward View** (removals, CDR, net zero, frontier tech, geopolitics)

A chapter may sit primarily in one track but draw from the others. The syllabus is ordered by *logical dependency*, not by track.

## Chapter map

### Chapter 1 — Carbon the Element, Carbon the Problem *(Track A + B foundation)*
*Prerequisite for:* essentially everything else.
The chemistry and physics of carbon: allotropes, the carbon cycle (fast and slow), atmospheric CO₂ and the greenhouse effect, the distinction between *carbon* (element), *CO₂* (molecule), *CO₂e* (accounting unit), *GHG* (basket), and *carbon* (the colloquial shorthand for all of the above). Why this terminological sloppiness causes real confusion in policy and markets. Order of magnitude: how big is the global carbon stock, by reservoir? How big are the annual fluxes? Where do anthropogenic emissions sit relative to natural flux?

### Chapter 2 — Solid Carbon as Commodity *(Track A)*
*Prerequisite:* Ch. 1.
Carbon as a *material commodity* — separate from the climate framing entirely. Petroleum coke (fuel-grade and anode-grade), metallurgical coke, carbon black, activated carbon, graphite (natural flake, vein, amorphous, and synthetic), anode-grade graphite for batteries, carbon fiber, graphene, industrial diamond. Producers, buyers, prices, trade flows. The steel-coke-iron axis. The aluminum smelter anode question. The China dominance story (>65% of natural graphite, ~80% of anode-grade processing). Why this matters for the energy transition: the EV battery requires more carbon (as graphite) than lithium, by mass.

### Chapter 3 — The CO₂ Molecule Value Chain *(Track A, links forward to B + C)*
*Prerequisite:* Ch. 1.
What happens to CO₂ once you have it as a molecule. The full value chain:
- **Sources:** industrial point sources (cement, ammonia, ethanol, gas processing — ranked by purity and capture cost), DAC, biogenic.
- **Midstream:** CO₂ pipelines (Denbury/ExxonMobil's network, Summit Carbon Solutions, the cancelled Navigator project as instructive failure), trucking, marine shipping (Northern Lights model), liquefaction infrastructure.
- **Hubs:** Baytown / Houston Ship Channel CCS Alliance, US Gulf Coast generally, North Sea (Northern Lights, Porthos, Aramis, Acorn), Alberta (ACTL), Wabash, the Midwest ethanol corridor.
- **End uses, in honest order of current scale:**
  - **CO₂-EOR** — ~70 Mt/yr in the US, the historical anchor, contested climate value.
  - **Merchant CO₂** — ~230 Mt/yr globally for food/beverage carbonation, greenhouse enrichment, water treatment, welding shield gas, dry ice, supercritical CO₂ extraction. Most people in "climate" don't know this market exists.
  - **Geological storage** — saline aquifers and depleted reservoirs (deeper treatment in Ch. 10).
  - **Conversion to products** — e-fuels (methanol, jet, diesel via Fischer-Tropsch), polymers (Covestro, Econic), building materials (CarbonCure, Solidia, mineralization-as-product), synthetic graphite from CO₂ (frontier).
- The regulatory regime for CO₂ transport (PHMSA, FERC's role/non-role) and the **Satartia, Mississippi pipeline rupture (2020)** as the safety-and-permitting case study that's reshaping the politics.

This chapter exists because "CO₂ pipeline-type projects like Baytown" sit in a different value chain than solid carbon materials, and both deserve their own treatment before we get to the emissions/policy framing.

### Chapter 4 — Fossil Fuels and the Carbon Stock We're Burning *(Track A → B bridge)*
*Prerequisite:* Ch. 1.
Coal, oil, gas: where they came from geologically, how much carbon they contain, how the global energy system mobilizes that carbon into the atmosphere. The concept of the "carbon budget" — remaining emissions consistent with 1.5°C / 2°C. Stranded assets, unburnable carbon, the IEA Net Zero scenario. This is the chapter that explains *why there is a carbon problem to manage*.

### Chapter 5 — Emissions Accounting: Scopes, Boundaries, and the GHG Protocol *(Track B foundation)*
*Prerequisite:* Ch. 1.
Scope 1, 2, 3. Operational vs. equity vs. financial control. The GHG Protocol Corporate Standard. ISO 14064. Inventory boundaries and double-counting risks. Why Scope 3 is both the most important and the most contested. Financed emissions (PCAF). Avoided emissions vs. reduced emissions vs. removed emissions — terminology that matters and is constantly misused. This chapter is the rosetta stone for every subsequent chapter on policy and markets.

### Chapter 6 — The History of Carbon Policy, 1988–2015 *(Track B)*
*Prerequisite:* Ch. 1, helpful: Ch. 5.
IPCC formation. UNFCCC. Kyoto Protocol — Annex I, CDM, JI, the architecture and its failure modes. The Bush rejection. The 2009 Copenhagen collapse. The road to Paris. Why Paris is structurally different (NDCs, bottom-up, ratchet). What "common but differentiated responsibilities" actually means and why it still shapes negotiations. The shift from cap-and-trade-as-default to a more pluralistic policy landscape.

### Chapter 7 — Compliance Carbon Markets *(Track B)*
*Prerequisite:* Ch. 5, Ch. 6.
The EU ETS in detail — phases, MSR, free allocation, CBAM linkage. California cap-and-trade and RGGI. China's national ETS (power sector). UK ETS post-Brexit. New Zealand. South Korea. Mechanics: caps, allowances (EUA, CCA, RGA), auctions, banking/borrowing, price floors and ceilings, leakage, free allocation, benchmarking. Empirical performance: have they reduced emissions, and how do we know? Waxman-Markey's failure and what it taught the US.

### Chapter 8 — Voluntary Carbon Markets: Offsets and Credits *(Track B + C)*
*Prerequisite:* Ch. 5. Helpful: Ch. 7.
The structure of the VCM: project developer → standard/registry → verifier → credit → buyer. Verra (VCS), Gold Standard, ACR, CAR, Puro.earth, Isometric. Project types: REDD+, ARR, IFM, cookstoves, renewable energy (and why they fell out of favor), methane destruction, DAC, biochar, enhanced rock weathering, mineralization. Additionality, permanence, leakage, baselines, MRV. The 2023 reckoning — *Guardian/SourceMaterial* investigation, the Verra crisis, ICVCM and VCMI as response. Where the VCM is going.

### Chapter 9 — Carbon Pricing in Theory and Practice *(Track B)*
*Prerequisite:* Ch. 7.
The economics: Pigouvian taxes vs. cap-and-trade, price vs. quantity instruments under uncertainty (Weitzman 1974, still the canonical reference). The social cost of carbon — EPA's number, the Biden interagency working group, RFF's update, what discount rate assumptions do to it. Carbon taxes in practice: British Columbia, Sweden, Switzerland. Border adjustments — CBAM in detail, the WTO question, US proposals (Foreign Pollution Fee, Clean Competition Act). Why economists love carbon pricing and why it's been politically anemic in much of the world.

### Chapter 10 — Carbon Capture, Utilization, and Storage (CCUS) *(Track A + C)*
*Prerequisite:* Ch. 1, Ch. 3, Ch. 4.
Point-source capture: post-combustion (amines), pre-combustion, oxyfuel. The CO₂-EOR origins and why that legacy is contested (links back to Ch. 3). Storage geology — saline aquifers, depleted reservoirs, basalt mineralization. Class VI wells in the US, the regulatory regime. The 45Q tax credit and how the IRA transformed CCUS economics. Hubs treated as projects (the value chain itself is in Ch. 3): Gulf Coast, North Sea (Northern Lights, Acorn), Alberta. Honest assessment: what's actually been built, what's failed (Petra Nova, Gorgon underperformance), what's plausible by 2035.

### Chapter 11 — Carbon Dioxide Removal (CDR) *(Track C)*
*Prerequisite:* Ch. 1, Ch. 5, Ch. 8. Helpful: Ch. 10.
The taxonomy: nature-based (afforestation, soil carbon, blue carbon), engineered (DAC, BECCS), hybrid (biochar, enhanced rock weathering, ocean alkalinity enhancement, mCDR more broadly). Permanence tiers. The Frontier model (offtake agreements as market-making). Stripe Climate, Microsoft's procurement, the emerging buyer's market. Cost curves — what's $100/ton, what's $1,000/ton, what would it take to get DAC to $100. The "residual emissions" framing and how much CDR the IPCC scenarios actually require. Where the science is genuinely uncertain (oceans, soils).

### Chapter 12 — Hard-to-Abate Sectors *(Track A + B)*
*Prerequisite:* Ch. 4, Ch. 5. Helpful: Ch. 10.
Steel (DRI-EAF, hydrogen, molten oxide electrolysis, Stegra/H2 Green Steel). Cement (clinker substitution, calcined clay, CCS, Sublime Systems' electrochemical approach). Chemicals and plastics (the carbon-as-feedstock question — links to Ch. 3 on CO₂-to-products). Aviation (SAF pathways, book-and-claim). Shipping (ammonia, methanol, the IMO's 2023 strategy). Long-haul trucking. Each sub-sector: current emissions, technology options, cost gap, policy levers, who's actually doing it.

### Chapter 13 — Corporate Net Zero and the Disclosure Regime *(Track B + C)*
*Prerequisite:* Ch. 5, Ch. 8.
SBTi — what it requires, what it doesn't, the offset wars of 2024. ISSB / IFRS S2. EU CSRD and ESRS E1. SEC climate disclosure rule (the saga). California SB 253 and SB 261. TCFD's absorption into ISSB. The transition plan concept (TPT, GFANZ). What "net zero" actually means when a company says it, and the divergence between SBTi-aligned, Paris-aligned, and marketing-aligned claims. Greenwashing law: EU Green Claims Directive, FTC Green Guides revision.

### Chapter 14 — Carbon Geopolitics and Trade *(Track B + C)*
*Prerequisite:* Ch. 7, Ch. 9.
CBAM and the response from China, India, Turkey, US. The "carbon club" idea (Nordhaus). G7 climate club. WTO and Article XX. Critical minerals overlap (graphite, lithium — links to Ch. 2) and the IRA/EU CRMA response. China's dominance of the physical carbon value chain (graphite, anodes, solar polysilicon) and what it means for the energy transition. The petrostate transition question — Saudi/UAE/Norway strategies. Climate finance: $100B pledge, loss and damage, the COP28 outcome.

### Chapter 15 — The Future: 2030, 2050, and the Speculative Frontier *(Track C)*
*Prerequisite:* most of the above.
What the next decade plausibly looks like under current policy. Where we're behind. The interaction of AI compute demand with the transition. Carbon-to-products at scale (LanzaTech, Twelve, Solidia). Mineralization (Heirloom, 44.01). Ocean CDR — the genuine scientific debate. Solar geoengineering as the elephant. The geopolitical scenarios. The honest version of "what works, what doesn't, what we don't know."

## Working assumptions (flag to change)

- Geographic emphasis: global, with depth on US/EU/China; secondary on UK, California, Canada, Australia, Japan, Korea, Brazil, India, Gulf states.
- Time horizon: pre-1990 origins → 2050 pathways.
- Numerical fluency: I'll assume you're comfortable with units (MtCO₂, GtCO₂, $/tCO₂, ppm), basic finance concepts (NPV, LCOE), and basic policy concepts (regulation vs. market instrument). I'll define everything that's jargon, but not those.
- I will **not** assume current-events knowledge past ~2024 without confirming. For anything post-2024 (e.g., a specific COP outcome, a current EUA price, a regulation's most recent revision), I'll search before answering.
