## Why this chapter exists

When people in climate policy say "carbon," they almost always mean the emissions kind — CO₂ in the atmosphere, the credits and allowances and budgets we built around it. But the world has a parallel carbon economy that's been around for far longer than carbon markets, sells carbon by the ton in physical form, and has its own producers, buyers, traders, prices, and geopolitical fights. It's also the *biggest carbon market in the world*, by mass — by a wide margin.

A first calibration: in 2024, the world made approximately:

- **~700–800 Mt of metallurgical coke**, almost all of it consumed in steelmaking.
- **~14–15 Mt of carbon black**, almost all of it consumed in tire and rubber manufacturing.
- **~2–3 Mt of activated carbon**, consumed in water and air treatment, food processing, gold recovery.
- **~1.6 Mt of natural graphite**, increasingly consumed in lithium-ion battery anodes (and also refractories, lubricants, foundry).
- **~1.5–2 Mt of synthetic graphite**, also increasingly going to batteries (and electrodes for steel, aluminum smelter anodes).
- **~150 kt of carbon fiber**, going to aerospace, wind blades, automotive lightweighting, sporting goods.

For perspective: the voluntary carbon credit market trades on the order of **~200–250 Mt CO₂e per year**, a market worth roughly $1–2 billion at current prices. Metallurgical coke alone is **3 to 4 times larger by mass** and, valued as coke, worth on the order of **$200 billion or more** — and far more under broader definitions (we unpack the factor-of-ten methodology range in Part 1). Carbon black is a $15–21 billion market. The four major industrial gas companies — Linde, Air Liquide, Air Products, Messer — collectively sell more *merchant CO₂* than the entire voluntary carbon market traffics in credits.

The physical carbon economy is not a sidebar to the climate carbon economy. It is in many ways the dog; climate is the tail. Decarbonizing steel — the largest single source of industrial CO₂ emissions globally — *is* a project in restructuring the metallurgical coke value chain. The Chinese export restrictions on graphite — a critical material for lithium-ion batteries — *is* the front line of the energy transition's supply-chain politics. The two carbon economies are colliding.

This chapter walks through the major commodity carbon materials, who makes them, who buys them, where the supply chains are concentrated, and where they intersect with the emissions and energy-transition stories that dominate the rest of the curriculum.

A note on data quality before we start. The market-size figures I'll cite are aggregations from a mix of USGS (good for production tonnage), industry-association data (good but often not directly verifiable), private market research firms (Mordor, Persistence, Grand View, Future Market Insights, etc. — *substantially* divergent), and company filings (good but only covering the listed majors). You'll see citations of "$37 billion" and "$358 billion" for the same metallurgical coke market in the same year from different reports. This is not because anyone is lying — the methodologies differ on what they include (just coke? coke plus the coking coal feedstock? whole BF-BOF cost stack?). When I cite a number, I'll try to indicate which methodology I'm relying on. When the divergence is wide, I'll flag it. Treat market-research firm numbers as directional, not precise.

# Part 1: Coke — the largest carbon commodity in the world

## Metallurgical coke and the blast furnace

The world's largest single use of carbon as a material is metallurgical coke ("met coke") in primary steelmaking. To understand why, you need to understand the chemistry of the blast furnace.

Iron ore is iron oxide (mostly Fe₂O₃ — hematite, and Fe₃O₄ — magnetite). To get usable iron, you have to strip the oxygen off. The classical method, perfected over centuries, is to react the iron oxide with carbon at high temperatures. The simplified overall reaction is:

> 2 Fe₂O₃ + 3 C → 4 Fe + 3 CO₂

In a real blast furnace, the reaction proceeds through carbon monoxide as an intermediate, and the actual chemistry is more complex, but the essential point is that carbon — in the specific form of coke — is doing two jobs simultaneously:

1. **Chemical role: reducing agent.** Carbon (or CO derived from it) strips oxygen off the iron oxide, producing CO₂ as the inevitable waste.
2. **Physical role: structural support.** A blast furnace is a 30+ meter tall column packed with ore, flux (limestone), and coke. The mixture must remain permeable to the hot gas blast rising from below. Coke is uniquely suited to this — it's mechanically strong enough not to crush under the column weight, porous enough to allow gas flow, and resistant to the alkali attack that would degrade other materials.

This double role is why coke is essentially irreplaceable in the conventional blast furnace. You can swap *some* coke for pulverized coal injection (PCI) at the bottom — that's a way to use cheaper coal as fuel and save on coke costs — but you cannot eliminate coke from a blast furnace and have it still function as a blast furnace. The structural role binds you to a coal-derived carbon source.

This is the fundamental reason steel decarbonization is so hard, and why it dominates Chapter 12 (Hard-to-Abate Sectors). The alternative pathways — direct reduced iron (DRI) with hydrogen, molten oxide electrolysis, biochar-substituted blast furnaces — all aim to eliminate or replace the coke entirely, which means reinventing the metallurgy, not just retrofitting the equipment.

## The numbers

In 2022, global steelmaking consumed approximately **755 million tons of coking coal** (Global Efficiency Intelligence), which converts at roughly 1.4 tons of coal per ton of coke into about **540–550 Mt of metallurgical coke**. Different sources give somewhat different production figures — call it **700–800 Mt/yr** with reasonable error bars, the variance largely depending on whether the figure includes captive coke produced by integrated steel mills.

Geographic distribution (approximate, 2024):
- **China: ~470 Mt**, about 60–65% of global production. China both produces and consumes the most.
- **India: ~50 Mt**, the fastest-growing major producer.
- **Japan: ~30 Mt**, declining slowly.
- **Russia, EU, Korea, US, Brazil:** mid-single-digit-to-twenties Mt each.

China is also the major net exporter of met coke (~5 Mt of exports in 2023 per market research sources, though export volumes are volatile because of domestic policy interventions). India is a net importer to feed its growing steel industry.

Pricing is notoriously volatile. Metallurgical coke is closely tied to the coking-coal market (the upstream feedstock) and the steel market (the downstream demand). In 2021–2022, coking coal prices spiked to over **$600/ton** on supply disruptions; by 2024 they had settled to roughly **$200–250/ton** for premium hard coking coal. Met coke itself prices at roughly 1.3–1.5× the underlying coking coal cost — so call it **$300–400/ton** for blast furnace coke in normal market conditions, with wide swings.

The market-research firms cite vastly different total market values:
- One source: $37 billion in 2024 (Market Research Future).
- Another: $99.7 billion in 2024 (Intel Market Research).
- A third: $358 billion in 2024 (Research and Markets, Grand View).
- A fourth: $218 billion in 2024 (FactMR).

The factor-of-ten range is *not* a measurement error. It's a definitional disagreement: some are pricing the coke alone, some are including the coking coal feedstock, some are including the full coke-and-blast-furnace cost stack. Use these numbers directionally. A defensible "narrow" market value (coke only) is on the order of **$200–250 billion** at 700 Mt × $300–350/ton. A "broad" market value including upstream and integrated steel impacts is whatever you want to argue.

## The producers

The structure of met coke production is unusual: most of it is produced *captively* by integrated steel mills, not sold on the merchant market. ArcelorMittal, Nippon Steel, POSCO, JSW, Tata Steel, Baowu (the world's largest steel company by output), HBIS, Ansteel — these companies all run their own coke ovens adjacent to their blast furnaces. The merchant market — coke sold to third parties — is a much smaller slice, dominated by:

- **SunCoke Energy** (US) — the largest independent merchant coke producer in North America.
- **Various Chinese producers** — Shanxi Coking, Mei Jin Energy, etc., with consolidation accelerating under environmental regulation.
- **JFE Steel, Nippon Steel** sell some merchant coke.

Top-five global concentration is only about 15% of the market because so much is captive. This contrasts sharply with carbon black, where the top five command >50%.

The merchant coke market is therefore mostly a marginal-pricing mechanism between captive producers and a few buyers without their own coke ovens — Electric Arc Furnace mills using DRI feed, foundries, ferroalloy producers, calcium carbide producers. For the average policy-curious reader, the merchant market is less interesting than the captive ovens inside the integrated steel mills, which are where most of the carbon and most of the CO₂ actually flow.

## Where the emissions come in

Coke production itself emits CO₂ — coke ovens drive off the volatile fraction of coking coal as coke oven gas (which is itself often combusted for energy), and a fraction of the carbon is lost in the process. But the dominant emissions come later: when the coke meets the iron ore in the blast furnace and the carbon is converted to CO₂. Primary steelmaking via the integrated blast furnace + basic oxygen furnace (BF-BOF) route emits roughly **1.8–2.3 tCO₂ per ton of crude steel**, and global crude steel production is about 1.9 billion tons per year. That's about 3.5 GtCO₂/yr — close to **10% of global fossil CO₂ emissions** — and the coke is the biggest single source within the steel-making process.

This is the thread that connects Chapter 2 to nearly every later chapter. Steel decarbonization (Ch. 12) means restructuring this carbon flow. CCS on blast furnaces (Ch. 10) means capturing the CO₂ rather than abating the carbon. Climate policy on imports (CBAM, Ch. 14) is fundamentally about pricing the carbon embedded in steel exports.

## Petroleum coke ("petcoke")

Petroleum coke is a related but distinct product — the heavy carbon residue left behind when crude oil is refined in a delayed coker. It comes in two main grades:

- **Anode-grade (calcined) petcoke** — low sulfur, low metals, used as a precursor for carbon anodes in aluminum smelting (the Hall-Héroult process consumes about 0.4 tons of carbon anode per ton of aluminum). Also used as a precursor for some synthetic graphite. Premium product; tight supply.
- **Fuel-grade petcoke** — higher sulfur and metals content. Used as a cheap fuel substitute for coal in cement kilns, power plants, and as a blast-furnace injectant. The largest single fuel-grade petcoke importers are India (cement industry) and Turkey.

Total global petcoke production is roughly **150 Mt/yr**, with the US (refineries on the Gulf Coast running heavy crudes) being the largest producer and exporter. The petcoke market is downstream of refinery configurations — heavier crudes produce more coke, lighter crudes produce less. The product is essentially a coproduct of gasoline and diesel, sold at a steep discount to coal in many markets because its high sulfur and metals content make it environmentally problematic.

Petcoke is a striking case of the embedded-carbon problem. It's effectively pure carbon (>85% C, often >90%) and burning it releases nearly all of that carbon as CO₂. India's cement industry is one of the world's largest petcoke consumers, and the resulting emissions are not generally accounted for as "petroleum emissions" — they're cement emissions or industrial emissions, downstream of the original refining decision. The carbon attribution is muddy.

## **Stop-and-check 2.A**

1. Why is coke uniquely well-suited to the blast furnace, and why doesn't the same thing work with raw coal or charcoal?
2. The merchant coke market is small relative to captive coke production. What does this imply about price discovery and transparency in the global coke market?
3. India is the fastest-growing met coke producer. Why is that strategically significant in the next decade, given what you know about steel demand and decarbonization?
4. **Socratic prompt:** If you were running a major integrated steel mill and you saw CBAM coming (Chapter 14), would you defend your coke-fueled blast furnaces or start building DRI-EAF capacity? What does the answer depend on?

**Answers**

1. Raw coal lacks coking coal's structural properties — it doesn't form a stable, porous mass that allows hot air to flow through the blast furnace charge. Coke's mechanical strength and porosity are essential: it must support the weight of iron ore above it, allow reducing gases to percolate upward, and simultaneously combust to produce the CO and heat needed for the reaction. Charcoal is structurally weaker and energy-poor by comparison. Only metallurgical-grade coking coal, after coking, produces a coke strong enough to bear blast furnace conditions.

2. Because most coke is produced and consumed captively — the steelmaker owns or contracts the coking coal directly from mines and does not sell coke on the open market. Merchant market prices are set by a thin, residual float. This makes price discovery harder and more volatile: a few large transactions can swing benchmark prices. It also means pricing relationships (spot vs. contract, quarterly vs. annual) are opaque relative to commodities like oil or LME metals.

3. India's fast-growing met coke capacity combined with rapid steel demand growth means India becomes both a major buyer and increasingly a major seller of coke globally in the next decade. As Indian steel mills scale, they need coke; as Indian coke capacity grows, India competes with China for export markets. At the same time, India's steelmakers are facing European CBAM pressure, creating an economic incentive to adopt DRI-EAF earlier. The tension between India's coal-heavy industrial base and CBAM-driven trade incentives makes India a pivotal player in coke market trajectory.

4. The answer depends on the asset's remaining useful life, the cost of green hydrogen in your region, and your CBAM exposure. If your blast furnaces have 20+ years left and you're selling primarily to non-EU markets, defending them and adding CCS may be NPV-positive. If you're heavily EU-exposed, CBAM costs (~€100–150/ton of steel by 2026+) start competing with the green premium for DRI-EAF. The decisive variable is the cost of green hydrogen: if you can access it at under ~$2–3/kg, H₂-DRI pencils against CBAM costs; above $5/kg, BF-BOF+CCS may be cheaper even at current EU ETS prices.

# Part 2: Carbon black — the carbon you ride on

## What it is, where it comes from

Carbon black is a fine particulate amorphous carbon, produced by the partial combustion of heavy petroleum oils (the dominant route — "furnace black") or natural gas ("thermal black," "acetylene black"). It's not the same as soot, although chemically related — carbon black is engineered to controlled particle size, structure, and surface chemistry, while soot is whatever combustion happens to produce.

Two-thirds of global carbon black goes to tire manufacturing. The remainder is distributed across rubber goods (hoses, conveyor belts, seals), plastics and polymers (UV stabilization, conductivity), pigments and inks, paints and coatings, and a small but rapidly growing slice in battery applications (conductive additives in lithium-ion electrodes).

The reason tires are 66% of the market is mechanical: carbon black reinforces the rubber matrix, dramatically improving wear resistance, tear strength, and tensile properties. A modern passenger tire is roughly 25–30% carbon black by weight; a heavy-truck tire is even higher. Without carbon black, tires would wear out in a few thousand miles and lose grip rapidly. The next-most-mature reinforcing alternative — precipitated silica with a silane coupling agent — is used in "green" low-rolling-resistance tires but doesn't fully replace carbon black; it's typically blended.

## The numbers

Global carbon black production in 2024 was approximately **14 million tons**, with a market value of roughly **$15–21 billion** depending on which market research firm you trust (smaller divergence than for met coke because more of carbon black is sold on the merchant market, with discoverable pricing).

Geographic concentration:
- **China: ~45%** of global output (roughly 6.3 Mt/yr).
- **India: ~12%**, fast-growing.
- **Europe + Russia/CIS: ~15%**.
- **North America: ~10%**.
- **Rest of Asia (Japan, Korea, Thailand): ~12%**.

Top five producers account for over half the market — a far more consolidated structure than coke:
- **Birla Carbon** (Aditya Birla Group, India-headquartered, global) — the largest by capacity.
- **Cabot Corporation** (US, NYSE: CBT).
- **Orion Engineered Carbons** (Germany/Luxembourg, NYSE: OEC).
- **Tokai Carbon** (Japan).
- **Jiangxi Black Cat Carbon Black** (China).

Plus regional players: PCBL (India), Omsk Carbon Group (Russia), Continental Carbon, Mitsubishi Chemical. Specialty grades — used in conductive plastics, EV battery applications, high-end coatings — command premium prices and are concentrated in Cabot, Orion, and a handful of others.

Pricing depends heavily on the feedstock (carbon black oil, CBO — itself a refinery byproduct) and on grade. Standard tire-grade prices typically run **$1,000–1,500/ton**. Specialty grades — high-purity, narrow particle size distribution, surface-modified — can run **$3,000–10,000/ton**, with the conductive grades for lithium-ion batteries at the high end. EV battery applications are a structural tailwind for the specialty segment.

## The decarbonization angle

Carbon black manufacturing emits substantial CO₂ — roughly **2–3 tCO₂ per ton of carbon black** produced, both from the petroleum feedstock and from process fuels. Global carbon black emissions are therefore on the order of 30–40 MtCO₂/yr — not a huge number on the global scale, but material for the producing companies.

The emerging story is **recovered carbon black (rCB)** — pyrolyzing end-of-life tires to recover the carbon black that was originally compounded into them. rCB has been a niche for years but is scaling up with EU and US regulatory pressure on tire-derived waste. Capacity is projected to exceed 1 Mt/yr by 2030, growing roughly 7% CAGR vs. furnace black's 4%. rCB doesn't fully match virgin carbon black on performance for the most demanding applications (high-end passenger tires) but is good enough for many industrial and lower-end uses. Black Bear Carbon (Netherlands), Bolder Industries (US), Pyrum Innovations (Germany), and others are competing in this space.

A second emerging vector: **carbon black from methane pyrolysis** — splitting natural gas into hydrogen and solid carbon at high temperatures, producing carbon black as a coproduct of hydrogen. Companies like Monolith (Nebraska, NEXT supported) and Hazer Group (Australia) are building plants. The economics depend on getting paid for both products — hydrogen for fuel/feedstock value and carbon black for industrial value — and the carbon black quality has to meet tire-industry specifications, which is non-trivial. Watch this space; it has the potential to materially shift hydrogen production economics, but commercial scale-up has been slower than the press releases suggested.

## **Stop-and-check 2.B**

1. Why is tire performance so dependent on carbon black, when so much of the rest of the tire is the elastomer? What does carbon black actually *do* mechanically?
2. The carbon black market is far more consolidated (top 5 = >50%) than the coke market (top 5 = ~15%). Why?
3. If recovered carbon black scales up faster than expected, what does that do to virgin carbon black margins? What does it do to virgin carbon black feedstock demand (and therefore refinery economics)?
4. **Socratic prompt:** Methane pyrolysis produces hydrogen and solid carbon black. If you're decarbonizing the gas industry, is this a desirable pathway? What does the answer depend on, including what happens to the carbon black?

**Answers**

1. Carbon black mechanically reinforces the rubber matrix by forming strong polymer-filler bonds that constrain chain slippage under stress. The effect is dramatic: tensile strength, tear resistance, and abrasion resistance all increase roughly 2–10× compared to unfilled rubber. Without this reinforcement, tire tread would wear away in thousands rather than tens of thousands of miles. The mechanism is surface-chemistry-mediated adhesion between the carbon black particle surface and the elastomer chains — not just physical mixing.

2. Carbon black is largely a merchant commodity sold on visible spot markets; tires are manufactured globally and carbon black producers serve many buyers with relatively standardized grades. This creates enough buyer and seller competition for price discovery. Coke is mostly captive — steelmakers and cokemakers are often vertically integrated — and when trades occur, they're bilateral between large counterparties who negotiate confidentially. The thinner merchant float produces opaque pricing.

3. If rCB scales rapidly, virgin carbon black producers face margin compression on standard tire grades (the largest volume segment). They would likely retreat toward specialty grades (conductive, battery-application, narrow-particle-size) where rCB doesn't yet match virgin quality. On the feedstock side, reduced virgin CB demand means lower demand for carbon black oil (CBO) — a refinery byproduct — which is actually a problem for refiners who already struggle to find markets for heavy residuals.

4. Desirable depends critically on what happens to the carbon black (solid carbon byproduct). If it's used in long-lived industrial applications — tire reinforcement, battery electrodes, industrial composites — the carbon stays out of the atmosphere and the pathway is net-negative relative to conventional H₂ production (which produces CO₂). If the carbon black is burned for energy, the sequestration is temporary. The other variable is energy: methane pyrolysis is endothermic; if you run it on renewable electricity, it's green; if you run it on natural gas heat, you've partly undone the climate benefit.

# Part 3: Activated carbon — the surface area trade

## What it is

Activated carbon is amorphous carbon that has been processed to maximize its internal surface area — typically **500–1,500 m²/gram** — by creating a network of pores at multiple length scales. Think of it as carbon with a controlled honeycomb of holes at the molecular level. The pores adsorb molecules from the surrounding fluid (gas or liquid), making activated carbon a workhorse of separation and purification across the chemical industry.

It's produced by two main routes:

- **Steam activation** — Carbonize a feedstock (coconut shell, wood, peat, lignite, coal), then partially oxidize the resulting char with steam at ~800–900°C. Burns off some of the carbon, creating porosity.
- **Chemical activation** — Impregnate the feedstock with a chemical activating agent (phosphoric acid, zinc chloride, potassium hydroxide), then carbonize. Different pore structure than steam activation.

The feedstock matters enormously. Coconut shell activated carbon has small, uniform micropores and is preferred for gold recovery, gas-phase applications, and high-end water filtration. Coal-based activated carbon has a broader pore distribution and is cheaper, used in bulk water treatment. Wood-based products are mostly chemically activated and used for liquid-phase decolorization (sugar refining, edible oils, pharma).

## The numbers

Global activated carbon production is roughly **2.5–3 Mt/yr** with a market value around **$5–7 billion** (more consistent across sources than for coke). The largest end-use segments:

- **Water treatment** (drinking water, wastewater, industrial process water) — the largest application, growing steadily.
- **Air and gas treatment** (mercury control in power plant flue gas, VOC capture, industrial off-gas).
- **Food and beverage** (sugar decolorization, edible oil purification, decaffeinated coffee).
- **Pharmaceutical and chemical** (purification, catalysis).
- **Gold recovery** ("carbon-in-pulp" gold mining).
- **Automotive evaporative emissions** (the canister that captures gasoline vapors from your fuel tank — a hidden but mass-market application).

Major producers: **Cabot** (acquired Norit, the historic global leader; now under Cabot's specialty chemicals business after a 2018 spin), **Kuraray** (Japan, includes Calgon Carbon, a major US brand), **Donau Carbon** (Germany), **Haycarb** (Sri Lanka, coconut-shell specialist), **Jacobi Carbons** (Sweden/Sri Lanka).

China is a substantial producer but less internationally dominant in this commodity than in graphite or carbon black — partly because feedstock geography matters (coconut shell, certain coals) and partly because activated carbon quality requirements are demanding enough that mature producers retain advantages.

## Where carbon meets climate, indirectly

Activated carbon shows up in the climate story in three places:

**1. Coal-plant mercury control.** US EPA Mercury and Air Toxics Standards (MATS) drove a major expansion of powdered activated carbon (PAC) demand for flue-gas injection at coal power plants in the 2010s. As coal plants retire, this demand declines, creating an interesting wrinkle: activated carbon producers built capacity for a market that is now contracting.

**2. Direct air capture.** Some DAC technologies (Heirloom's mineralization-based approach being a different mechanism, but Climeworks' early sorbents and various others) use modified activated carbon or related porous carbon materials. The volumes are tiny relative to other DAC sorbent classes (amine-functionalized polymers, metal-organic frameworks, alkaline solutions), but it's a connection point.

**3. Battery and supercapacitor markets.** Specialized activated carbon, particularly with very high surface area (>2,000 m²/g) and controlled porosity, is the dominant electrode material in supercapacitors. Sodium-ion batteries — an emerging alternative to lithium-ion, particularly for stationary storage — also use hard carbon (a related material) on the anode side. These are small markets today but potentially growing.

The activated carbon industry is less geopolitically charged than graphite, but it sits at the boundary between commodity carbon and emerging energy-transition demand in interesting ways.

# Part 4: Graphite — the battery anode, the geopolitical front

This is where Chapter 2 most directly intersects with the energy transition. Graphite is *the* carbon material whose supply chain has become a national security issue in the past three years. We're going to spend serious time here.

## Why graphite is the battery anode material

Lithium-ion batteries have two electrodes: a cathode (usually a lithium-containing metal oxide — NMC, NCA, LFP) and an anode (usually graphite). During charging, lithium ions move from cathode to anode and intercalate — slide between the layers of graphite. During discharge, they move back.

Graphite works as a battery anode because of the layered structure I described in Chapter 1: each layer is a sheet of hexagonal carbon (graphene), held to adjacent layers by weak van der Waals forces. The gap between sheets is just wide enough for lithium ions to slip in and out. The structure also conducts electrons well along the planes, so charge can flow.

Approximately **95% of commercial lithium-ion battery anodes are graphite**, either natural, synthetic, or (increasingly) a blend. Silicon anodes — which can store much more lithium per gram than graphite — are an active R&D and early-commercial area (Sila Nanotechnologies, Group14, Enovix), but silicon anodes typically use silicon as a *coating* or *blend* on a graphite substrate rather than replacing it entirely, because pure silicon swells too much during charging. So even silicon-anode batteries are largely graphite anodes with silicon enhancements. Lithium-metal anodes (pure lithium foil) are the long-promised replacement for graphite that hasn't materialized at commercial scale.

For practical purposes, for the next 10+ years, every commercial lithium-ion cell will have a graphite anode. The question is what kind of graphite, made where, by whom.

## Natural vs. synthetic graphite

This is the fork in the road that you need to understand to follow the rest of this section.

**Natural graphite** is mined. It comes in three main forms:
- **Flake graphite** — large crystals, the form preferred for batteries after processing.
- **Vein graphite** — high-purity, found in Sri Lanka almost exclusively. Niche.
- **Amorphous graphite** — Lower purity, used in lubricants, foundries, refractories. Lower value.

Natural flake graphite to be used in batteries must be processed into **spherical graphite (also called CSPG — coated spherical purified graphite)**. This involves:
1. **Micronization** — Grinding the flakes into smaller particles.
2. **Spheronization** — Mechanical rounding to produce 10–25 micron spherical particles (poor man's image: tumbling rocks in a stream until they're round).
3. **Purification** — Acid leaching (typically hydrofluoric acid) to remove mineral impurities and bring purity to >99.95% carbon.
4. **Coating** — A thin layer of pyrolytic carbon (or pitch-derived carbon) deposited on each spherical particle to control the surface chemistry that interfaces with the electrolyte.

The mining-to-flake step is relatively distributed globally; the spheronization, purification, and coating steps are heavily concentrated in China. This is the choke point.

**Synthetic graphite** is manufactured. It starts with carbon-rich feedstocks — typically needle coke (a premium petroleum coke or coal-tar pitch product) — and processes them through graphitization, which involves heating to **2,500–3,000°C** for extended periods to convert the disordered carbon into ordered graphite structure. This is extremely energy-intensive: synthetic graphite production consumes roughly **15,000–20,000 kWh per ton**. At a US industrial electricity price of $0.08/kWh, that's $1,200–1,600/ton just in electricity. In China, with industrial electricity around $0.05–0.07/kWh and lower labor and capital costs, synthetic graphite can be produced for $5,000–7,000/ton.

Synthetic graphite offers better performance on some metrics (faster charging, longer cycle life) but at higher cost and much higher embodied energy (and therefore CO₂ if the electricity is fossil-fired — which it largely is in China). Natural graphite is cheaper, lower-energy, but harder to qualify for high-end battery applications because of variability in raw material properties.

In practice, modern battery anodes increasingly use **blends** — typically 70–80% natural, 20–30% synthetic, optimized for the specific cell application. EVs requiring fast charging tend toward higher synthetic content; energy-storage cells for grid applications can tolerate higher natural content.

## The numbers

Global natural graphite production in 2024 (USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025):
- **Total: ~1.63 Mt**
- **China: 1.27 Mt (78%)**
- **Madagascar: 89 kt (5.5%)** — up 41% YoY, surpassed Mozambique
- **Mozambique: ~75 kt** (Balama mine, owned by Syrah Resources)
- **Brazil: ~60 kt**
- **South Korea, Russia, Tanzania, Norway, others:** smaller producers

China also controls roughly **90% of the world's graphite *refining* capacity** — the spheronization-purification-coating steps that are required to turn mined flake into battery-ready anode material. This is even more concentrated than the mining picture suggests. A non-Chinese flake graphite mine can be developed, but if its output has to be shipped to China for refining and then re-imported as finished anode material, the supply chain remains Chinese-controlled.

Synthetic graphite production is concentrated similarly: China produces an estimated **65–75% of global synthetic graphite**, with the rest split among Japan, the US, and Europe (Tokai Carbon, Showa Denko, GrafTech, SGL Carbon, Imerys Graphite & Carbon).

## December 2023: China's export licensing

On October 20, 2023, China's Ministry of Commerce announced that as of **December 1, 2023**, exports of high-purity natural graphite (>99.9% carbon) and certain synthetic graphite materials would require export licenses. This was framed as a national security measure but landed in the middle of the EV transition and was widely read as a response to US chip export controls.

The immediate effect was a sharp drop in Chinese exports while licenses were processed. USGS reports that China's spherical graphite exports in the first two months of 2024 were **65% below** the same period in 2023; the next six months saw a more modest 19% decline as the licensing system processed approvals. Prices for non-Chinese flake graphite outside the Chinese system spiked. Western anode manufacturers and battery cell makers scrambled.

The deeper implications:

**1. Demonstrated leverage.** China can effectively throttle Western battery production by adjusting graphite export approvals. It hasn't (so far) done so on a large scale, but the option is established.

**2. Acceleration of non-Chinese capacity.** US Treasury, the IRA's foreign entity of concern (FEOC) rules, and the EU Critical Raw Materials Act have explicitly identified graphite as a focus. In 2024:
   - Three US graphite-related projects were funded through Bipartisan Infrastructure Law and Defense Production Act Title III ($125M each for an Alabama spherical graphite facility and a Kentucky/Louisiana recycling facility; $8.3M for a Canadian flake graphite mine).
   - Westwater Resources, Novonix, and Anovion are building synthetic graphite capacity in the US.
   - Syrah Resources operates a spherical graphite facility in Vidalia, Louisiana — the only major non-Chinese operation at scale.
   - The Inflation Reduction Act's §30D EV tax credit rules require battery critical materials (including graphite) to be increasingly sourced from the US or free-trade-agreement partners (with a temporary exception for graphite that has been extended multiple times because compliance is essentially impossible at current production scales).

**3. Tariff escalation.** The US announced in May 2024 a tariff increase on Chinese natural graphite from 0% to 25% beginning in 2026.

**4. Industry response.** The major battery cell makers — CATL, BYD, LG Energy Solution, SK On, Panasonic, Samsung SDI — are diversifying. CATL has invested in Indonesian and Mexican facilities; Korean and Japanese manufacturers are building synthetic graphite capacity in North America to meet IRA-eligible content rules.

The graphite story is the canonical example of what happens when an industrial commodity that almost nobody outside the industry tracks suddenly becomes critical to a major energy-transition technology. It's likely to repeat across other critical minerals (lithium hydroxide, cobalt refining, rare earth permanent magnets) and *will recur in Chapter 14*, where we'll treat the geopolitics in depth.

## Other graphite uses (briefly)

While batteries are the growth story, graphite has substantial legacy markets:

- **Refractories** (~30% of natural graphite demand historically): Heat-resistant linings for furnaces, especially in steelmaking. Magnesia-carbon and alumina-carbon refractories use flake graphite.
- **Foundry** (~10%): Mold coatings to prevent metal-mold reaction.
- **Lubricants** (~5%): Graphite's layered structure makes it a solid lubricant in high-temperature applications where oils would fail.
- **Pencils** (yes, still): A few percent. Wrongly assumed to be the main use of graphite by people who haven't thought about it.
- **Brake linings, gaskets, conductive coatings:** Smaller specialty uses.

Synthetic graphite has different distribution:
- **Electrodes for electric arc furnace (EAF) steelmaking** — the largest non-battery use. EAF growth (driven by steel decarbonization, increasing scrap recycling, US Section 232 tariffs, and CBAM-style pressure on BF-BOF steel) is a structural tailwind for synthetic graphite electrode demand. GrafTech, Showa Denko, SGL Carbon, Tokai Carbon dominate.
- **Aluminum smelter anodes**: Hall-Héroult cells consume about 0.4 t of carbon anode per ton of aluminum. Anode-grade petcoke is the precursor; the calcined and shaped anodes are themselves a manufactured product.

The intersection point: as the world increases EAF steelmaking (good for emissions) and battery production (good for the energy transition), demand for synthetic graphite rises along two channels simultaneously, while the energy intensity of synthetic graphite production is itself a decarbonization challenge.

## **Stop-and-check 2.C**

1. Why must natural graphite be spheroidized before use in a battery anode? What problem does the spherical shape solve?
2. China controls ~78% of mining and ~90% of refining. Which choke is more strategic, and why?
3. Synthetic graphite is more energy-intensive to produce than natural. But suppose you can run the synthetic-graphite plant on cheap renewable electricity. Does that change the long-run competitive landscape vs. natural graphite? What's the catch?
4. **Socratic prompt:** The IRA's FEOC rule says that after 2025, EVs with battery materials from "foreign entities of concern" (read: China) lose the $7,500 consumer credit. Graphite is in the rules, but the deadline has been extended because compliance is impossible. Should the deadline be enforced anyway, accepting the short-term price increase to force the non-Chinese supply chain? Make the case both ways.

**Answers**

1. Natural graphite flakes are irregular platelet shapes. When lithium ions intercalate and deintercalate during battery cycling, irregular shapes create mechanical stress concentrations that crack the graphite particle. Spheroidization (tumbling flakes until they're rounded) distributes stress more evenly and dramatically improves cycle life — the number of charge-discharge cycles before capacity degrades. Without it, batteries would fail in hundreds rather than thousands of cycles. The process also creates a more predictable packing density, improving volumetric energy density in the anode.

2. Mining is more strategic for direct supply of raw material; refining is more strategic for control of the value-added steps that determine performance. China dominates both, but the mining choke is easier to challenge — graphite deposits exist globally (Mozambique, Madagascar, Norway, Canada) and mine development is feasible within 5–10 years. The refining choke is harder: spheroidization, purification to 99.95%+, and coating require highly specialized equipment and process knowledge concentrated almost entirely in China. A non-Chinese miner shipping to a Chinese refiner solves nothing for supply-chain independence. The refining choke is more strategic.

3. Renewable electricity for synthetic graphite would significantly reduce the dominant operating cost (electricity is ~40–50% of production cost). It could make synthetic graphite competitive on cost with processed natural graphite at scale — synthetic has the advantage of more consistent quality and no geopolitical supply concentration. The catch: the Achilles heel of synthetic graphite is graphitization temperature (~2,500–3,000°C in Acheson furnaces), which is difficult to electrify with current technology. You need very-high-temperature industrial electric heating, which is available but expensive and not yet at full commercial scale for graphite volumes.

4. For enforcement: sustained ambiguity about compliance deadlines weakens the policy signal and delays non-Chinese supply-chain investment. Automakers and suppliers won't build the necessary infrastructure unless they believe the deadline is real. A deadline with repeated extensions is no deadline. Against enforcement: no non-Chinese anode graphite supply chain exists at scale in 2025; enforcing the rule would price American consumers out of EV credits, harm domestic EV adoption exactly when it's competing with Chinese EVs, and benefit no one. The pragmatic case is for phased enforcement with hard stop-dates — announce that the 2027 extension is truly final, and accompany it with supply-chain investment incentives that make compliance achievable by then.

# Part 5: Carbon fiber — the lightweight composite

## What it is

Carbon fiber is a manufactured material, not really an allotrope of carbon in the strict sense. Filaments are produced by heating an organic precursor — almost always **polyacrylonitrile (PAN)** for high-performance applications, or **pitch** for some industrial uses — in a controlled atmosphere. The thermal process drives off the non-carbon elements and leaves behind a filament that is roughly 90–95% carbon, with the carbon arranged in turbostratic graphitic structures aligned along the fiber axis. This alignment is what gives carbon fiber its remarkable mechanical properties — tensile strength up to 7 GPa, modulus over 900 GPa for the highest-end products, and densities around 1.8 g/cm³, vastly better strength-to-weight than steel or aluminum.

Carbon fiber is almost never used alone. It's woven or laid into fabrics or unidirectional tapes, then impregnated with a polymer resin (typically epoxy) and cured into a **carbon fiber reinforced polymer (CFRP)** composite. The composite is what gets used in aircraft, wind blades, hydrogen storage tanks, sporting goods, automotive parts, and increasingly in pressure vessels for industrial gas storage.

## The numbers

Global carbon fiber production capacity is roughly **200 kt/yr** as of 2024, with actual production around **150 kt/yr** (utilization runs in the 70–80% range). The market is more concentrated than most carbon commodities:

- **Toray** (Japan) — the global leader, roughly 30%+ market share.
- **Hexcel** (US) — major aerospace supplier.
- **Mitsubishi Chemical** (Japan).
- **Teijin** (Japan).
- **SGL Carbon** (Germany).
- **Hyosung Advanced Materials** (Korea).
- **Several Chinese producers** (Zhongfu Shenying, Jilin Carbon Valley, etc.) — rapidly growing capacity, mainly for wind, sporting goods, and increasingly industrial.

End-use distribution (approximate):
- **Aerospace** (~25%) — Highest-value, low-volume, qualifications-intensive. Modern wide-body aircraft (787, A350) are roughly half composite by weight.
- **Wind energy** (~25%) — Carbon fiber in the spar cap of large wind blades. Critical for blades longer than ~70 m, where glass fiber alone is too heavy and flexible.
- **Sporting goods** (~10%) — Bikes, golf clubs, tennis rackets, fishing rods. Mature mass-market.
- **Automotive** (~10%) — BMW i3/i8 led the high-end attempt; now mostly limited to high-performance and luxury vehicles. Cost has stayed too high for mainstream automotive.
- **Pressure vessels** (~10%) — Hydrogen storage tanks, compressed natural gas tanks. Growing rapidly with hydrogen mobility.
- **Construction, marine, industrial** — the rest.

Prices range from **$10–15/kg** for industrial/wind-grade to **$30–80/kg** for aerospace-grade to **>$100/kg** for specialty high-modulus and intermediate-modulus aerospace grades. The "cost reduction" story — getting carbon fiber to a price point that supports mass-market automotive use — has been "five years away" for about thirty years and shows no signs of arriving.

## Why this matters for energy transition

Carbon fiber is a critical enabler for two transition technologies:

**1. Wind energy.** Modern offshore wind turbines have blades 100+ meters long. Glass fiber alone cannot support these blade lengths because of weight and flex. Carbon fiber spar caps are essentially mandatory for the upper end of blade size. As wind blades get longer (cost per MWh declines roughly as the square of blade diameter), carbon fiber demand grows. Wind already consumes about 25% of global carbon fiber output and is the fastest-growing application.

**2. Hydrogen storage.** Storing hydrogen as a compressed gas (350 or 700 bar) requires tanks that can hold high pressure safely with minimum weight (especially for mobile applications). Carbon fiber composite tanks (Type IV, with a polymer liner and full CFRP overwrap) are the standard for vehicle hydrogen storage. As hydrogen mobility scales — fuel-cell trucks, buses, trains — carbon fiber demand scales with it.

There's an emissions paradox here. Carbon fiber production is energy-intensive — roughly 200–500 MJ/kg (compared to ~30 MJ/kg for steel and ~150 MJ/kg for aluminum). Embodied emissions for carbon fiber are roughly 20–30 kg CO₂/kg of fiber, depending on the precursor route and energy mix. This is high in absolute terms but is justified in lifecycle terms because the lightweighting enables huge operational emissions savings (aircraft fuel burn, wind turbine output, hydrogen vehicle range). The full LCA depends on application.

# Part 6: Other solid carbon commodities (briefly)

A handful of smaller markets that you'll see referenced and should know exist:

**Industrial diamond.** Synthetic diamond (HPHT and CVD processes) plus natural mined industrial-grade. Used in cutting, grinding, drilling tools. Roughly $15–20 billion market globally. About 95%+ of industrial diamond is synthetic; natural industrial diamond is now niche. China dominates production. The boundary between industrial and gem-quality CVD diamond is becoming a market disruption story for the De Beers/Alrosa natural diamond industry, but that's mostly outside our scope.

**Graphene.** Two decades after isolation, commercial applications are mostly limited to specialty additives (paints, coatings, composites, conductive inks). The "graphene revolution" did not arrive. Production volumes are in the thousands of tons per year globally — three orders of magnitude smaller than graphite. Watch for emerging applications in batteries (graphene as a conductive additive blended into anodes), thermal management, and electronics, but most of what gets called "graphene" in industry today is more accurately "graphene nanoplatelets" or "few-layer graphene," not single-layer graphene in the academic sense.

**Carbon nanotubes (CNTs).** Single-walled and multi-walled. Multi-walled CNTs have a real and growing market as conductive additives in lithium-ion batteries (replacing or supplementing carbon black on this dimension), reaching tens of thousands of tons per year globally. LG Chem, OCSiAl, Cabot, Arkema are players. Single-walled CNTs remain expensive and niche.

**Fullerenes (C₆₀ and related).** Specialty research and niche electronic applications. Tiny market.

**Carbon-derived activated catalysts and specialty carbons.** Used in fuel cells (Pt-on-carbon catalysts), supercapacitors (already mentioned), and various petrochemical processes. Catalyst-grade carbon is a high-purity, specialty product.

**Anode-grade petcoke and needle coke.** Pre-cursor materials for synthetic graphite and aluminum smelter anodes. Strategic upstream products that don't get much attention but are themselves global commodities with their own trade flows. China's tightening of needle coke supply has been an underappreciated factor in synthetic graphite pricing for the past decade.

# Part 7: Cross-cutting themes

## China dominance is structural, not accidental

Across most of the solid carbon commodity space — coke, carbon black, natural graphite, synthetic graphite, industrial diamond, CNTs — China is either the largest producer, the largest exporter, or both. This is not bad luck. It reflects a combination of:

1. **Resource endowment** — substantial natural graphite reserves, abundant coal for coke, large refining base for carbon black feedstock.
2. **Process scaling** — China industrialized these materials at scale during the 1990s–2010s while Western producers were either offshoring, environmentally constrained, or commodity-margin-squeezed.
3. **Industrial policy** — Sustained state support for downstream industries (steel, batteries, aluminum) that consumed these carbon commodities.
4. **Energy economics** — Cheap industrial electricity (much of it coal-fired) for energy-intensive processes (synthetic graphite, calcined petcoke, industrial diamond).
5. **Environmental cost externalization** — Historically lax enforcement on coke ovens, carbon black plants, and graphite processing, which involve substantial local air and water pollution. China has been tightening, but the legacy capacity advantage is still there.

The implication for the energy transition is uncomfortable: the materials needed to scale clean energy are concentrated in a country whose energy mix is itself heavily fossil-fueled, and whose environmental and labor standards have historically been weaker than the OECD's. Building non-Chinese supply chains is a years-long project that requires either accepting higher prices or substantially expanding global production capacity outside China — both options carry political costs.

We'll come back to this in Chapter 14.

## The embodied-carbon question

Several of the materials in this chapter are themselves carbon (literally) and embed substantial CO₂ in their manufacturing. To rank them very approximately (kg CO₂ per kg of product, full cradle-to-gate):

- **Metallurgical coke:** 0.4–0.8 (mostly process emissions and feedstock decomposition).
- **Carbon black:** 2–3.
- **Activated carbon:** 1–3, depending heavily on feedstock.
- **Natural graphite (mined to spherical):** 4–10, heavily dependent on the acid-purification step and electricity mix.
- **Synthetic graphite:** 15–25, dominated by the graphitization energy.
- **Carbon fiber:** 20–30, dominated by the carbonization energy.

For comparison: aluminum is roughly 12–17 kg CO₂/kg (primary, current mix), steel via BF-BOF is roughly 1.8–2.3, EAF steel is 0.4–0.7.

The synthetic graphite number stands out. As battery production scales, the embodied emissions of the graphite anode become a material part of the lifecycle emissions of an EV. This is one reason natural graphite, despite its supply-chain concentration in China, retains a structural cost and emissions advantage if the supply chain can be diversified.

## When solid carbon meets the emissions market

The materials in this chapter are not (yet) major participants in carbon markets — there are no large flows of carbon credits generated by switching from BF-BOF steel to EAF, or from synthetic to natural graphite. But the boundary is blurring:

- **Cement and steel CBAM.** EU CBAM (Ch. 14) now prices the embedded carbon in imported cement and steel — including, implicitly, the coke used to make the steel.
- **Battery passport regulation (EU).** From 2027, all batteries sold in the EU above 2 kWh capacity must carry a "battery passport" with cradle-to-gate carbon footprint disclosure. This will spotlight graphite emissions.
- **Tire and rubber product carbon footprint regulation.** EU CSRD (Ch. 13) will pull tire-manufacturer emissions disclosure including carbon black.
- **CO₂-to-products.** The frontier of Chapter 3 (CO₂ molecule value chain) includes routes to produce some of these materials from atmospheric or industrial CO₂ — synthetic graphite from captured CO₂ (early R&D), carbon black from methane pyrolysis with carbon byproduct (commercial scale-up underway), even diamond from CO₂ (academic only).

The two carbon economies — the physical materials one we've covered here, and the climate-emissions one we'll spend most of the curriculum on — are converging through regulation, through supply-chain accountability, and through new production routes. Understanding both is necessary to understand either.

2. Of the materials we've covered, which one's supply chain restructuring will be the most consequential for the energy transition in the next 5 years? Defend your answer.
3. The largest carbon commodity by mass (met coke) is also the one most threatened by the energy transition. The smallest mainstream one (graphite, ~1.6 Mt) is the one most likely to grow because of the energy transition. What does this tell you about the trajectory of "carbon as a material" over the next two decades?
4. **Socratic prompt:** Suppose you're advising a sovereign wealth fund on long-term industrial investments. Of the commodity carbon industries in this chapter, which would you long, which would you short, and which would you avoid? Be specific about your reasoning, including time horizons and embedded assumptions.

**Answers**

1. By tonnage: met coke (~700 Mt/yr) ≫ carbon black (~14 Mt/yr) > activated carbon (~2.5–3 Mt/yr) > graphite (~1.5–1.6 Mt/yr) > carbon fiber (~150 kt/yr). By revenue the rankings reshuffle, because price per ton varies by ~100× across these commodities. Carbon black is a ~$15–21B market on ~14 Mt/yr. So no — tonnage and revenue rankings are not the same; a high price per ton can lift a tiny-tonnage commodity like carbon fiber above much larger-volume ones.

2. Anode-grade graphite (natural and synthetic) for EV batteries. It's the commodity with the most structural growth driver (EV penetration), an emerging supply-chain reconfiguration underway (FEOC, IRA), and the highest complexity of quality requirements that create durable competitive moats for well-positioned producers and processors. The bet's key assumption: EV adoption continues at roughly current trajectory and lithium-ion chemistry (graphite anode) remains dominant through 2030. Silicon-dominant anodes or sodium-ion chemistries could undermine this.

3. Carbon as a material is shifting from an industrial byproduct of fossil combustion (met coke as coal derivative) to an engineered, precision material (battery anode graphite, advanced carbon fiber). Over two decades: coke demand peaks and declines as green steel displaces BF-BOF; carbon black faces growth in specialty segments as tire market evolves; graphite demand explodes as batteries scale. The commodity is transitioning from "what comes from burning coal" to "what enables the energy transition." The physical nature of the element is unchanged; the end markets are rotating dramatically.

4. Long: anode-grade graphite (natural spheroidization capacity outside China, and synthetic capacity on renewable electricity), specialty carbon black (battery-grade, high-performance industrial), carbon fiber (structural composite demand from wind, EVs, aerospace). Short: met coke producers with no DRI/EAF adjacent strategy, especially in OECD jurisdictions with carbon pricing — structurally stranded over a 10–20 year horizon. Avoid: commodity virgin carbon black for standard tire grades, as rCB and synthetic alternatives will compete on cost.

# Closing exercise

Three things to take away and act on:

**1. The mass-balance picture.** The world makes about a billion tons of solid carbon as commodity material every year (coke, carbon black, graphite, activated carbon, carbon fiber, plus all the smaller markets). Most of that carbon will eventually become CO₂ — coke is burned in blast furnaces, carbon black goes into tires that eventually wear away or get burned, even graphite anodes get recycled or landfilled with some carbon loss. Sit with that number against the ~38 GtCO₂/yr of fossil emissions we covered in Chapter 1. The commodity carbon throughput is roughly 1 Gt of carbon per year; that's ~3.7 GtCO₂ if it all oxidized eventually, or about 10% of global fossil emissions. Not the dominant flow — but not nothing.

**2. The geopolitical map.** China dominates the production of every major solid carbon commodity except aerospace-grade carbon fiber. Most of the energy transition's material requirements (graphite, synthetic graphite for EAF and batteries, carbon black for tires) are downstream of Chinese industrial capacity. The CBAM and IRA-FEOC responses are the West's structural response. The next decade will be a slow, expensive un-concentration of these supply chains — partial and contested.

**3. The connection points to come.** When we get to Chapter 10 (CCUS), one of the candidate uses for captured CO₂ is making synthetic graphite or carbon black at lower energy cost than the conventional route. When we get to Chapter 12 (Hard-to-Abate Sectors), nearly every steel decarbonization pathway is a question about how to displace coke. When we get to Chapter 14 (Geopolitics), graphite will be one of three or four materials that defines the China-West fight. The commodity carbon picture you now have is foundational to all of those.

# What this chapter simplified

**1. "Market size" disagreements.** I gave you market-size ranges and flagged the methodology problem. Different market-research firms include different things and produce numbers that vary by factors of 2–10. The volumes I cited (tonnage) are more reliable than the revenues. For any quantitative use, go back to the underlying tonnage data (USGS for graphite, industry-association data for carbon black) rather than relying on summary numbers.

**2. Coke quality grades.** I treated "metallurgical coke" as a single product. In reality, there are multiple grades (premium hard coking coal → top-grade coke; semi-soft coking coal → lower-grade; ferro-coke; foundry coke; technical coke) with different specs, pricing, and applications. For an actual market participant, these distinctions matter a lot.

**3. The natural vs. synthetic graphite optimization story.** I made it sound like a clean binary; in reality, modern battery anode optimization is a multi-dimensional materials science problem involving particle size, coating, blend ratios, electrolyte compatibility, and target cell chemistry. The "70/30 natural/synthetic" number I cited is a rough industry average, but specific cell makers' formulations vary widely and are often closely held trade secrets.

**4. China's December 2023 export licensing.** I described this as a single event. In reality, China's MOFCOM has been adjusting the list of controlled materials, license processing speeds, and approved destinations on a continuous basis since 2023. The current state may be different from the "as of late 2024" snapshot I gave; this is a regulation-current topic that needs live searching before any consequential analysis.

# Glossary delta (Chapter 2)

- **Anode (battery)** — Electrode where lithium ions intercalate during charging in a Li-ion cell. Almost always graphite-based in current commercial cells.
- **Anode-grade petcoke** — Low-sulfur, low-metals petroleum coke used as a precursor for aluminum smelter anodes and some synthetic graphite. Distinct from fuel-grade petcoke.
- **Battery passport** — EU regulatory regime (effective 2027) requiring cradle-to-gate carbon and material disclosure for batteries above 2 kWh sold in the EU.
- **Blast furnace** — The classical primary steelmaking reactor: tall column where iron ore, coke, and limestone are reduced to molten iron at high temperatures.
- **BF-BOF (Blast Furnace–Basic Oxygen Furnace)** — The dominant primary steelmaking route globally. Coke-intensive, CO₂-intensive (1.8–2.3 tCO₂/t steel).
- **Carbon black** — Engineered amorphous carbon particulate, produced primarily by partial combustion of heavy petroleum oils. Largest use: tire reinforcement.
- **Carbon fiber (CFRP)** — Manufactured carbon filaments, almost always combined with a polymer matrix (epoxy) to form Carbon Fiber Reinforced Polymer composites. Aerospace, wind, hydrogen storage applications.
- **Coke (metallurgical)** — Carbon-rich solid produced by destructive distillation of coking coal in the absence of air. Reducing agent and structural support in blast furnaces.
- **Coking coal** — Premium bituminous coal with the specific properties (volatile content, swelling behavior) needed for coke production. Subset of all bituminous coal.
- **CSPG (Coated Spherical Purified Graphite)** — Battery-anode-grade natural graphite, after spheronization, acid purification, and pyrolytic carbon coating. The product that actually goes into a battery.
- **EAF (Electric Arc Furnace)** — Alternative steelmaking route using electricity to melt scrap or DRI. Lower CO₂ emissions than BF-BOF if powered by clean electricity. Consumes synthetic graphite electrodes.
- **FEOC (Foreign Entity of Concern)** — US regulatory category under the IRA disqualifying battery materials from "foreign entities of concern" (operationally, China and Russia) from EV tax credit eligibility.
- **Flake graphite** — Natural graphite in large-crystal form, the variant most suitable for battery anode processing.
- **Furnace black** — The dominant carbon black production process (partial combustion of carbon black oil).
- **Graphitization** — Thermal process at 2,500–3,000°C to convert disordered carbon precursors (typically needle coke or pitch) into ordered synthetic graphite.
- **Hall-Héroult process** — The conventional electrolytic aluminum smelting process. Consumes ~0.4 t carbon anode per ton of aluminum.
- **Hard carbon** — Disordered amorphous carbon used as a sodium-ion battery anode material. Related to but distinct from activated carbon.
- **MATS (Mercury and Air Toxics Standards)** — US EPA rule, finalized 2012, requiring mercury and air toxics emission controls at coal-fired power plants. Drove powdered activated carbon demand.
- **Met coke** — Industry shorthand for metallurgical coke.
- **Needle coke** — Premium petroleum coke or coal-tar pitch product used as the precursor for synthetic graphite electrodes and battery anodes.
- **PAC (Powdered Activated Carbon)** — Powder-form activated carbon, used for injection in flue gas treatment and in some water-treatment applications.
- **PAN (Polyacrylonitrile)** — The dominant precursor polymer for high-performance carbon fiber.
- **PCI (Pulverized Coal Injection)** — Practice of injecting pulverized coal directly into the blast furnace tuyeres to reduce coke consumption. Cost-saving measure, but limited by the structural role of coke.
- **Petroleum coke (petcoke)** — Carbon residue from petroleum refining. Two main grades: anode (calcined) and fuel.
- **rCB (recovered carbon black)** — Carbon black recovered by pyrolysis of end-of-life tires. Emerging circular-economy product.
- **Spherical graphite** — Natural graphite that has been micronized and mechanically rounded to ~10–25 micron spherical particles, the form required for battery anode use.
- **Synthetic graphite** — Manufactured graphite produced by high-temperature graphitization of needle coke or pitch precursors. Higher cost and energy intensity than natural graphite, but more consistent properties.
