**Track:** Forward View (C)

## Why this chapter exists

Every preceding chapter has been, in some sense, descriptive: here is the chemistry, here is the market, here is the policy, here is the firm, here is the geopolitics. This chapter is different. It asks the question the whole book has been building toward: **given everything we now understand, where does this actually go?**

That requires a different kind of honesty. Forecasting the carbon future is a domain saturated with motivated reasoning — optimists who assume every cost curve bends down on schedule, pessimists who assume every promise fails, advocates whose scenarios are arguments in disguise. The discipline this chapter tries to model is the one the book has practiced throughout: state what is genuinely known, flag what is contested, and be explicit about what is simply unknown. The most useful thing a final chapter can do is not predict — it is to equip you to read other people's predictions critically.

I'll organize this in seven parts: where current policy actually points; the AI-and-energy collision; carbon-to-products and the utilization question; the engineered-removal frontier; solar geoengineering (the elephant in the room); a few honest scenarios for 2050; and the closing synthesis — what works, what doesn't, what we don't know.

A note on this chapter's epistemic status, since it matters more here than anywhere else in the book: the further out it looks, the more it is *informed speculation* rather than reportage. Where it gives numbers, they are scenario figures with wide error bars, not forecasts. Read them as the shape of the possibility space, not as predictions.

# Part 1: Where current policy actually points

## The emissions gap

The single most important number in climate policy is the **gap** between where current policies lead and where the temperature goals require. Strip away the rhetoric and the picture as of the mid-2020s is sobering and reasonably stable across the major assessments (UNEP's annual *Emissions Gap Report*, the IEA, Climate Action Tracker):

- **Current policies** (what governments are actually doing) point toward roughly **2.5–2.9°C** of warming by 2100.
- **Full implementation of all pledges and NDCs** (including conditional and net-zero promises, many not backed by policy) would bring that down toward roughly **2.1–2.5°C**.
- The **Paris goals** require ~1.5°C (with limited overshoot) or "well below 2°C."

The gap between current policy (~2.7°C) and 1.5°C is enormous, and it has narrowed only modestly over a decade of negotiations. The encouraging counterpoint: a decade ago, pre-Paris trajectories pointed toward 3.5–4°C, so the combination of policy and — more importantly — the collapse in clean-technology costs has bent the curve meaningfully. We are no longer on the worst-case path. We are on a bad-but-not-catastrophic path, drifting slowly toward a better one, far too slowly to hit the headline target.

## Is 1.5°C "alive"?

The honest answer, by the mid-2020s, is: **not as originally framed.** Holding warming to 1.5°C with no or limited overshoot required global emissions to peak immediately and fall ~43% by 2030 — and global emissions have not yet clearly peaked. The remaining 1.5°C carbon budget (Ch. 4) is, on most accounting, only a few years of current emissions. Barring an implausibly abrupt break in the trajectory, **the world will overshoot 1.5°C** — temporarily exceed it, then attempt to come back down later in the century using net-negative emissions (large-scale CDR — Ch. 11).

This reframes the entire forward problem around two concepts:

- **Overshoot.** Peak warming above the target, followed by a decline. The deeper and longer the overshoot, the greater the risk of triggering irreversible tipping points (ice-sheet collapse, permafrost carbon release, ecosystem state shifts) that no later cooling can undo. Overshoot is not symmetric: the damages during the peak are real even if temperature later falls.
- **Net-negative dependence.** Coming back down requires removing CO₂ at gigatonne scale for decades — which is why the IPCC's 1.5°C-consistent pathways assume **5–10 GtCO₂/year of *novel* CDR by mid-century** (Ch. 11). Current *durable* (novel) CDR is well under a megatonne per year — against ~2 Gt/yr of conventional, mostly land-based removal (Ch. 11). The pathways quietly assume a several-thousand-fold scale-up of the durable-removal industry, which barely exists. This is the largest single act of faith embedded in the scenarios.

## The "delivery gap" beneath the emissions gap

There is a gap beneath the gap. Even the pledges that exist are frequently not backed by the policy, capital, or institutional capacity to deliver them — the "implementation gap." Net-zero targets cover most of global GDP and emissions on paper (Ch. 13), but the share backed by detailed, costed, legislated plans is far smaller. The 2025 reversals (US OBBBA and Paris withdrawal, the EU's CSRD retrenchment — Ch. 13–14) widened this gap by pulling back commitments that had looked firm. The lesson the book keeps returning to: **commitments are reversible, and the ratchet can run backward.**

## **Stop-and-check 15.A**

1. Distinguish the emissions gap, the implementation gap, and the delivery gap. Why does each matter separately?
2. A decade ago the world was on a ~4°C path; now it's on ~2.7°C. Is that progress to celebrate or a failure to lament? Defend a position that holds both.
3. Explain why overshoot is "not symmetric" — why temporarily exceeding 1.5°C and coming back is not the same as never exceeding it.
4. **Socratic prompt:** The 1.5°C pathways assume a several-thousand-fold scale-up of an industry (durable CDR) that barely exists. Is relying on that assumption prudent (we'll need it, so build it) or reckless (it licenses delay today against a removal that may never materialize)? Can it be both?

**Answers**

1. The emissions gap is the distance between projected emissions under current policy and the levels consistent with temperature goals — the headline ~2.7°C-vs-1.5°C divergence. The implementation gap sits beneath it: the difference between the pledges and net-zero targets that exist on paper and the policy actually enacted to hit them. The delivery gap is narrower still: even where policy exists, the capital, institutional capacity, and legislated plans to deliver it frequently do not. Each matters separately because closing one does not close the others — stronger pledges (closing the emissions gap) mean nothing without enacted policy (implementation) and without the means to execute it (delivery).

2. Both, defensibly. It is real progress: bending the trajectory from ~4°C to ~2.7°C over a decade avoids the worst-case, civilization-stressing outcomes, and was driven substantially by the clean-tech cost collapse — not a given. It is also a failure to lament: ~2.7°C still overshoots 1.5°C badly, the gap has narrowed only modestly through a decade of negotiation, and the damages at that level are severe. The honest position holds both — we have moved off the catastrophic path but remain far from the safe one, drifting toward something better far too slowly to hit the headline target.

3. Overshoot is asymmetric because the damages incurred during the peak are real and partly irreversible, even if temperature later falls. Temporarily exceeding 1.5°C can trigger tipping points — ice-sheet collapse, permafrost carbon release, ecosystem state shifts — that no subsequent cooling can undo; the system does not simply retrace its path back down. Coming back also depends on net-negative removal at a scale that may not materialize. So "peak at 1.6°C then return" is not equivalent to "never exceed 1.5°C": the former locks in damage and risk that the latter avoids entirely, making the path-dependence, not just the endpoint, what matters.

4. It can be both, and recognizing that is the point. It is prudent in that the pathways genuinely need gigatonne removal, the industry will not exist unless someone builds it now, and early investment is how cost curves bend — so treating CDR as a necessary buildout is rational. It is reckless when the assumption functions as a *plan*: if "we'll remove it later" licenses slower abatement today, it bets the climate on a several-thousand-fold scale-up of an industry that may hit a cost or thermodynamic floor and never arrive. The resolution is to build CDR aggressively as insurance while refusing to let its assumed future scale substitute for near-term emission cuts — fund the removal, but do not borrow against it.

# Part 2: The AI-and-energy collision

## The demand shock no one priced in

The most consequential development of the mid-2020s that almost no climate scenario anticipated is the explosion of **electricity demand from AI and data centers.** After roughly two decades of flat electricity demand in most wealthy economies (efficiency gains offsetting growth), AI compute has reversed the trend abruptly.

The numbers, from the IEA's analysis (current as of writing):

- Global data-center electricity consumption is on track to **roughly double from ~485 TWh in 2025 to ~950 TWh by 2030**, reaching around **3% of global electricity demand**.
- Data-center electricity use grew **~17% in 2025 alone**, far outpacing the ~3% growth in overall electricity demand.
- **AI-specific** data-center consumption is growing fastest — roughly tripling between 2025 and 2030 — and could rise from a small share of data-center power today to 35–50% by 2030.
- CO₂ emissions from data-center electricity are projected to **peak around 320 MtCO₂ by 2030** before a shallow decline — material, though still a small slice of the ~37 Gt global total.
- The stress is intensely **regional**: in Ireland data centers already consume around a fifth of national electricity (potentially ~32% by 2026); Virginia, Oregon, and parts of Texas face acute local grid strain.

## Why it cuts both ways

The instinct is to treat AI demand as simply bad for the climate — more load, more emissions, more pressure to keep gas and coal online. That is half the picture. The honest accounting is a **two-sided ledger**:

**The cost side.** Surging, inflexible, around-the-clock demand for "firm" power is straining grids and, in the near term, propping up fossil generation. It has triggered a scramble for any available firm capacity — gas turbine order books are full, coal-plant retirements have been delayed in some markets, and there is a genuine revival of **nuclear** (including the high-profile reopening or life-extension of plants and a wave of small-modular-reactor and corporate-PPA deals with hyperscalers). The transition's hardest-won assumption — that wealthy-economy electricity demand was flat, so clean additions could *displace* fossils rather than just *add* to supply — has broken.

**The benefit side.** AI is also a tool for the transition: optimizing grids and demand response, accelerating materials discovery (better batteries, catalysts, cements), improving climate and weather modeling, and increasing efficiency across industry. Some of the largest clean-power purchasers in history are now the hyperscalers, whose demand is funding new wind, solar, geothermal, and nuclear at scale. Whether AI is net-positive or net-negative for emissions depends on whether its demand is met with clean firm power (it accelerates the buildout) or fossil firm power (it entrenches it) — and on whether its capabilities actually accelerate decarbonization elsewhere by more than its own footprint.

The unresolved question — and it is genuinely unresolved — is which side dominates, and over what timeframe. The near-term effect is clearly to *raise* emissions and strain grids; the long-term effect is contingent on choices being made right now about how the new load is powered.

## **Stop-and-check 15.B**

1. Why did flat electricity demand in wealthy economies matter so much for the transition, and what breaks when it suddenly grows?
2. Lay out the two-sided ledger for AI and climate. What single factor most determines which side wins?
3. AI demand has revived nuclear and propped up gas. Are those the same kind of event (both "firm power") or fundamentally different for the climate?
4. **Socratic prompt:** A hyperscaler says its new data center is "100% renewable" via PPAs but draws around-the-clock from a grid that fires gas to meet its nighttime load. Is the claim true, misleading, or both? Tie this back to the matching/attribute problems of Chapter 8 and the green-claims law of Chapter 13.

**Answers**

1. Flat demand was the load-bearing assumption behind a clean transition in wealthy economies: if total electricity use is not growing, every gigawatt of new wind or solar can *displace* fossil generation rather than merely add to supply, so the grid actually decarbonizes as clean capacity comes on. When demand suddenly grows — as AI/data-center load is doing, up ~17% in 2025 against ~3% overall — clean additions get absorbed by the new demand instead of retiring fossils, and the scramble for firm power can prop up gas and coal. The transition stops being a substitution and becomes a race between clean additions and rising load.

2. Cost side: surging, inflexible, around-the-clock demand strains grids and props up fossil generation in the near term — full gas-turbine order books, delayed coal retirements, the assumption that clean additions displace fossils broken. Benefit side: AI is also a transition tool — optimizing grids and demand response, accelerating discovery of better batteries, catalysts, and cements, improving climate modeling — and hyperscalers are now among the largest clean-power purchasers in history, funding new wind, solar, geothermal, and nuclear. The single factor that most determines which side wins is whether the new load is met with clean firm power (accelerates the buildout) or fossil firm power (entrenches it).

3. They are both "firm power" — dispatchable, around-the-clock generation that the inflexible AI load demands — but for the climate they are fundamentally different. Reviving nuclear adds low-carbon firm capacity, so meeting AI demand that way is broadly consistent with decarbonization. Propping up gas (and delaying coal retirements) meets the same firmness need with fossil generation, directly raising emissions. The shared "firm power" label conceals the decisive difference: firmness is the grid attribute AI requires, but carbon intensity is what determines whether satisfying that requirement helps or harms the climate. Same function, opposite climate consequence.

4. Both. It is true in the narrow accounting sense — annual PPA purchases of renewable attributes may match or exceed annual consumption, so on a yearly-megawatt-hour basis the center is "100% renewable." It is misleading because the physical grid fires gas to meet the nighttime load the PPAs do not temporally cover: this is exactly Chapter 8's matching/attribute problem, where an attribute certificate is averaged across time and location rather than matched hour-by-hour to actual draw. Under Chapter 13's green-claims law (the EU Empowering Consumers Directive, FTC scrutiny), an unqualified "100% renewable" claim that ignores this temporal mismatch is increasingly legally hazardous — the credible version is hourly/24-7 matched clean power, not annual netting.

# Part 3: Carbon-to-products and the utilization question

## The seductive idea

There is a perennially attractive vision: instead of paying to bury CO₂ (Ch. 10) or to remove it (Ch. 11), *use* it — turn captured carbon into fuels, chemicals, plastics, and building materials, creating a revenue stream that funds capture and closes the loop. This is the "U" in CCUS and the frontier of the CO₂-molecule value chain from Chapter 3. The companies are real and well-funded: **LanzaTech** (microbial conversion of waste CO/CO₂ into ethanol and chemicals), **Twelve** (CO₂ electrolysis to syngas, fuels, and materials), **Solidia** and **CarbonCure** (CO₂ mineralized into concrete), the e-fuels makers (CO₂ + green hydrogen → methanol, jet — Ch. 3, Ch. 12), and the CO₂-to-polymers firms (Covestro, Econic).

## The honest taxonomy

The hard discipline here is separating utilization pathways by what they actually do for the climate, because they differ by orders of magnitude:

**Durable storage as product (genuinely climate-useful).** When CO₂ is mineralized into concrete or aggregate, the carbon is locked into a stable solid for a very long time. **CO₂-cured concrete and mineralized aggregates** are the most credible utilization route: durable, scalable in principle (the world pours enormous volumes of concrete — Ch. 12), and they can also reduce cement demand. The quantities of CO₂ stored per tonne of concrete are modest, but the total addressable volume is large.

**Short-lived products (climate-neutral at best, often worse).** When CO₂ is turned into a **fuel** (e-methanol, e-jet) or a short-lived chemical, the carbon is re-released within months to a few years when the fuel is burned or the product degrades. This is not removal; at best it is *recycling* one molecule of fossil-or-air carbon through a use-phase before it returns to the atmosphere. It can displace virgin fossil carbon (valuable for hard-to-abate transport — Ch. 12) but only genuinely helps if the CO₂ feedstock is biogenic or direct-air-captured *and* the energy input is clean. With fossil-derived CO₂ and grid power, "CO₂ utilization" can be a net emitter dressed as a climate solution.

**The thermodynamic tax.** The inconvenient physics: CO₂ is the low-energy, stable end-state of combustion. Turning it back into a fuel or chemical means pushing it *back up* the energy hill — which costs a great deal of energy. That energy must be clean and cheap, or the whole exercise emits more than it saves. This is why e-fuels are expensive (Ch. 12) and why the scale of climate-useful utilization is constrained: you are fighting the second law of thermodynamics, and it charges a steep toll.

## The realistic verdict

Utilization is real and useful in specific niches — durable mineralization in building materials, and clean-energy-powered e-fuels for the transport modes that have no better option (aviation, shipping — Ch. 12). It is *not* a general-purpose answer to the carbon problem, and claims that we can "just use the CO₂" usually founder on either the short product lifetime or the thermodynamic tax. The total volume of CO₂ that utilization can durably consume is small relative to the gigatonnes that must be removed or stored. Utilization is a portfolio member, not the portfolio.

## **Stop-and-check 15.C**

1. Why is CO₂-cured concrete climate-useful in a way that CO₂-derived jet fuel mostly is not? Be precise about where the carbon ends up.
2. Explain the "thermodynamic tax" on utilization. Why does it make the *cleanliness and cost of the energy input* the decisive variable?
3. An e-fuel made from fossil-flue-gas CO₂ and grid electricity is marketed as a climate solution. Construct the case that it could be a net emitter.
4. **Socratic prompt:** Utilization creates a revenue stream that could fund capture. Does that make it valuable even if the climate benefit is marginal — i.e., is "it pays for the capture infrastructure we'll need anyway" a good argument or a rationalization?

**Answers**

1. The decisive question is where the carbon ends up and for how long. In CO₂-cured concrete the carbon is mineralized into a stable solid and locked in for a very long time — durable storage as product — so it genuinely keeps that carbon out of the atmosphere. In CO₂-derived jet fuel the carbon is bound only until the fuel is burned, which happens within months to a couple of years, returning it to the air. The concrete route stores; the fuel route at best recycles one molecule through a use-phase before release. So mineralization can be a removal-like sink, while the e-fuel is, at most, a substitution for virgin fossil carbon — not storage.

2. CO₂ is the low-energy, stable end-state of combustion; turning it back into a fuel or chemical means pushing it back *up* the energy hill, which takes a large energy input — the thermodynamic tax. Because that energy is the dominant cost and the dominant emission source, the cleanliness and cost of the energy input become decisive: if the energy is fossil-derived, converting CO₂ can emit more than it saves, and if it is expensive, the product is uncompetitive. Only clean, cheap energy makes utilization climate-useful and affordable, which is precisely why e-fuels are costly and why the climate-useful scale is constrained.

3. Trace the carbon and energy. The feedstock CO₂ comes from a fossil flue gas — it would have been captured and could have been stored, but instead it is released again when the e-fuel burns, so no removal occurs. The conversion runs on grid electricity that is partly fossil-generated, adding fresh emissions to push the stable CO₂ back up the energy hill (the thermodynamic tax). If those conversion-and-energy emissions plus the eventual re-release exceed the emissions of the fossil fuel the e-fuel displaces, the product is a net emitter — yet it is marketed as a climate solution because the CO₂ was "used." Fossil feedstock plus dirty energy is the recipe for that outcome.

4. It is partly a good argument and partly a rationalization, and the line is whether the revenue genuinely builds capacity the climate needs. The good version: utilization revenue can sustain capture plants, supply chains, and learning-by-doing that durable storage and removal will ultimately require, bootstrapping infrastructure that pure-storage economics cannot yet support. The rationalization version: if the "use" is a short-lived product made with dirty energy, the climate benefit is marginal or negative, and "it funds capture we'll need anyway" becomes a cover for an activity that mostly recycles fossil carbon while claiming virtue. The argument holds only when the capture infrastructure it funds is genuinely transferable to durable storage and the utilization itself is not net-emitting.

# Part 4: The engineered-removal frontier

## The scale problem restated

Chapter 11 established the removal taxonomy and the central tension: the 1.5°C pathways assume gigatonnes of durable CDR, the current industry delivers well under a megatonne, and the highest-durability removals (DAC) cost roughly $400–800/tonne today (Ch. 11). The forward question is whether this gap can close — whether engineered removal can scale by three to four orders of magnitude this century at a cost the world can afford.

## Where the frontier is moving

The most dynamic developments (building on Ch. 11):

**Mineralization at scale.** **Heirloom** (accelerating the natural carbonation of crushed minerals to pull CO₂ from air) and **44.01** (injecting CO₂ into peridotite to mineralize it permanently underground) represent the push toward removals that are *permanent by chemistry* — the carbon becomes rock — rather than permanent by management (forests, soils). Permanence-by-chemistry is the holy grail because it sidesteps the reversal risk that plagues nature-based credits (Ch. 8). The constraint is energy and cost.

**The march toward $100/tonne DAC.** The aspirational target — the price at which DAC becomes broadly deployable — is ~$100/tonne, against current costs several times that. Whether it is reachable depends on the same dynamics that drove solar and batteries down their cost curves: scale, learning rates, modular manufacturing, and cheap clean energy. The optimistic case points to those precedents; the skeptical case notes that DAC is fighting thermodynamics (CO₂ is only ~0.04% of air, so you must process enormous volumes) in a way solar panels never had to. The truth is genuinely unknown — DAC may follow solar's trajectory, or it may hit a thermodynamic floor well above $100.

**The buyer's market as scaffolding.** The crucial innovation isn't a technology — it's a *market structure*. Advance purchase commitments (**Stripe's Frontier coalition**, Microsoft's massive removal procurements, the broader advance-market-commitment model — Ch. 8, Ch. 11) are deliberately overpaying for early, expensive removal to fund the learning that brings costs down — the same playbook that scaled vaccines and, arguably, solar. Whether voluntary corporate demand (plus, eventually, compliance demand as removals enter systems like the EU ETS — Ch. 7) can sustain the multi-decade, multi-trillion-dollar investment that gigatonne-scale removal requires is the open question.

## The ocean and soil uncertainty

Two large potential CDR reservoirs are scientifically *unsettled* in ways the engineered routes are not:

- **Ocean CDR** (ocean alkalinity enhancement, ocean iron fertilization, electrochemical methods) could in principle operate at vast scale — the ocean is the planet's largest fast carbon sink. But the science of *measuring* how much carbon is actually durably sequestered, and the *ecological consequences* of altering ocean chemistry at scale, are genuinely immature. This is the frontier where the gap between theoretical potential and verifiable, safe reality is widest.
- **Soil carbon** is similarly large in potential and similarly hard to verify and keep permanent (Ch. 8, Ch. 11) — measurement is noisy, and gains reverse easily with a change in practice.

The honest position: these may become important, or they may not, and right now we cannot reliably tell. The science needs to mature before the credits can be trusted.

## **Stop-and-check 15.D**

1. Why is "permanence by chemistry" (mineralization) considered superior to "permanence by management" (forests)? Connect to the reversal problem from Chapter 8.
2. Make the case for and against DAC reaching $100/tonne, using the solar/battery cost-curve precedent on one side and thermodynamics on the other.
3. Advance market commitments deliberately overpay for early removal. Why is that rational rather than wasteful?
4. **Socratic prompt:** Ocean CDR has the largest theoretical scale and the deepest scientific uncertainty. Should we (a) deploy and learn by doing, (b) research first and deploy only when verified, or (c) avoid it as too risky? What does your answer reveal about your tolerance for type-I vs. type-II errors in a high-stakes domain?

**Answers**

1. Permanence by chemistry turns the CO₂ into stable carbonate rock — the carbon becomes a mineral and stays put on geological timescales, with essentially no reversal risk. Permanence by management (forests, soils) depends on continued human stewardship and favorable conditions: a fire, a harvest, a drought, a policy change, or a land-use shift can re-release the stored carbon. That is exactly the reversal problem that plagues nature-based credits in Chapter 8, where a forest credit can be undone by a wildfire years later. Mineralization sidesteps reversal entirely, which is why it is considered the higher-integrity, holy-grail form of durable storage despite its energy and cost constraints.

2. For $100/tonne: DAC could follow the trajectory of solar and batteries — scale, learning rates, modular mass manufacturing, and access to cheap clean energy drove those technologies down cost curves far faster than experts forecast, and DAC is an early-stage, manufacturable technology that has barely begun that descent. Against: DAC fights thermodynamics in a way solar never did — CO₂ is only ~0.04% of air, so enormous volumes must be processed to capture each tonne, implying an energy and equipment floor that learning may not breach. Solar harvested an abundant input; DAC must concentrate an extremely dilute one. The honest verdict is that it may follow solar's path or hit a thermodynamic floor well above $100, and we do not yet know which.

3. Because the goal of advance market commitments is not to buy cheap removal today but to *create* the industry that delivers cheap removal tomorrow. Deliberately overpaying for early, expensive removal funds the deployment, scale, and learning-by-doing that bend the cost curve down — the same playbook that scaled vaccines and arguably solar. If buyers waited for removal to be cheap, it never would be, because nothing would fund the early scale-up. So the "overpayment" is really an investment in cost reduction and capacity: rational scaffolding for an industry that must grow by orders of magnitude, not waste.

4. There is no risk-free choice, which is what the question exposes. Option (b) — research first, deploy only when verified — is the most defensible default in a high-stakes, poorly understood domain: ocean chemistry alteration at scale carries ecological consequences we cannot yet measure or reverse, and the measurement of actual durable sequestration is immature, so deploying blind risks a large, irreversible mistake (a type-I error: acting on a benefit that is not real or that causes net harm). But favoring (b) over (a) reveals a tolerance for the opposite error — type-II, failing to develop a tool we may desperately need as overshoot deepens. Choosing (c) reveals the strongest aversion to ecological downside; choosing (a) reveals greater fear of missing a needed lever. The right posture is staged: well-governed, bounded research and small reversible trials that reduce uncertainty before any scale deployment.

# Part 5: Solar geoengineering — the elephant in the room

## What it is and why it can't be ignored

Every honest treatment of the carbon future must confront **solar radiation management (SRM)** — deliberately reflecting a small fraction of incoming sunlight to cool the planet, most prominently via **stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI)** (mimicking the cooling effect of large volcanic eruptions) or **marine cloud brightening.** It is not carbon removal — it does nothing about CO₂ concentrations or ocean acidification — it simply masks warming by reducing absorbed sunlight.

It belongs in this chapter for an uncomfortable reason: it is **cheap, fast, and technically within reach**, in a way that nothing else in the book is. Crude estimates put the direct deployment cost of stratospheric injection in the low billions of dollars per year — small enough that a single wealthy state, or even a sufficiently motivated lesser actor, could in principle do it unilaterally. As overshoot becomes more likely (Part 1) and climate damages mount, the pressure to deploy SRM as an emergency measure will grow. Ignoring it does not make it go away; it makes its eventual arrival more dangerous.

## The case for, the case against

**The case for research (not deployment).** If overshoot is coming and tipping points loom, SRM might shave the peak — buying time and reducing the worst damages while removal scales. Given that it may be deployed regardless, we are far better off understanding it — its effects, risks, and governance — than being caught ignorant. Refusing even to research it is a choice to deploy (if it is ever deployed) in the dark.

**The case against.** The objections are formidable and partly irreducible:

- **Termination shock.** If SRM is deployed to mask significant warming and then stopped abruptly (war, economic collapse, political reversal), the masked warming returns extremely fast — far faster than gradual warming, and potentially catastrophically. SRM thus creates a dangerous dependency: once started at scale, stopping is itself a hazard.
- **It treats the symptom, not the disease.** SRM does nothing for CO₂ or ocean acidification; it could even *reduce* the incentive to cut emissions (the **moral hazard** objection — "we can just dim the sun, so why decarbonize?").
- **Distributional and governance chaos.** SRM's effects on monsoons, regional rainfall, and agriculture would be uneven — some regions could be harmed while others benefit. *Who decides* the global thermostat setting? There is no governance framework, and the prospect of unilateral deployment by one actor against the wishes of others is a recipe for conflict.

## The governance vacuum

The honest status: SRM sits in a near-total **governance vacuum.** There is active research, growing (and contested) attention, and essentially no agreed international framework for whether, how, or by whom it could be researched or deployed. This is arguably the single most underexamined high-stakes issue in the entire climate domain — a technology that is cheap and fast colliding with an absence of rules and a fragmenting geopolitics (Ch. 14). The book's position is the cautious-research one: SRM is too dangerous to deploy lightly and too plausible to ignore, which means the urgent task is research and governance, not advocacy or taboo.

## **Stop-and-check 15.E**

1. Why does SRM belong in a carbon book at all, given that it does nothing about carbon concentrations?
2. Explain termination shock. Why does it make SRM uniquely dangerous among climate interventions — a hazard that *grows* the more you rely on it?
3. The moral-hazard objection says SRM reduces the incentive to decarbonize. The research-advocates' reply is "it may happen anyway, so we should understand it." Can both be right?
4. **Socratic prompt:** Suppose a single state, facing catastrophic heat, begins stratospheric injection unilaterally in 2040. Walk through the geopolitical consequences (tie to Ch. 14). What governance, built now, might prevent the worst of it — and is such governance achievable in a fragmenting order?

**Answers**

1. Because it cannot be ignored, and ignoring it makes its eventual arrival more dangerous. SRM does nothing about CO₂ concentrations or ocean acidification — it only masks warming by reflecting sunlight — so it is not a carbon solution. But it belongs in any honest treatment of the carbon future because it is cheap, fast, and technically within reach in a way nothing else in the book is, and as overshoot becomes more likely and damages mount, pressure to deploy it as an emergency measure will grow. The carbon problem's trajectory (deepening overshoot) is precisely what makes SRM increasingly probable, so a book about that trajectory must confront the response it will invite.

2. Termination shock is the rapid return of masked warming if SRM is deployed at scale and then abruptly stopped — by war, economic collapse, or political reversal. Because SRM only masks warming rather than removing its cause, all the warming it was suppressing comes back very fast, far faster than gradual warming would have arrived, and potentially catastrophically because ecosystems and societies cannot adapt at that speed. It is uniquely dangerous because the hazard *grows* with reliance: the more warming you mask and the longer you do it, the more catastrophic an interruption becomes, so scaling up creates a dependency that is itself increasingly dangerous to exit.

3. Yes, both can be right because they address different questions. The moral-hazard objection is about *incentives*: the availability of a cheap "dim the sun" option can reduce political will to do the hard work of cutting emissions, which is a genuine risk. The research advocates' reply is about *preparedness given that SRM may be deployed regardless*: if some actor might do it anyway under duress, we are far safer understanding its effects, risks, and governance than being caught ignorant. One claims research/availability erodes mitigation incentives; the other claims ignorance is more dangerous than knowledge. Both hold simultaneously — which is why the cautious position is bounded research plus governance, not deployment and not taboo.

4. A unilateral 2040 injection would be read as an act with planetary effects imposed on everyone without consent. Tied to Chapter 14's fragmenting, multipolar order: SRM's uneven regional effects — disrupted monsoons, shifted rainfall, harmed agriculture in some regions while others benefit — would create clear winners and losers, inviting accusations of climate aggression, demands to stop (raising termination-shock risk), retaliation, and possibly conflict, with no hegemon to arbitrate and no framework to legitimize or constrain the act. Governance built now that might blunt the worst: an international research-and-monitoring regime, agreed transparency and notification norms, a multilateral decision body for any deployment, and attribution science to adjudicate harms. Whether such governance is achievable is doubtful in exactly the fragmenting order that makes unilateral deployment plausible — which is the core dilemma, and the argument for building the rules before the crisis rather than after.

# Part 6: Scenarios for 2050

Forecasting a single future is folly; sketching a few distinct, internally coherent ones is useful. None of these is a prediction; together they map the possibility space. Each is consistent with the physics and the institutions described in this book.

**Scenario A — Muddle-through (the central case).** Clean-tech costs keep falling; deployment accelerates unevenly; emissions plateau in the late 2020s and decline slowly. The world overshoots 1.5°C, holds somewhere around **2.2–2.6°C**, and limps toward net zero in the 2060s–2070s rather than 2050. CDR scales but to hundreds of megatonnes, not the assumed gigatonnes. Damages are serious but adaptation copes in wealthy regions and struggles elsewhere. This is the trajectory current trends most support — neither catastrophe nor triumph.

**Scenario B — Fragmented acceleration.** Clean-tech cost collapse (driven partly by Chinese overcapacity flooding the world with cheap solar, batteries, and EVs) decarbonizes power and transport faster than policy required, even as geopolitics fragments (Ch. 14). The transition happens *despite* weak cooperation, pulled by economics rather than pushed by treaties. Warming peaks nearer **1.8–2.2°C**. Hard-to-abate sectors and the Global South's development emissions remain the laggards.

**Scenario C — Reversal and overshoot.** Policy backsliding (the 2025 US retreat generalizes; the EU's competitiveness retrenchment deepens; AI/data-center demand entrenches fossil firm power), a clean-tech cost plateau, and geopolitical conflict slow the transition. Emissions fall too slowly; overshoot is deep and long (**2.7°C+**); tipping points are crossed; SRM is deployed under duress with all its attendant risks. The pessimistic but far-from-impossible path.

**Scenario D — Breakthrough.** A combination of cheap, abundant clean firm power (advanced nuclear, geothermal, perhaps fusion), DAC down its cost curve to ~$100/tonne, and AI-accelerated materials and process innovation makes deep decarbonization and gigatonne removal genuinely affordable. Net-negative becomes economically rational. Warming peaks low and declines. The least likely on current evidence, but not foreclosed — cost-curve collapses have repeatedly outrun expert forecasts (solar, batteries, LED lighting, gene sequencing).

The useful exercise is not to bet on one, but to notice that the *difference* between them is driven by a small number of swing variables: the clean-firm-power cost curve, the DAC cost curve, the speed of Chinese clean-tech deployment, the durability of policy, and whether AI demand is met clean or dirty. Those are the things to watch.

## **Stop-and-check 15.F**

1. Scenario B has the transition happening *despite* weak cooperation, pulled by economics. What single assumption is doing the most work, and how would you check whether it's holding?
2. The scenarios differ mainly along a few swing variables. Name them, and for each, name a real-world indicator you could track to tell which scenario you're in.
3. Why is Scenario D (breakthrough) "least likely on current evidence, but not foreclosed"? What's the strongest historical argument that experts systematically *underestimate* cost-curve collapses?
4. **Socratic prompt:** Which scenario does your own reading of this book make you expect — and, separately, which do you *want*? Are they the same? What would it take to move from the one you expect toward the one you want?

**Answers**

1. The assumption doing the most work is that economics alone — clean-tech costs falling below fossil costs — drives decarbonization fast enough *despite* weak cooperation, with Chinese overcapacity flooding the world with cheap solar, batteries, and EVs as the engine. You would check whether it is holding by tracking real deployment and cost data: are solar, battery, and EV cost curves still descending; is clean-tech deployment outrunning what policy required; is Chinese export capacity actually flooding global markets at falling prices. If costs plateau or deployment stalls where policy is weak, the assumption — and Scenario B — is failing.

2. The swing variables are: the clean-firm-power cost curve (track advanced nuclear/geothermal/SMR project costs and PPA prices); the DAC cost curve (track $/tonne from CDR.fyi and company disclosures against the ~$100 target); the speed of Chinese clean-tech deployment (track solar/wind/EV installation and export volumes); the durability of policy (track reversals or reaffirmations — Paris, IRA-type programs, CSRD); and whether AI/data-center demand is met clean or dirty (track the carbon intensity of new firm capacity serving data centers, gas vs. nuclear vs. renewables). Each indicator tells you which scenario the world is drifting toward.

3. It is least likely because it requires *several* favorable cost-curve and innovation outcomes to land together — cheap clean firm power, DAC down to ~$100/tonne, and AI-accelerated materials breakthroughs — and current evidence shows none of those is assured, with DAC in particular fighting thermodynamics. It is not foreclosed because the strongest historical argument is that experts have repeatedly and systematically *underestimated* cost-curve collapses: solar, batteries, LED lighting, and gene sequencing all fell faster and further than mainstream forecasts predicted. The track record of expert pessimism on learning-rate-driven technologies is the reason a breakthrough scenario, while unlikely, cannot be dismissed.

4. This is genuinely yours to answer, but the discipline is to separate the two questions honestly and resist letting the want color the expectation. A clear-eyed reading of the book points most people toward Scenario A (muddle-through) as the expectation — it is the trajectory current trends most support — while many will *want* B or D. The gap between them is the useful thing: naming it forces you to identify which swing variables would have to break favorably (faster clean-firm-power and DAC cost declines, durable policy, clean-powered AI load) to move from the expected path toward the wanted one. The honest move is to state both, notice they differ, and locate the specific levers — not to let optimism masquerade as forecast.

# Part 7: What works, what doesn't, what we don't know

This is the book's closing synthesis. Across every chapter, the most useful organizing question has been: which is this — a thing that works, a thing that doesn't, or a thing we genuinely don't yet know?

## What works (the defensible optimism)

- **Clean-power cost collapse.** Solar, wind, and batteries have fallen in cost faster than almost anyone forecast, and now out-compete fossils for new generation in most of the world. This is the single most important fact in the entire domain, and it was not a given.
- **Compliance carbon pricing, where it exists.** The EU ETS and the durable subnational US systems demonstrably price carbon, raise revenue, and have contributed to power-sector decarbonization (Ch. 7). The instrument works where it is allowed to.
- **Electrification of light transport and low-temperature heat.** EVs and heat pumps are mature, improving, and scaling.
- **A few hard-to-abate beachheads.** Commercial cement CCS (Brevik), green-steel pilots, and a real shipping framework (the IMO 2025 agreement) show the hardest sectors are not hopeless (Ch. 12).
- **Policy-manufactured revenue floors, where governments hold them.** The single most recurring pattern in this book — a public floor (Norway's Longship and the UK's CCS-CfD, 45Q/45V, CDR advance market commitments, SAF mandates) de-risking a high-capital, long-payback asset until private capital will finance it — is the mechanism behind nearly every hard-tech project that has actually reached FID (Ch. 2, 3, 10, 11, 12). It works *when the floor is durable*; its failure mode is the reversibility problem below.

## What doesn't work (the defensible pessimism)

- **The voluntary market as a substitute for reductions.** The 2022–2024 crisis showed that cheap avoidance offsets largely did not deliver the climate benefit claimed (Ch. 8). Offsets-as-substitute does not work; the market is being painfully rebuilt around removals and integrity.
- **Relying on global cooperation to set a single price.** Three decades of negotiation produced a fragmented patchwork, not a global price (Ch. 9, Ch. 14). The top-down dream does not work; the bottom-up reality is what we have.
- **Treating commitments as durable.** Net-zero pledges, the IRA, the SEC rule, the GFANZ alliances, the CSRD — all proved reversible under political and economic pressure (Ch. 13, Ch. 14). Promises without legislated, capitalized delivery do not work.
- **Scenarios that assume gigatonne CDR on schedule.** The pathways assume a removal industry that does not yet exist at anything like the required scale. The assumption, treated as a plan, does not work — though the industry might still be built.

## What we don't know (the honest uncertainty)

- **Whether DAC and engineered removal reach affordable gigatonne scale** — or hit a cost floor that strands the 1.5°C pathways.
- **Whether AI's energy demand nets out positive or negative** for the transition.
- **Whether ocean and soil CDR are safe, verifiable, and large** — the science is not settled.
- **Whether and how SRM gets deployed and governed** — a cheap, fast, ungoverned technology meeting a fragmenting world.
- **How deep and long the overshoot will be**, and whether it triggers irreversible tipping points.
- **Whether the clean-tech cost collapse continues** down the curve or plateaus.

## The closing frame

The carbon problem is, in the end, two problems wearing one name — which is where this book began (Ch. 1). There is **carbon the material**: the graphite, the coke, the CO₂ molecule, the fossil stock, the physical and industrial economy of an element (Track A). And there is **carbon the liability**: the emissions, the accounting, the markets, the policy, the corporate and geopolitical apparatus built around carbon as a pollutant (Tracks B and C). The reason the field is so confusing — the reason "a ton of carbon" can mean five different things and "net zero" can mean anything from rigorous to meaningless — is that these two carbons share a name and are constantly conflated.

The single most valuable thing this book can leave you with is the habit of asking, of any carbon claim, instrument, or number: *which carbon is this, what exactly is being measured, against what baseline, by whom, and how durable is it?* That question — applied relentlessly, without optimism or pessimism, to whatever the next decade throws up — is the real curriculum. The facts in this book will age; that discipline will not.

## **Stop-and-check 15.G**

1. Of the items in "what works," which is most load-bearing — the one whose failure would most damage the whole effort? Defend your choice.
2. "Treating commitments as durable doesn't work" is a strong claim built on the 2025 reversals. Is it a permanent truth about climate politics, or an artifact of one bad cycle?
3. Pick one item from "what we don't know" and explain what evidence, in the next five years, would move it into "what works" or "what doesn't."
4. **Socratic prompt:** The book's closing claim is that a *habit of inquiry* matters more than any fact, because the facts will age. Is that wisdom or an evasion — a way of declining to say what will actually happen? What would you have wanted the book to commit to, and could it honestly have done so?

**Answers**

1. The most load-bearing item is the clean-power cost collapse. The book itself calls it the single most important fact in the entire domain — it is what bent the trajectory from ~4°C toward ~2.7°C, what makes clean generation out-compete fossils for new build, and what underwrites the "economics drives the transition" hope in the fragmented order where cooperation fails. If solar, wind, and battery costs had not fallen and instead plateaued or reversed, almost everything else — electrification, the Chinese-deployment engine, the affordability of the whole buildout — would unravel, and policy alone could not carry it. The other items matter, but they ride on cheap clean power.

2. It is probably more durable than "one bad cycle," though not a timeless law. The claim rests not only on the 2025 reversals (US OBBBA and Paris withdrawal, the CSRD retrenchment, the SEC rule, GFANZ) but on a structural pattern: voluntary and even legislated commitments lack the self-enforcing delivery — capital, legislation, institutional lock-in — that would make them stick across political cycles. As long as climate policy depends on contestable political majorities and imposes near-term costs for diffuse long-term benefits, reversibility is a standing feature, not an accident. What would soften it is when decarbonization becomes economically self-sustaining (cheaper than the alternative), at which point markets, not promises, carry it — making the claim contingent on that transition, not permanent.

3. Take "whether AI's energy demand nets out positive or negative." Evidence over five years that would move it toward "what works": the carbon intensity of new firm capacity serving data centers falls because the load is met largely with nuclear, geothermal, and clean firm power; hyperscaler clean-power procurement demonstrably funds net-new clean capacity rather than absorbing existing supply; and AI-driven efficiency and discovery gains (grid optimization, better batteries/catalysts) produce measurable emissions reductions exceeding the data-center footprint. Evidence toward "what doesn't": new data-center load is met by gas with delayed coal retirements, data-center CO₂ overshoots the ~320 Mt projection, and claimed AI efficiency benefits fail to materialize at scale.

4. It is more wisdom than evasion, but the charge has teeth. The book is candid that the further out it looks the more it is informed speculation with wide error bars, and it does commit to specifics where the evidence supports them — the ~2.7°C trajectory, near-certain 1.5°C overshoot, the swing variables to watch, and explicit scenarios. So elevating the *habit of inquiry* is not a refusal to say anything; it is honesty about which claims will age (the numbers) and which will not (the method). Where the critique bites: a reader might reasonably want a firmer probabilistic commitment — say, which scenario is most likely and with what confidence. The book could honestly have committed to "muddle-through is the central case" (it nearly does), but a single point-prediction of 2050 outcomes it could not have made honestly, because the swing variables genuinely are unresolved.

## **Stop-and-check 15.H (whole book)**

1. A startup advertises "carbon-negative jet fuel — every gallon removes more CO₂ than it emits." Apply the book's five-part framing — *which carbon, measured how, against what baseline, by whom, how durable* — to work out what you'd need to know to believe it.
2. The book's organizing claim is that carbon is simultaneously a *material* and a *liability*, and that conflating the two seeds most of the field's confusion. Give one concrete case from the book where the *same physical carbon* is both at once, and show how keeping the senses separate resolves a confusion.
3. "A ton of carbon," "carbon price," "carbon neutral," "net zero," "CDR" — each was shown to carry several distinct meanings. What is the single cognitive error underneath all of them, and what one habit defends against it?
4. **Socratic prompt:** The book declines to tell you what will happen and instead hands you a method. Take a real carbon claim you expect to encounter in the next year and walk through how you'd interrogate it. What's the first question you ask — and why is it almost always the one that does the most work?

**Answers**

1. The framing decomposes the slogan into checkable parts. *Which carbon?* "Carbon-negative" asserts net atmospheric removal, so the unit is CO₂ (and CO₂e for any supply-chain methane), and you must know whether the feedstock CO₂ is fossil, biogenic, or direct-air-captured — only atmospheric or biogenic carbon could even in principle make a fuel net-negative. *Measured how?* On a full well-to-wake lifecycle including the energy to synthesize the fuel (the thermodynamic tax) and any methane leakage — not tailpipe alone. *Against what baseline?* Negative relative to *what* — the fossil jet fuel displaced, or zero? *By whom?* Certified under which standard, and is the same attribute also claimed by a compliance regime (the double-counting/additionality trap of Ch. 8)? *How durable?* A burned fuel re-releases its carbon within months, so any "negative" must come from durable storage *elsewhere* in the system, not the fuel itself. The likely verdict: "carbon-negative jet fuel" is almost certainly an avoided-emissions or bundled-removal-credit claim, not a fuel that is physically net-removing — and the five questions are exactly what expose that.

2. Graphite is the cleanest case (Ch. 2, Ch. 14). As a *material* it is the battery anode — a precision-engineered commodity and a strategic chokepoint China dominates. As a *liability* its synthetic production is highly CO₂-intensive (graphitization energy) and its trade is an embedded-carbon/CBAM question. The same physical carbon both *enables* the transition (anode material) and *emits* in its making (embodied carbon) — so a policy that sees only the liability (tax its embodied carbon) can raise the cost of the very clean-energy buildout that needs it. Keeping the two senses separate lets you hold both truths at once instead of collapsing into "graphite is green" or "graphite is dirty." (Metallurgical coke, the CO₂ molecule itself, and biomass are other cases where one carbon is simultaneously commodity and emission.)

3. The common error is treating a *word* as if it named one well-defined thing when it names several — letting a single label smuggle in an unstated choice of unit, boundary, baseline, counterfactual, or durability. "A ton of carbon" hides tC-vs-tCO₂; "carbon price" hides market-price vs. tax vs. shadow vs. damage-estimate; "net zero" hides reduced vs. removed vs. offset and the durability of the offset. The one habit that defends against all of them is to refuse the label and demand its operationalization — *which carbon, measured how, against what baseline, by whom, how durable* — before evaluating the claim. The discipline is to force the implicit choices into the open; almost every confusion, and most greenwashing, lives in the gap an unexamined label leaves.

4. There's no single right claim to pick, but the discipline is invariant. The first question is almost always *which carbon, and measured against what baseline?* — because it is the one that most often dissolves the claim. Most carbon claims are really *counterfactual* claims ("avoided," "reduced," "lower than," "removes more than"), and a counterfactual is only as sound as its baseline — which is exactly where additionality, REDD+ overstatement, REC-washing, negative-CI fuel scores, and "carbon-negative" slogans all break down. Pin down the unit and the baseline and you have usually already found the weak point: you know whether you're looking at a genuine removal or a counterfactual comparison, whether the attribute is additional or already claimed, and whether the durability matches the emission being offset. That question does the most work because the field's confusion is overwhelmingly a matter of *baselines and counterfactuals dressed as facts* — establish those and the rest of the evaluation follows. That, in the end, is the book.

# Closing exercise

Three things to take away — from this chapter and, really, from the whole book:

**1. We are on a bad-but-improving path, far too slow for the headline target.** Current policy points to ~2.5–2.9°C; the trajectory has bent down from ~4°C a decade ago, driven mostly by collapsing clean-tech costs; 1.5°C will almost certainly be overshot. The future hinges on a handful of swing variables — clean-firm-power and DAC cost curves, Chinese deployment speed, policy durability, and how AI's demand is powered — not on any single treaty or technology.

**2. The frontier is real but oversold in pieces.** Utilization helps in narrow niches (durable mineralization, clean e-fuels for hard-to-abate transport) but fights thermodynamics; engineered removal must scale by orders of magnitude and may or may not get cheap; ocean/soil CDR is scientifically unsettled; and solar geoengineering is cheap, fast, dangerous, and ungoverned — the most underexamined high-stakes issue in the field.

**3. The discipline outlasts the facts.** Every number in this book will change. What endures is the habit of asking, of any carbon claim: *which carbon, measured how, against what baseline, by whom, how durable?* Carbon is simultaneously a material and a liability, and almost all the confusion in the field comes from conflating the two. Keeping them straight — without optimism or pessimism — is the whole point.

# What this chapter simplified

**1. The scenario quantifications.** The temperature ranges are rounded synthesis figures across UNEP, IEA, and Climate Action Tracker assessments, each with wide error bars and differing methodologies. Real scenario analysis (the IPCC's pathway database, integrated assessment models) is vastly more detailed and more uncertain than my four sketches suggest.

**2. The technology specifics.** Each frontier technology (DAC variants, ocean CDR methods, SRM delivery mechanisms, fusion, advanced geothermal, SMRs) is its own deep field with its own literature, costs, and uncertainties. I gave the strategic shape, not the engineering.

**3. The AI-energy numbers.** Data-center demand forecasts are moving fast and vary widely by source; the IEA figures I used are one credible estimate among several, and the field is changing quarter to quarter.

**4. Adaptation.** This book — and this chapter — focused almost entirely on *mitigation* (reducing and removing emissions). The other half of the climate response, *adaptation* (sea walls, drought-resistant crops, heat resilience, managed retreat), is a vast domain I barely touched. As overshoot becomes likely, adaptation grows in relative importance.

**5. The human dimension.** Justice, equity, the distribution of harms and of transition costs, climate migration, and the politics of who bears the burden run through every chapter and deserve fuller treatment than a markets-and-materials book gave them.

# Glossary delta (Chapter 15)

- **DAC cost curve** — The trajectory of direct-air-capture cost over time; the ~$100/tonne target is the threshold for broad deployability. Whether DAC follows solar's cost collapse or hits a thermodynamic floor is unknown.
- **Emissions gap** — The difference between projected emissions under current policy/pledges and the levels consistent with temperature goals. Current policy points to ~2.5–2.9°C.
- **Implementation/delivery gap** — The gap between pledges that exist and the policy, capital, and capacity to deliver them.
- **Marine cloud brightening** — An SRM method that brightens low marine clouds to reflect more sunlight.
- **Mineralization (engineered)** — Converting CO₂ into stable carbonate rock for permanence-by-chemistry; e.g., Heirloom (air), 44.01 (peridotite injection).
- **Net-negative emissions** — Removing more CO₂ than is emitted; required globally to draw temperatures back down after overshoot.
- **Overshoot** — Temporarily exceeding a temperature target before returning below it, relying on later net-negative emissions; carries irreversible tipping-point risk during the peak.
- **SRM (Solar Radiation Management)** — Deliberately reflecting incoming sunlight to cool the planet; does not address CO₂. Includes stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) and marine cloud brightening.
- **Stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI)** — The leading SRM concept: injecting reflective aerosols into the stratosphere, mimicking volcanic cooling. Cheap, fast, and dangerous (termination shock, governance vacuum).
- **Termination shock** — The rapid return of masked warming if SRM is deployed at scale and then abruptly stopped; the defining hazard of SRM dependence.
- **Thermodynamic tax** — The large clean-energy input required to convert stable CO₂ back into fuels/chemicals; the physical constraint limiting climate-useful utilization.
- **Utilization (CCU)** — Using captured CO₂ as feedstock; climate-useful only for durable products (mineralized building materials) or clean-energy-powered e-fuels displacing fossil carbon.
