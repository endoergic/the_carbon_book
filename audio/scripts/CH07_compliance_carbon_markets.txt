Chapter 7, Compliance Carbon Markets.

Why this chapter exists.

Compliance carbon markets are where most carbon is actually priced. They are the largest single climate-policy lever in operation today: the E.U. E.T.S. alone has raised 245 euros billion in auction revenue since 2005 and prices roughly 40 percent of E.U. greenhouse gas emissions. Globally, compliance systems cover about 24 percent of greenhouse gas emissions, and that share is growing as more jurisdictions launch or expand systems (China's national E.T.S., India's planned CCTS, Brazil's pending system, Mexico's full-launch, Indonesia's expansion).

The substantive importance of compliance markets is twofold. First, they directly affect operating decisions in covered sectors, power, heavy industry, aviation, increasingly road transport and buildings. Second, they generate revenue that flows to government budgets (and in some jurisdictions, redistribution programs), creating a political-economic constituency for continued strengthening.

This chapter focuses on operational compliance markets, cap-and-trade systems imposed by law, where covered entities must surrender allowances or face penalties. Voluntary markets (Chapter 8) operate under entirely different logic and are best understood as a separate institution that shares some technical features.

The chapter is organized in eight parts: cap-and-trade fundamentals, the E.U. E.T.S. (the most consequential system), C.B.A.M. (the E.U.'s import-side counterpart), the North American systems (WCI, Reggie, Washington), other major markets (China, U.K., Korea, others), the broader case for and against cap-and-trade, the carbon-tax alternative, and fuel-standard regimes (a structurally distinct compliance instrument).

Part 1: Cap-and-trade fundamentals.

The basic mechanism.

A cap-and-trade system has three core elements:

1. The cap. A legal limit on total emissions from covered entities over a compliance period (typically annual). The cap is denominated in tons of CO2-equivalent and declines over time toward a target emissions level by a target date.

2. The allowances. The cap is divided into one-ton emission allowances. Each allowance permits the emission of one ton of CO2-equivalent by a covered entity. Allowances are distributed via some combination of:
Free allocation, given to certain emitters (typically industries deemed at risk of "carbon leakage", losing competitiveness to non-covered foreign producers).
Auction, sold by the regulator to covered entities and (in some systems) financial intermediaries.

3. The compliance obligation. Covered entities must, at the end of each compliance period, surrender (give back) one allowance for each ton of CO2-equivalent they emitted. Entities that fail to surrender enough allowances face penalties, typically large per-ton fines plus a requirement to make up the shortfall in the next period at a penalty rate.

The "trade" part: allowances are tradeable. An entity that emits less than it was allocated can sell its surplus to an entity that emitted more. The market price emerges from supply (the cap) and demand (covered entities' emissions). Crucially, the system is agnostic about who reduces, as long as total emissions stay under the cap, the system is satisfied. The market identifies the lowest-cost reductions and concentrates them there.

The theoretical case for cap-and-trade is elegant: the regulator sets the quantity (the cap), and the market finds the price. Total emissions are guaranteed; the cost is distributed efficiently.

The reality is messier, and most of this chapter is about the messiness.

Allowance distribution: the political crux.

The biggest design question in any cap-and-trade system is how to distribute the allowances. Free allocation has obvious political appeal, incumbents prefer not paying for permits they used to emit freely. Auctioning has obvious efficiency appeal, making polluters pay produces revenue and avoids windfall gains.

The historical pattern: cap-and-trade systems typically start with heavy free allocation (to bring industry along politically) and shift toward auctioning over time. The E.U. E.T.S. started with ~95 percent free allocation in Phase 1 (2005-2007); by 2025, more than half of EUAs are auctioned. California started with substantial free allocation to industry; auctioning share has been increasing.

Free allocation rules matter enormously:
Grandparenting, allocation based on historical emissions. Easy administratively; rewards historical polluters; creates large windfalls for entities that would have reduced anyway.
Benchmarking, allocation based on production output multiplied by an industry-average emissions intensity. Rewards efficient producers within each sector; the E.U. has used this approach extensively.
Output-based allocation, allocation tied to current production. Avoids the trap of penalizing companies that grow, but weakens the cap's bindingness.
Conditional free allocation, allocation conditional on emission-reduction actions. The E.U.'s 2026-2030 Phase 4 framework moves in this direction for energy efficiency.

Critics have argued that free allocation undermines the entire point of carbon pricing: it gives valuable assets (allowances) to incumbents for free, often producing windfall profits when those companies pass through the opportunity cost in product prices. Defenders argue that free allocation is essential to prevent carbon leakage (production shifting to non-covered jurisdictions) and to maintain political support.

The carbon leakage question is empirically contested. There's some evidence of leakage for cement and steel in particular contexts, less clear evidence for other sectors. C.B.A.M. (Part 3) is the E.U.'s attempt to address leakage through trade policy rather than free allocation.

Banking and borrowing.

Banking lets covered entities save unused allowances for future compliance periods. Almost all major cap-and-trade systems allow banking, often without limits. Banking dampens price volatility and rewards early action, an entity that reduces emissions ahead of schedule can bank allowances for later use or sale.

Borrowing, using future allowances against current obligations, is generally prohibited or strictly limited, since it undermines cap integrity.

The interaction of banking with cap declines creates the standard cap-and-trade price dynamic: when the market expects future scarcity, current prices rise as entities accumulate banks. When future scarcity expectations weaken, banks get drawn down and prices fall.

Market stability mechanisms.

Cap-and-trade systems can experience price volatility, extreme highs (from supply shocks, regulatory uncertainty) or extreme lows (from over-allocation, demand collapse). Most modern systems include stability mechanisms:

Market Stability Reserve. The E.U.'s primary stabilization tool, introduced in 2019. When the market surplus exceeds a threshold (currently 833 million allowances), 24 percent are pulled into the reserve. When supply gets tight, the MSR can release allowances back. The MSR has become substantially more important than originally envisioned and is itself a subject of reform debate.

Price floor. A minimum auction price below which the regulator won't sell. California uses a floor (currently ~25 dollars per tonne of CO2-equivalent nominal) escalating annually with inflation plus 5 percent. Reggie uses a Reserve Price Trigger that varies by year.

Price ceiling. A maximum price at which the regulator will release additional allowances from a reserve. California has a "price ceiling" (~103 dollars per tonne of CO2-equivalent in 2025) that triggers release of contingency allowances. The E.U. does not have a hard ceiling but the MSR functions similarly under tight supply.

Vintage-specific allowances. Some systems issue allowances dated to specific years; borrowing/banking rules can be vintage-restricted.

These mechanisms exist because the political-economic durability of cap-and-trade depends on price stability. Extreme prices in either direction create pressure for legislative intervention that can undermine the system.

Stop-and-check 7.A.

The cap "guarantees" emissions outcomes. Why do critics argue that real-world caps are weaker guarantees than this framing suggests? (Hint: think about over-allocation, banking, and offsets.)
Free allocation versus auctioning, what's the political-economic logic behind starting with free and shifting to auction? When is free allocation justified, and when does it become a giveaway?
The Market Stability Reserve introduces administrative discretion into a system that's supposed to be market-driven. Is the MSR a feature or a bug?
Socratic prompt: A cap-and-trade system has a stringent declining cap, broad coverage, transparent governance, and a stable price between 50 dollars-100 dollars per tonne of CO2. Is this enough to count as a successful climate policy? What does "successful" require?

Answers

The cap only "guarantees" emissions if it actually binds, and several features loosen it. Over-allocation, issuing more allowances than emissions, as in E.U. Phase 1, means the cap sits above business-as-usual and constrains nothing. Banking lets entities carry surpluses forward indefinitely, so a soft early cap pushes emissions into the future rather than eliminating them. Offsets (where permitted) let covered entities meet obligations with credits of contested integrity rather than real reductions under the cap. Each mechanism converts a nominally hard quantity limit into something more elastic, which is why critics treat the "guarantee" as conditional on stringency and design.

Free allocation buys political entry: incumbents who would fight a system that forces them to buy permits will tolerate one that hands them allowances, so most systems start free to get launched, then shift to auctioning once the institution is entrenched and industry is locked in. Free allocation is justified where genuine carbon-leakage risk exists, production could relocate to uncovered jurisdictions, producing no climate benefit and lost competitiveness. It becomes a giveaway when allocated to sectors not actually exposed to leakage, or when it generates windfall profits because firms pass through the allowance's opportunity cost in prices while paying nothing for it (as documented in E.U. Phase 1).

It is genuinely both. As a bug, the MSR injects administrative discretion into a system whose appeal was that the regulator sets quantity and the market sets price; intake and release thresholds become new policy levers subject to lobbying and reform debate. As a feature, pure quantity systems proved dangerously volatile, over-allocation crushed prices for a decade, and that volatility itself threatened the system's political durability. The MSR trades some market purity for price stability, and because durability is what lets a cap-and-trade system survive long enough to tighten, the trade is defensible even if it is not elegant.

Those four features are necessary but not obviously sufficient. "Successful" depends on what you ask the policy to do. If the test is cost-effective emission reductions within covered sectors, a stringent binding cap at 50 dollars-100 dollars plausibly qualifies. But several things can still be missing: the price may be below the level needed for the hardest abatement (I.P.C.C. scenarios point much higher), coverage may exclude transport, buildings, and agriculture, free allocation may insulate industry from the signal, and the absolute emissions trajectory may not align with a 1.5-2 degrees Celsius pathway. The decisive variables are stringency relative to a climate target, breadth of effective (not just nominal) coverage, and whether complementary policies fill the gaps the price alone cannot.

Part 2: The E.U. E.T.S., the dominant system.

The European Union Emissions Trading System is the world's first major cap-and-trade system, the largest by emissions covered, and the most consequential by financial value and policy precedent. Understanding the E.U. E.T.S. is essential for understanding compliance carbon markets generally.

Architecture.

The E.U. E.T.S. covers approximately 10,000 large industrial facilities plus aviation operators and (since 2024) maritime transport across 30 countries (the E.U.-27 plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway). Coverage includes:

Power generation (electricity and heat production above 20 MW thermal)
Energy-intensive industry, steel, cement, aluminum, fertilizers, refining, chemicals, paper, glass, ceramics
Intra-EEA aviation (domestic and intra-E.U. flights), extra-E.U. flights remain under Corsia (Chapter 8/12)
Maritime transport (>5000 GT vessels), phasing in 2024-2026, with 70 percent of CO2 emissions covered in 2025 and full coverage 2026; methane and nitrous oxide added 2026

A note on Corsia, referenced here and throughout the markets chapters: Corsia (the Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation) is ICAO's scheme requiring international airlines to offset emissions above a baseline by purchasing eligible credits. It is a separate regime from the E.U. E.T.S., intra-EEA flights sit under the E.U. E.T.S., while most international flights sit under Corsia. Because it accepts voluntary-market credits to meet a compliance obligation, it is best understood as a hybrid compliance/voluntary mechanism; it is picked up in Chapter 8 (as a demand source for the voluntary market) and treated in depth in Chapter 12 (aviation).

Total coverage is approximately 40 percent of E.U. greenhouse gas emissions. The remaining 60 percent, transport, buildings, agriculture, smaller industry, F-gas applications, sits under other policy instruments, including the new ETS2 (see "ETS2" in Part 2).

The phases.

The E.U. E.T.S. has gone through four phases, each tightening the system:

Phase 1 (2005-2007): Learning phase. Free allocation based on national plans (grandparenting). Cap was over-allocated; prices collapsed near zero by 2007. But the institutional infrastructure, registries, MRV requirements, compliance procedures, was established.

Phase 2 (2008-2012): Kyoto's first commitment period. Tighter caps, expanded coverage (aviation added 2012), introduction of E.U.-wide cap rather than national caps. Linked to CDM (use of CERs allowed for partial compliance). Prices ranged 10-30 euros per tonne of CO2-equivalent through most of the period before collapsing during the financial crisis aftermath.

Phase 3 (2013-2020): Auctioning takes off. E.U.-wide cap declining 1.74 percent annually. Major shift to auctioning (>50 percent of allowances by end of phase). Free allocation moved to benchmarking. Aviation use of CERs progressively restricted. The Market Stability Reserve was introduced in 2019. Phase 3 prices were depressed through most of the 2010s by oversupply but began rising sharply in 2018-2019.

Phase 4 (2021-2030): Fit for 55. Linear reduction factor, the rate at which the cap declines annually, increased to 4.3 percent from 2024. Cap target: 62 percent below 2005 emissions by 2030. Maritime transport added. C.B.A.M. phasing in (Part 3). Free allocation phasing out for C.B.A.M.-covered sectors 2026-2034. Conditional free allocation introduced for 2026-2030 (free allocation contingent on energy efficiency implementation).

The Phase 4 changes accelerated the system's stringency. Combined with the energy crisis after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, EUA prices rose dramatically.

EUA prices.

The European Union Allowance is the unit traded in the E.U. E.T.S., one ton of CO2-equivalent. EUA prices have moved through several distinct phases:

2005-2007 (Phase 1): Started at 20 euros-30; collapsed to near zero by end of Phase 1 as over-allocation became clear.
2008-2012 (Phase 2): 10 euros-30 range; declined during financial crisis aftermath.
2013-2017: Persistently low, 5-10 euros per tonne of CO2-equivalent. Many analysts wrote off the system as failed.
2018-2020: Rapid increase to 20 euros-30 as MSR began operating and Phase 4 stringency was clarified.
2021-2023: Crossed 100 euros per tonne of CO2-equivalent in February 2023, a milestone level long sought by climate economists.
2024-2026: Volatile but elevated. Range 60-80 euros per tonne of CO2-equivalent through 2025 and early 2026, with the April 2026 price around 72-75 euros per tonne of CO2-equivalent. Markets are pricing supply-shock expectations from the 2026 E.T.S. reform and C.B.A.M. phase-in.

The price-setting drivers in 2025-2026:
Cap tightening, annual 4.3 percent reductions plus the MSR absorbing surpluses produce structural tightening.
Fuel-switching dynamics, when natural gas is expensive relative to coal, coal-fired generation rises and EUA demand spikes. The 2022 gas crisis pushed EUAs above 100 euros. Calmer gas markets in 2024-2025 brought prices back to the 60 euros-80 range.
C.B.A.M. phase-in, full enforcement begins Q1 2026 with C.B.A.M. certificates priced at 75.36 euros per tonne of CO2-equivalent (first published official price). The certificate price tracks EUA prices and increases the cost to non-E.U. producers selling into the E.U..
Regulatory uncertainty, the 2026 E.T.S. reform (planned) will likely tighten the system; the ETS2 launch (delayed to 2027) has added to short-term volatility.

The consensus forecast (BloombergNEF, ABN Amro, ICIS, S and P, Aurora Energy Research, PIK) is for EUA prices to reach 85 euros per tonne of CO2-equivalent on average in 2026 and cross 100 euros per tonne of CO2-equivalent in 2027, with a 2030 central estimate around 126 euros per tonne of CO2-equivalent (range 80 euros-147 across analysts). The wide range reflects regulatory uncertainty.

Revenue and what it funds.

The E.U. E.T.S. auction revenues totaled approximately 245 euros billion from 2005 through mid-2025. 2023 alone produced a record ~44 euros billion in auction revenue.

Revenue distribution:
Member states receive the majority, with substantial discretion over use. Many member states channel revenue to climate-related investments.
Modernisation Fund, supports lower-income member states' transition (~10 percent of allowances).
Innovation Fund, finances innovative low-carbon technologies (NER300 successor; ~450 million allowances over Phase 4).
RePowerEU, additional allowance auctions (additional ~250 million EUAs) channeled to RePowerEU funding under the Recovery and Resilience Facility.

The revenue stream has become a substantial budget item in many E.U. member states and a significant climate finance source, comparable in scale to the total annual IRA tax-credit value in the U.S..

ETS2, the new system for buildings and road transport.

In 2023, the E.U. adopted ETS2, a separate emissions trading system covering road transport and building heating fuels. ETS2 operates upstream, fuel suppliers (the entities placing fuel into circulation) must surrender allowances for the CO2 their fuels would emit when burned.

ETS2 was originally scheduled to launch in 2027. In November 2025, the European Parliament voted to delay full operation, with the system now expected to be fully functional in 2028. The delay reflects affordability concerns, ETS2's projected impact on heating and motor fuel costs (estimated 80 euros-150/household/year in the early phase) was politically sensitive given the cost-of-living context.

ETS2 has several distinctive features:
Full auctioning, no free allocation.
Social Climate Fund, 65 euros billion earmarked for 2026-2032 to mitigate price impact on vulnerable households, transport users, and micro-enterprises. Most ETS2 revenue is channeled here.
Price stability provisions, additional allowance releases triggered if prices exceed 45 euros per tonne of CO2-equivalent (in 2020 prices, escalated for inflation).
Coverage, fuel suppliers serving road transport, buildings, and small industrial installations not covered by ETS1.

ETS2 is the E.U.'s attempt to apply cap-and-trade to the harder-to-decarbonize "fragmented" sectors where individual emitters are too small for direct regulation. Its launch will be a major political test of whether cap-and-trade can extend beyond large industrial emitters.

The 2026 reform.

A comprehensive revision of the E.U. E.T.S. Directive is planned for 2026, addressing several open issues:

Post-2030 trajectory, what happens after Phase 4 ends? The E.U.'s 2040 climate target (90 percent net reduction below 1990 levels, with up to 5 percent flexibility from international credits) is now law, Regulation 2026/667, in force 7 April 2026 (political deal Dec 2025; EP vote Feb 2026; Council final approval Mar 2026), and requires Phase 5 design.
MSR redesign, possible threshold adjustments, intake/release rate changes, possible suppression of the invalidation mechanism (which absorbs allowances permanently when surpluses persist).
Integration of carbon removals, whether to allow C.D.R. credits as compliance units within the E.T.S.. The European Commission is expected to evaluate this in 2026.
C.B.A.M. expansion, possible extension of C.B.A.M. to additional sectors beyond the current scope.
Possible linkage with U.K. E.T.S., discussed in May 2025; negotiating mandate granted to the Commission.

The 2026 reform is the most consequential pending decision in the international carbon-pricing landscape. The direction (more stringent versus accommodating) will substantially affect prices through 2030.

Stop-and-check 7.B.

The E.U. E.T.S. Phase 1 was widely considered a failure (over-allocation, price collapse). Why is Phase 1 nevertheless described as essential? What did it accomplish?
The Market Stability Reserve was introduced in 2019, fourteen years after the E.T.S. launched. Why did it take so long, and what does this say about the institutional learning curve?
EUA prices reached 100 euros+ per tonne of CO2-equivalent in 2023 but settled to 60 euros-80 in 2025. Three different observers might interpret this as (a) successful cost-effective decarbonization (price signal worked), (b) overreaction followed by correction (oscillation around fundamentals), or (c) policy weakness (prices should be higher). Which interpretation does your reading favor?
Socratic prompt: If you were a U.S. senator proposing a national cap-and-trade system in 2027, what features of the E.U. E.T.S. would you adopt, modify, or reject? What's the case for and against each choice?

Answers

Phase 1 was a failure as a price signal, it over-allocated and prices collapsed toward zero, but it was essential as institution-building. It established the registries, the monitoring-reporting-verification requirements, the compliance procedures, and the administrative machinery that every later phase ran on. Building that infrastructure under a low-stakes, low-price regime meant the system could absorb its mistakes before the cap began to bind. The accomplishment was not abatement but a working, tested institution capable of being progressively tightened, which is exactly what Phases 2-4 did.

The MSR arrived late because the problem it solves, chronic oversupply, only became undeniable after a decade of depressed prices, and because adding regulatory discretion to a market system was politically and conceptually contentious. Fixing it required diagnosing that the surplus was structural rather than cyclical, then negotiating a Europe-wide mechanism. The lesson is that cap-and-trade institutions learn slowly and reactively: design flaws are corrected only after they manifest as sustained dysfunction, and the correction is itself a multi-year political process. The fourteen-year lag is a caution about how long it takes to get a major carbon market's architecture right.

The text supports the "oscillation around fundamentals" reading most directly: the 100 euros+ spike in 2023 was driven substantially by the gas crisis pushing coal generation and EUA demand up, and calmer gas markets in 2024-2025 brought prices back to 60 euros-80. That suggests the swing reflected a genuine fundamental (fuel-switching economics) rather than either pure policy success or pure weakness. But the interpretations are not mutually exclusive: the structural tightening from cap declines and MSR intake is real (supporting (a)), while 60 euros-80 remains below the central I.P.C.C.-aligned estimates and below where free allocation lets full industrial signals through (supporting (c)). A careful reading favors (b) as the proximate explanation, with (c) as the longer-run concern.

Several E.U. features are worth adopting: a declining cap with a clear long-run trajectory, banking to reward early action, transparent MRV and registries, and a stability mechanism to avoid the volatility that nearly killed the E.U. system. The harder choices are political. Free allocation is the case for and against in one: adopting it eases industry opposition and addresses leakage, but it dilutes the signal and risks windfalls, a U.S. system might pair limited free allocation with a C.B.A.M.-style border adjustment instead. A U.S. senator would likely reject the E.U.'s slow, reactive governance (the fourteen-year MSR lag) in favor of pre-committed automatic adjustments, and would have to decide whether to preempt or coexist with existing state programs (California, Reggie, Washington). The decisive variables are the strength of the industrial lobby, exposure to import competition, and whether the price floor/ceiling can be set credibly enough to survive the next administration.

Part 3: C.B.A.M., the border adjustment.

The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism is the E.U.'s attempt to extend its carbon price beyond its borders, by charging importers of carbon-intensive goods the equivalent of the E.U.'s domestic carbon price. C.B.A.M. is one of the most consequential trade-and-climate policy innovations of the past decade.

The problem C.B.A.M. addresses.

A domestic carbon price creates an asymmetry: E.U. producers face a cost that foreign producers don't, undercutting E.U. competitiveness. The traditional response has been free allocation to "carbon leakage-exposed" sectors, which preserves competitiveness but undermines the price signal for those sectors. C.B.A.M. takes a different approach: charge foreign producers the same carbon price on imports.

The result is meant to be symmetry: E.U. and non-E.U. producers face the same carbon cost on goods sold into the E.U. market. This protects domestic competitiveness without preserving free allocation.

How C.B.A.M. works.

C.B.A.M. covers (initially): electricity, iron and steel, aluminum, cement, fertilizers, and hydrogen products, approximately 54 percent of the value of free allowances historically allocated under ETS1.

Importers of C.B.A.M.-covered goods must:
Report the embedded greenhouse gas emissions of the imported goods (transitional reporting phase started October 2023).
Purchase C.B.A.M. certificates equal to the embedded emissions, at a price tracking E.U. E.T.S. allowance prices (full enforcement starts January 2026).
Deduct any carbon price already paid in the country of origin (avoiding double-charging if exporters' home countries have their own carbon pricing).

C.B.A.M. certificate prices track EUA prices. The first official price (announced April 2026): 75.36 euros per tonne of CO2-equivalent for Q1 2026, based on the Q4 2025 EUA auction average. For perspective, at full coverage this would add approximately 140 euros/ton to imported steel (with ~1.85 tonnes of CO2-equivalent embedded per ton), but in 2026, with only 2.5 percent of embedded emissions covered (see the phase-in below), the actual obligation is roughly 3.50 euros/ton, rising as coverage ramps toward 100 percent by 2034.

C.B.A.M. is being phased in to coincide with the phase-out of free allowances for C.B.A.M.-covered sectors. The phase-in schedule:
2023-2025: Reporting only; no certificate purchase required.
2026: Certificate purchase begins; importers cover 2.5 percent of embedded emissions.
2027 onward: Coverage ratio increases annually, reaching 100 percent by 2034.
2026-2034: Free allowances for C.B.A.M.-covered E.U. producers phase out in parallel.

Political response.

C.B.A.M. has generated intense political pushback from non-E.U. producers:

China, India, Brazil, South Africa have explicitly criticized C.B.A.M. as protectionist, arguing it violates WTO non-discrimination principles and the U.N.F.C.C.C.'s CBDR-RC principle by imposing equivalent obligations on developing countries.
The U.S. under the Biden administration was cautiously supportive (C.B.A.M.-like mechanisms align with U.S. industrial policy goals). The Trump administration has been less clear.
Turkey, Ukraine, Western Balkans, major exporters of C.B.A.M.-covered goods to the E.U., have moved to develop their own carbon pricing systems partly to capture the revenue domestically rather than paying it to the E.U..

The WTO compatibility question is contested. C.B.A.M. proponents argue it's compatible because it's tied to a domestic environmental measure and applies symmetrically (the embedded carbon, not the country of origin). Critics argue the practical effect is discriminatory and the verification mechanisms create asymmetric burdens.

The empirical effect of C.B.A.M. is just beginning to manifest. Initial Q1 2026 enforcement at 75 euros per tonne of CO2-equivalent represents a substantial competitive shift. Whether C.B.A.M. achieves its stated goal, extending the E.U.'s carbon price beyond its borders, incentivizing trading partners to adopt their own pricing, depends on the trading partners' responses and the political durability of C.B.A.M. itself.

C.B.A.M. as institutional precedent.

Even if C.B.A.M. doesn't fully achieve its stated goals, it has established a precedent: a major trading bloc can use border adjustments to extend its domestic climate policy to imports. Several other jurisdictions are considering similar mechanisms, the U.K. has proposed U.K. C.B.A.M. (consultation 2024-2025), Australia and Canada have examined the concept. The "carbon club" concept (linked carbon-pricing regimes with mutual recognition and shared border adjustments against non-participants) has gained traction in academic and policy literature.

The next decade will likely see C.B.A.M.-style mechanisms become more common, fragmenting the global carbon-pricing landscape into linked-but-distinct regional regimes rather than a single global price.

Stop-and-check 7.C.

C.B.A.M. is meant to address carbon leakage without relying on free allocation. Explain the competitive asymmetry it's correcting and how charging importers restores symmetry.
C.B.A.M. lets importers deduct any carbon price already paid in the country of origin. Why is that single feature what turns C.B.A.M. from a pure trade barrier into an incentive for other countries to adopt their own carbon price?
In 2026 C.B.A.M. covers only 2.5 percent of embedded emissions (~3.50 euros/ton steel), ramping to 100 percent by 2034 in lockstep with the phase-out of free allowances for E.U. producers. Why is that parallel phase-out essential to C.B.A.M.'s integrity, what would go wrong if the two schedules didn't match?
Socratic prompt: C.B.A.M. forces a hard measurement problem: the embedded carbon in a ton of imported steel depends on the exporter's production process, which the E.U. can't directly observe. Connect this to the methane-intensity verification problem (Chapter 4) and the avoided/reduced/removed discipline (Chapter 5). Who bears the burden of proof, and what happens to producers who can't or won't document their carbon intensity?

Answers

A domestic carbon price makes E.U. producers pay for emissions that foreign producers selling into the same market don't, so absent a fix, the carbon price hands an advantage to imports and risks "leakage," where production (and its emissions) simply relocates to uncovered jurisdictions, yielding no climate benefit and lost E.U. competitiveness. The traditional fix, free allocation, preserves competitiveness but guts the price signal for the protected sectors. C.B.A.M. restores symmetry from the other side: it charges importers the same carbon price on the embedded emissions of C.B.A.M.-covered goods, so E.U. and non-E.U. producers face an equivalent carbon cost on goods sold into the E.U., letting the E.U. withdraw free allocation without re-opening the competitiveness gap.

Because the deduction means a ton of carbon is only ever charged once, and the exporting country gets to choose who collects the money. If a foreign producer's government already prices its carbon, that payment is deducted from the C.B.A.M. liability, so the revenue stays at home rather than flowing to Brussels. That converts C.B.A.M. from a wall into an incentive: a trading partner can capture the carbon revenue domestically (funding its own transition) simply by pricing carbon itself, which is exactly why Turkey, Ukraine, and the Western Balkans began building their own carbon-pricing systems in response. The deduction is what makes C.B.A.M. a price-spreading mechanism rather than just protectionism.

Because free allocation and C.B.A.M. are two solutions to the same leakage problem, and running both at full strength would double-protect E.U. producers, they'd get free allowances and a tariff wall, an obvious WTO-vulnerable subsidy. So as C.B.A.M. coverage ramps up, free allocation must ramp down by the same amount, keeping total protection roughly constant and the E.U. producer always facing a real carbon cost on its own emissions. If the schedules didn't match, if free allocation lingered while C.B.A.M. bit, E.U. producers would be over-compensated, the policy would look nakedly protectionist, and the WTO challenge (already threatened by China, India, Brazil) would be far stronger. The lockstep is what keeps C.B.A.M. defensible as a leakage measure rather than a trade weapon.

The burden of proof falls on the importer/producer, and that is the crux. Like methane intensity (Chapter 4), embedded carbon is a property of the production history, not of the delivered product, you can't assay a steel coil and read off how much CO2 its blast furnace emitted, so it must be traced, documented, and verified across a supply chain the E.U. doesn't control. C.B.A.M. handles this exactly as Chapter 5's discipline would predict: producers who document a genuinely lower carbon intensity pay less; producers who can't or won't document fall back to default values pegged to carbon-intensive production, so opacity is penalized. The deep parallel is that all three, C.B.A.M., methane-intensity standards, and emissions accounting generally, are regulating an attribute embedded in a process rather than in a product, which means the entire regime rests on measurement, verification, and default-value design, and those become the real battleground (and the real trade friction) rather than the headline price.

Part 4: North American compliance markets.

The U.S. lacks a federal cap-and-trade system. Several U.S. states and Canadian provinces have implemented sub-national systems, with significant variation in design, coverage, and price.

California Cap-and-Invest (formerly Cap-and-Trade).

California's cap-and-trade program launched January 2012 (allowance distribution) with compliance obligations starting January 2013. In September 2025, Governor Newsom signed AB 1207 and SB 840, extending the program through 2045 and renaming it "Cap-and-Invest." The renaming reflects the program's emphasis on directing auction revenue to climate investments.

Coverage: ~76 percent of California greenhouse gas emissions, including power generation, industrial facilities, fuel distributors (motor fuels and natural gas), and small-scale operations through fuel-distributor coverage. About 400 covered facilities; broader if upstream fuel coverage is included. The most comprehensive sectoral coverage of any cap-and-trade system in the world by emissions share, broader than the E.U. E.T.S. (which covers ~40 percent of E.U. emissions).

Targets: 40 percent reduction below 1990 levels by 2030; 85 percent by 2045; net-zero by 2045 (full economy, supported by Cap-and-Invest plus complementary policies).

Linkage: California is linked with Québec's cap-and-trade system since January 2014, forming the Western Climate Initiative market. Washington State launched its own Cap-and-Invest in 2023, currently operating separately but with potential future linkage.

Prices: California Carbon Allowance prices in 2025 were depressed for most of the year (around 25 dollars-30 nominal) due to oversupply and regulatory uncertainty. After the extension legislation passed in September 2025, CCA prices rallied 12 percent, returning above 30 dollars on the secondary market. The current price floor (~25 dollars nominal, escalating annually) provides a soft floor; the price ceiling (~103 dollars in 2025) provides a soft ceiling.

Distinctive features:
35 percent of revenue mandated for environmentally disadvantaged and low-income communities. California's strongest equity provision.
Offset usage limits: 4 percent of compliance obligations 2021-2025; 6 percent from 2026 onward (under the extension). At least half of offset usage must come from projects that provide "direct environmental benefits to the state".
AB 1207 offset reform: Offset credits used for compliance now require retirement of an equivalent number of allowances from the following year's allowance budget, effectively placing offsets "under the cap." This is a substantial integrity strengthening.

Reggie (Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative).

Reggie launched in 2009 as the first U.S. mandatory carbon cap-and-trade system. It covers only the power sector in 11 northeastern and mid-Atlantic states: New York, Massachusetts, Connecticut, Maine, New Hampshire, Vermont, Rhode Island, Maryland, Delaware, New Jersey, and Virginia. Pennsylvania moved to join via regulation but its participation has been tied up in litigation and is not in effect; Virginia's continued participation has also been politically contested.

Cap: Stringent power-sector cap declining steadily. Reggie has been credited with driving substantial power-sector decarbonization in member states, though much of the reduction came from natural-gas displacement of coal rather than direct cap-induced action.

Prices: Reggie prices have become unusually volatile. Auction clearing settled around ~13 dollars per tonne of CO2-equivalent in early 2026 (Auction 71, March 2026), but the secondary market spiked sharply to ~40 dollars-58/short ton in late April-early May 2026 on the news of Virginia's re-entry, before retreating after the Reggie states issued a market-monitoring notice. The book's earlier ~15 dollars-22 range (2024-25) is now too narrow; treat Reggie as a thin, headline-sensitive market where a single state's participation decision can move prices dramatically.

Limitations: Reggie's narrow sectoral scope (power only) and modest cap make it a smaller-impact system than California's. There has been recurring discussion of expanding Reggie to transportation fuels (Transportation and Climate Initiative, mostly stalled) or to other sectors.

Washington Cap-and-Invest.

Washington State launched Cap-and-Invest in January 2023, the most aggressive sub-national U.S. carbon-pricing program by initial design. Coverage: ~75 percent of state emissions, including power, industry, and (uniquely in the U.S.) transportation fuels at the supplier level.

Distinctive features:
Fuel coverage at the supplier level produces price pass-through to gasoline and diesel, a significant cost-of-living impact.
Substantial auction revenue earmarked for climate investments, transportation electrification, and air quality programs.
Tribal consultation and benefits requirements built into the statute.

The 2024 repeal initiative: Initiative 2117 in November 2024 sought to repeal Cap-and-Invest. Voters decisively rejected the repeal (around 62 percent no), preserving the program. This was a significant political victory, the program survived its first electoral test despite voter sensitivity to gasoline prices.

Prices: Washington allowance prices have been the highest in North America, often 50 dollars+ per tonne of CO2-equivalent, substantially above California, due to very tight supply-demand fundamentals.

Linkage: Washington is pursuing potential linkage with the WCI (California-Québec) market, though this has been delayed by the 2024 program review.

The federal absence.

Despite multiple attempts (Lieberman-Warner 2008, Waxman-Markey 2009, passed the House but died in the Senate), the United States has never adopted a federal cap-and-trade system. The political durability of state-level programs in the face of federal absence is itself a notable phenomenon. The 2025 federal climate retreat under the Trump administration has not so far collapsed state-level programs, California's extension passed despite federal headwinds, Washington's repeal failed, Reggie prices remained robust.

The state programs together cover roughly 15 percent of U.S. emissions. A federal system, if ever enacted, would face the design question of whether to preempt or coexist with state programs.

Stop-and-check 7.D.

California's Cap-and-Invest covers 76 percent of state emissions; Reggie covers only ~25 percent of New England-region emissions (power only). What's the trade-off in scope, and what does it imply about each system's effective stringency?
Washington's voters rejected the 2024 repeal initiative 62-38. What does this tell you about the political durability of state-level carbon pricing in the U.S.?
AB 1207's offset reform (offsets now "under the cap", each offset compliance use retires an equivalent allowance) is a substantial change. Why is this considered an integrity strengthening, and what does it cost?
Socratic prompt: If you were a state legislator in a non-cap-and-trade state in 2026, what features from California, Reggie, and Washington would you adopt or reject for your own state's program?

Answers

The trade-off is breadth versus depth of political exposure. California covers ~76 percent of state emissions, power, industry, and fuel distributors, making the program economically comprehensive but politically exposed to fuel-price pass-through. Reggie covers only the power sector (~25 percent of regional emissions), which is administratively simple and politically insulated (few visible consumer costs) but inherently limited in reach. Broader coverage means a given price touches more of the economy, so California's effective stringency per dollar of price is higher across sectors; Reggie's narrow scope means even a robust power-sector price leaves most regional emissions untouched. Scope and political durability trade against each other.

It signals that state-level carbon pricing can survive direct electoral tests even when costs are visible at the pump. Washington's coverage of transportation fuels at the supplier level produces real gasoline-price pass-through, which is exactly the kind of cost-of-living impact that sinks carbon policies elsewhere, yet voters rejected repeal 62-38. That suggests a durable constituency, plausibly built by the program's earmarking of revenue for popular climate and transportation investments. It does not prove invulnerability, but it shows that a well-designed program with visible benefits can withstand a referendum even under fuel-price sensitivity, which matters for the durability of state programs amid federal retreat.

AB 1207 makes each offset used for compliance retire an equivalent allowance from the following year's budget, placing offsets "under the cap." This is an integrity strengthening because, without it, an offset adds a compliance ton on top of the capped allowances, effectively loosening the cap by the offset volume, and inheriting any additionality or baseline weakness in the offset. Tying each offset use to an allowance retirement ensures the cap's total quantity is preserved regardless of offset quality. The cost is reduced flexibility and higher compliance cost: offsets no longer expand the available supply, so their value to covered entities falls and the cheap-compliance safety valve narrows.

A new-entrant state would likely adopt California's broad sectoral coverage and strong revenue-equity provision (35 percent to disadvantaged communities) for both impact and political durability, and adopt the AB 1207 "offsets under the cap" discipline to protect integrity. From Reggie it would take the lesson that a power-only program is simple and durable but limited, a reasonable starting point if political capital is scarce. From Washington it would weigh the risk of fuel-price pass-through against the demonstrated survivability when revenue funds visible benefits. The decisive variables are the state's industrial and import profile, its tolerance for visible consumer costs, and whether it can link to an existing market to deepen liquidity rather than launch alone.

Part 5: Other major compliance markets.

China national E.T.S..

China's national emissions trading system launched in July 2021 and is now the largest cap-and-trade system in the world by emissions volume covered (though not yet by price-weighted economic value).

Coverage: Initially the power sector only. Expanded in 2024 to include aluminum, cement, and steel sectors. Plans to expand further to additional industrial sectors.

Cap structure: Initially intensity-based, covered entities receive allowances based on emissions per unit of electricity output, not on absolute emissions. This means the cap doesn't bind on total emissions and the system functions more like an efficiency-based incentive than a pure cap.

Prices: Chinese Emission Allowance prices have been ~80 yuan-100 per tonne of CO2-equivalent (11-14 dollars per tonne of CO2-equivalent), low compared to E.U. or California, reflecting the intensity-based structure and modest stringency.

Transition to absolute cap: China has signaled intent to transition from intensity-based to absolute caps over time, but no specific timeline. The 2024-2025 sectoral expansion plus the planned transition would substantially increase the system's effective stringency.

Strategic significance: China's national E.T.S. covers ~40 percent of China's CO2 emissions and ~10 percent of global emissions. Even at low prices, the system is institutionally significant, it establishes the infrastructure that future tightening could leverage.

U.K. E.T.S..

The U.K. launched its own E.T.S. in January 2021 after Brexit, broadly modeled on the E.U. E.T.S. but operating separately. U.K. E.T.S. covers power, industry, and intra-U.K. aviation; approximately 30 percent of U.K. emissions.

Prices: U.K. E.T.S. allowances trade at a discount to EUAs (U.K. E.T.S. ~35-50 pounds per tonne of CO2-equivalent versus EUA ~70 euros-80) due to a smaller market and looser supply.

Linkage with E.U.: In May 2025, the E.U. and U.K. announced intent to link their respective ETSs. The Council granted the Commission a negotiating mandate. Formal linking negotiations are underway with potential implementation in 2026-2027. Linkage would effectively merge the markets, eliminating the price gap and creating a single E.U.-U.K. carbon-pricing zone.

Korea E.T.S..

Korea's E.T.S. launched in 2015 and is the third-oldest major operational E.T.S.. Coverage: ~73 percent of national emissions; ~600 covered entities across power, industry, and other sectors.

Prices: KAU prices have been volatile, generally in the ₩5,000-25,000 per tonne of CO2-equivalent range (4-20 dollars per tonne of CO2-equivalent), reflecting Korean cap structure and limited offset use.

Phase 3 (2021-2025): Tightened the cap and reduced free allocation. Phase 4 (2026-2030) is in design and is expected to further reduce free allocation and increase auctioning.

Other operational systems.

New Zealand E.T.S.: Operational since 2008 (the second-oldest major E.T.S.). Covers most sectors including forestry. Prices ~NZ50-70 dollars per tonne of CO2-equivalent (30 dollars-43). Has been politically contested under successive governments.
Quebec: Linked with California since 2014.
Switzerland: Linked with E.U. E.T.S. since 2020.
Mexico: Pilot phase ended; full operational phase in early stages.
Indonesia, Vietnam, Thailand: Various stages of system development.

Systems coming online.

Turkey: E.T.S. being developed (target launch 2025-2026), partly motivated by C.B.A.M. exposure.
Brazil: E.T.S. legislation passed in 2024; system in development. Expected operational phase mid-2020s.
India CCTS (Carbon Credit Trading Scheme): Hybrid system combining intensity-based emissions trading with renewable energy certificates. Operational phase starting.
Various ASEAN systems: Multiple smaller-scale developments.

The global trajectory: more jurisdictions implementing carbon pricing, with substantial variation in design and stringency. The International Carbon Action Partnership tracks roughly 35 operational ETSs globally as of 2026, covering ~24 percent of global greenhouse gas emissions. Coverage continues to grow, but the price-weighted coverage (emissions covered at a price high enough to materially influence behavior) is much lower, perhaps 5-8 percent of global emissions.

Stop-and-check 7.E.

China's E.T.S. is the largest in the world by emissions volume, yet its prices (~11-14 dollars per tonne of CO2-equivalent) are a fraction of the E.U.'s. The main reason is that its cap is intensity-based. Explain how an intensity-based cap differs from an absolute cap and why it produces low prices.
China's system covers ~10 percent of global emissions at a low, effectively non-binding price. Is it therefore climate-irrelevant, or is there a serious case that the institutional infrastructure matters more right now than the price?
The E.U. and U.K. are negotiating to link their ETSs. What does linkage do to the ~30 euros+ price gap between EUAs and U.K. allowances, and why might the smaller U.K. market want it, or fear it?
Socratic prompt: A jurisdiction launching an E.T.S. in 2026 must choose intensity-based caps (like China) or absolute caps (like the E.U.). Intensity-based is politically easier, it doesn't penalize growth, but doesn't guarantee an emissions outcome. Which would you advise, and what does the right answer depend on?

Answers

An absolute cap fixes total tons: the number of allowances is set and declines on a schedule regardless of output, so the price reflects the gap between that fixed quantity and what emitters would otherwise emit. An intensity-based cap fixes emissions per unit of output (for example, per MWh): allowances are issued in proportion to how much each entity produces, so total allowances expand if output grows. That means the system rarely creates genuine scarcity, if you produce more, you get more allowances, so it functions as an efficiency benchmark (rewarding cleaner-than-average producers) rather than a hard quantity limit. With no binding scarcity, demand for allowances is weak and the price stays low, which is exactly China's ~11 dollars-14 outcome.

It is not climate-irrelevant, and the stronger case is that the infrastructure matters more than today's price. A low intensity-based price drives little abatement now, true. But the system establishes the MRV, registry, compliance, and verification machinery across thousands of major emitters, the same institution-building that E.U. E.T.S. Phase 1 did at near-zero prices, and China has signaled intent to transition toward absolute caps and has already expanded coverage to aluminum, cement, and steel. The infrastructure is the option value: once it exists and covers ~40 percent of Chinese ( approximately 10 percent of global) CO2, tightening the cap is a policy dial rather than a build-from-scratch project. The price is weak; the latent leverage is large.

Linkage would let allowances from one system satisfy compliance in the other, which arbitrages the two prices toward a single level, closing the ~30 euros+ gap by pulling the cheaper U.K. price up toward the EUA price (and nudging the E.U. price slightly down). The U.K. might want it for liquidity, depth, and reduced price volatility in a small, thin market, plus reduced C.B.A.M. friction with its largest trading partner. It might fear it because convergence raises U.K. carbon costs (politically sensitive) and cedes some control over its own carbon price to E.U. policy decisions it doesn't vote on, the classic sovereignty-vs-efficiency trade of joining a larger market.

There's no universal answer; it hinges on the jurisdiction's growth profile, administrative capacity, and political constraints. Intensity-based caps are the pragmatic choice for a fast-growing economy that won't accept a hard limit on output (China, India), they get the institution built and start rewarding efficiency without a politically toxic absolute ceiling, accepting that emissions may still rise. Absolute caps are the right choice where the priority is a guaranteed emissions trajectory and the economy can tolerate the price (the E.U.). The decisive variables are whether the jurisdiction is willing to let total emissions grow in the near term in exchange for political feasibility, whether it has the data infrastructure to run either system, and whether it credibly intends to transition from intensity to absolute later, because an intensity system with no transition plan can become a permanent way to look like you price carbon while never creating real scarcity.

Part 6: The case for and against cap-and-trade.

The political economics of cap-and-trade have been intensely contested for two decades. The substantive critiques fall into several categories.

Critique 1: "It commodifies pollution".

The deepest philosophical objection: cap-and-trade creates property rights in the right to pollute. Once those rights are created, they have economic value, and the political constituency for preserving them grows.

The defense: cap-and-trade doesn't create the right to pollute; it limits it. Without the system, emissions are unbounded. The system creates a declining property right that progressively eliminates pollution. The political durability of the property right is the source of policy stability, not a flaw.

This is largely a values-based debate where reasonable people disagree.

Critique 2: "Free allocation undermines the system".

Empirically, free allocation has been the largest weakness in most cap-and-trade systems. E.U. Phase 1 over-allocated; Phase 2 still gave away most allowances. Even in Phase 4, C.B.A.M.-covered sectors continue to receive substantial free allocation through 2034.

The defense: Free allocation was a political necessity for system durability. Without it, industry opposition would have blocked the systems from launching. C.B.A.M. and the conditional-free-allocation framework address leakage without ongoing free allocation.

The empirical record: free allocation in E.U. Phase 1 produced significant windfalls (estimated 5 euros-8 billion in 2005 alone) without meaningfully advancing decarbonization. Free allocation in Phases 2-3 was more constrained but still substantial. The trajectory has been toward less free allocation, and the system has remained durable through that transition. The political-necessity argument has weakened over time as the system has institutionalized.

Critique 3: "Carbon prices are still too low to matter".

The I.P.C.C. AR6 estimates that consistent with 1.5 degrees Celsius, carbon prices need to reach 135-5,500 dollars per tonne of CO2-equivalent by 2030 (2010 USD; the range reflecting different modeling assumptions). Current E.U. E.T.S. prices around 75 euros per tonne of CO2-equivalent are within the low end of this range, but well below the central estimates.

The defense: prices have risen substantially (from ~10 euros per tonne of CO2-equivalent in 2017 to ~75 euros per tonne of CO2-equivalent in 2026, a roughly 7 times increase). The trajectory is clearly upward. Complementary policies (regulations, subsidies, R and D) supplement the price signal. The Paris-aligned price levels in I.P.C.C. scenarios are conditional on policy frameworks that don't exist yet.

The empirical question: whether 75-125 euros per tonne of CO2-equivalent is sufficient to drive substantial decarbonization, even within only the covered sectors. Recent E.U. power-sector emissions have fallen substantially; covered industrial emissions less so. The latter is partly because free allocation has insulated industry from the full price signal.

Critique 4: "The systems exclude the hardest sectors".

Most ETSs cover power generation and large industry but exclude transportation (where emissions are growing) and buildings (where stocks are slow-turnover). Agriculture, the second-largest source globally, is almost entirely uncovered.

The defense: cap-and-trade works best where emitters are large and concentrated. Distributed sectors are better addressed through standards, fuel taxes, or building codes. ETS2 (Part 2) is the E.U.'s attempt to extend cap-and-trade to fragmented sectors.

This critique is largely correct as a description of current systems, but partly addresses incomplete implementation rather than inherent design limitation. The next-generation systems (ETS2, India CCTS, various proposed designs) are exploring whether cap-and-trade can work for fragmented sectors.

Critique 5: "Carbon markets are subject to capture".

Cap-and-trade systems require ongoing rulemaking, caps to set, allocation rules to determine, offset methodologies to approve, exemptions to grant. Each rulemaking is a target for industry lobbying. The E.U. E.T.S. has been criticized for excessive industry influence over allocation methodologies, free allocation thresholds, and C.B.A.M. implementation.

The defense: all climate policies are subject to capture. Cap-and-trade is no worse than carbon taxes or standards. Transparency requirements and independent oversight bodies (in some systems) provide accountability.

The empirical record: cap-and-trade has clearly been subject to industry influence, especially in early phases. Whether this is more or less captured than carbon taxes or regulations is debatable.

Stop-and-check 7.F.

Of the five critiques, commodifies pollution, free allocation undermines, prices too low, excludes hard sectors, subject to capture, which are fixable implementation problems and which are closer to inherent features of the cap-and-trade instrument?
Free allocation is called "the dominant integrity issue," yet also "a political necessity for system durability." Can both be true? Reconcile them.
I.P.C.C. scenarios imply 1.5 degrees Celsius-consistent prices of 135-5,500 dollars per tonne of CO2-equivalent by 2030, but the E.U. sits around 75 euros. Does that gap mean cap-and-trade has failed, or is the headline price the wrong thing to look at?
Socratic prompt: A critic says cap-and-trade "commodifies the right to pollute and breeds a lobby that protects it." A defender says "the declining property right is precisely what makes the policy durable enough to keep tightening." Both describe the same mechanism. Which framing better predicts the E.U. E.T.S.'s actual 20-year trajectory?

Answers

Most are fixable implementation problems; one or two are closer to inherent. Free allocation, low prices, and excluded sectors are all design choices that systems have demonstrably tightened over time, the E.U. moved from ~95 percent free allocation toward majority auctioning, prices rose ~7 times since 2017, and ETS2 extends coverage to transport and buildings. Capture is partly fixable (transparency, independent oversight) but partly inherent, because any instrument requiring ongoing rulemaking offers ongoing lobbying targets. "Commodifies pollution" is the only genuinely inherent feature, creating a tradeable right is what the instrument is, so it's less a fixable bug than a values-based objection to the whole approach. The honest split: four critiques describe how systems have been implemented (and can be improved); one describes what cap-and-trade fundamentally is.

Both are true because they describe the same fact from different time horizons. At launch, free allocation was necessary: industry would have blocked a system that forced it to buy permits outright, so handing out allowances was the price of getting the institution built at all. But once built, that same free allocation became the integrity weakness, it muffled the price signal for protected sectors and generated windfalls (E.U. Phase 1, ~5 euros-8 billion in 2005). The reconciliation is sequencing: free allocation was scaffolding that let the building go up, and the long project has been removing the scaffolding (auctioning, C.B.A.M., conditional allocation) without the building collapsing. The political-necessity argument was strong at t=0 and weakens as the institution entrenches, which is exactly the trajectory observed.

The gap doesn't show failure so much as it shows the price is only part of the story. The I.P.C.C.'s high figures are conditional on price doing all the work in a first-best world; real decarbonization runs on a portfolio, price plus standards, subsidies, R and D, and infrastructure, so the marginal abatement the price needs to induce is lower than a price-only model implies. Within covered sectors the E.U. price has clearly moved behavior (power-sector emissions fell sharply), while industrial emissions lagged largely because free allocation blunted the signal there. So the headline number can mislead: the right questions are whether the price binds on the uncovered margin, whether complementary policies are filling the gaps, and whether the price trajectory is credibly rising, not whether 75 euros equals an I.P.C.C. modeling output.

The E.U. E.T.S.'s actual trajectory fits the defender's framing better, though the critic's mechanism is real. Over 20 years the system did create a valuable property right and a constituency around it, but that constituency (member-state treasuries collecting 245 euros billion, industries holding bankable allowances, financial participants) turned out to favor a functioning, tightening market over a collapsed one, and the cap kept declining (LRF rising to 4.3 percent, free allocation phasing out, C.B.A.M. added). The "lobby protects pollution" prediction would have forecast capture freezing the system; instead the property-right constituency coincided with two decades of progressive tightening. The critic correctly identifies that a lobby forms; the defender more accurately predicts what that lobby, facing a declining asset it has already capitalized, actually fights for, stability and predictability, which in a declining-cap system means continued decarbonization.

Part 7: Cap-and-trade versus carbon taxes.

The principal alternative to cap-and-trade is carbon taxation, directly imposing a per-ton charge on covered emissions without trading. The two instruments differ in important ways.

Conceptual differences.

When each is preferred.

The standard theoretical argument: cap-and-trade is preferred when the cost of marginal emissions damage rises sharply (so emissions certainty matters most), while carbon taxes are preferred when the cost of marginal abatement rises sharply (so price certainty matters most). This is the Weitzman (1974) prices-vs-quantities framework.

In practice, the choice is largely political:
Cap-and-trade has been preferred where political coalitions need allocation flexibility, business lobbies prefer the "market mechanism" framing, and policymakers want quantitative emissions guarantees. The E.U., California, and various other jurisdictions.
Carbon taxes have been preferred where simpler administration is valued, government revenue is the primary purpose, and businesses prefer price certainty. The Nordic countries, Switzerland (also has small E.T.S.), Canada (national backstop), and others.

Carbon tax record.

Major carbon tax systems include:
Sweden: Carbon tax since 1991. Current rate ~130 dollars per tonne of CO2-equivalent, the highest in the world. Substantial decarbonization of Swedish heating sector.
British Columbia: Carbon tax since 2008, revenue-neutral (offset by tax cuts). One of the most rigorously studied carbon-tax implementations.
Canada national backstop: Since 2019. Federal price floor that applies if provinces don't have their own systems. Current floor: 80 dollars per tonne of CO2-equivalent in 2025, escalating to 170 dollars per tonne of CO2-equivalent by 2030.
Various European national taxes (France, Ireland, Norway, Denmark, et cetera), most coexist with E.U. E.T.S. at a sectoral level.

The empirical record is broadly favorable for carbon taxation as a tool. Sweden's emissions fell substantially after the carbon tax was introduced (though attribution is difficult given parallel policies). British Columbia's economy did not appear to suffer noticeably from the revenue-neutral carbon tax.

Politically, carbon taxes are more sensitive to "yellow vest"-style backlash because the cost is more visible. The 2018 yellow-vest protests in France were partly triggered by carbon tax increases. ETS2's projected price impact on heating fuels in the E.U. is causing similar concerns, which is why ETS2 includes a substantial Social Climate Fund.

The hybrid approach.

In practice, most major economies use hybrid approaches, a price-on-something-via-something policy plus complementary regulations, subsidies, and standards. Pure cap-and-trade is rare; pure carbon tax is rare. The E.U. has E.T.S. plus member-state-level taxes plus regulations plus subsidies. California has Cap-and-Invest plus low-carbon fuel standards plus utility regulation plus vehicle electrification mandates.

The substantive question of which instrument is most effective in a given sector under given political conditions is genuinely contested, and the right answer is often "both, with complementary instruments."

Stop-and-check 7.G.

The theoretical case for cap-and-trade versus carbon tax under Weitzman is about whether quantity or price certainty matters more. In the climate context, which matters more, and why?
Sweden's carbon tax has been 130 dollars per tonne of CO2-equivalent since 2018, with no clear evidence of economic harm. Why hasn't the rest of the world simply adopted Sweden's policy?
ETS2 will impose price increases on heating fuels in the E.U. starting 2027-2028 (delayed). What's the political risk, and how is the Social Climate Fund supposed to manage it?
Socratic prompt: If a future U.S. administration in 2029 had a 60-vote Senate majority and wanted to pass a single climate-pricing law, would you advise them to propose cap-and-trade or a carbon tax? What's the trade-off and what's your defense?

Answers

Under Weitzman, the choice turns on which marginal cost curve is steeper. Cap-and-trade (quantity certainty) is preferred when the damage from an extra ton rises sharply, so getting the quantity wrong is very costly; carbon taxes (price certainty) are preferred when abatement costs rise sharply, so getting the price wrong is very costly. The climate case is genuinely ambiguous and arguably favors price certainty for the near term: annual climate damage is driven by cumulative stock, not one year's flow, so a single year's emissions overshoot does little marginal harm, while a runaway abatement cost from a rigid cap could be very expensive. That argues for price certainty short-term, though tail risks and tipping points push back toward valuing quantity certainty.

Sweden's 130 dollars tax works partly because of conditions other countries don't share. Much of Sweden's exposed industry sits under the E.U. E.T.S. and faces partial or zero national tax, so the high rate falls mainly on heating and fuels where alternatives (district heating, biomass) existed; the tax was introduced early and escalated gradually; and the political culture tolerated it. Elsewhere, visible per-ton charges trigger backlash (the French yellow vests were partly a carbon-tax revolt), trade-exposed industry resists, and the absence of revenue-recycling makes the cost salient. Sweden's policy is not freely transplantable because its success depended on sequencing, complementary infrastructure, sector composition, and political consent that vary widely across countries.

The political risk is that ETS2 raises heating and motor-fuel costs for households (estimated 80 euros-150/household/year early on) in a cost-of-living context, producing exactly the visible-cost backlash that froze France's carbon tax and that already forced ETS2's delay to 2028. The Social Climate Fund (65 euros billion, 2026-2032) is meant to manage this by channeling most ETS2 revenue back to vulnerable households, transport users, and micro-enterprises, softening the net cost for those least able to absorb it while preserving the marginal price signal. Whether it works depends on whether the transfers reach affected groups visibly and promptly enough to defuse the politics before the price bites.

For a single law with 60 votes, the defensible advice is a carbon tax, preferably revenue-recycled, with cap-and-trade as the alternative if political coalition-building requires allocation flexibility. The case for a tax: price certainty aids investment planning, administration is simpler, and a visible dividend (as in Canada's design, where most households get back more than they pay) can build durable support. The case against: a tax fixes price, not emissions, so the quantity outcome is uncertain, and the cost is politically salient (the yellow-vest risk). Cap-and-trade offers an emissions guarantee and lets you distribute allowances to assemble a coalition, but invites free-allocation giveaways and price volatility. The decisive variables are whether the administration values emissions certainty or political durability more, and whether a dividend can make the cost politically survivable.

Part 8: Fuel-standard compliance regimes.

The previous seven parts have covered cap-and-trade and carbon taxes, the dominant architecture for compliance carbon pricing. A third and structurally distinct class of compliance instrument operates in parallel: fuel-standard regimes, which mandate carbon-intensity performance in transportation fuels without imposing a system-wide cap on total emissions.

These regimes collectively move more money than many higher-profile carbon-pricing instruments. California's Low Carbon Fuel Standard generates roughly 1.5 billion dollars in credit revenue annually. U.S. renewable fuel mandates support a 6 dollars-8 billion RIN market. E.U. renewable fuel directives and saff mandates drive the largest clean-fuels capital deployment in the world. Understanding their architecture is essential context for Chapter 8's voluntary market discussion.

The defining design question: what's the baseline?.

Every compliance regime can be characterized by a single design question: what is the baseline against which reductions are measured and rewarded?

The answer sorts fuel-standard regimes into two fundamentally different types:

Step-function regimes use a statutory threshold. A fuel either qualifies (passes the lifecycle-emission hurdle) or it doesn't. Being cleaner than the threshold earns nothing extra. The entire reward is binary, you're in or you're out.

Continuous-function regimes use a declining benchmark. Credits are earned in proportion to distance below the benchmark. A fuel that is twice as clean earns twice the credit. Ongoing improvements are continuously monetizable.

This distinction matters for every downstream question: whether continuous operational improvement adds value, whether a project earns more by getting cleaner, and, critically for Chapter 8's discussion of voluntary carbon credits, whether there is any room left for a voluntary credit on top.

The major regimes.

Several things to notice:

None of the compliance regimes requires an additionality test. The fuel's CI score earns credits regardless of whether the project would have existed without credit revenue. These regimes are infrastructure policies, not project-crediting mechanisms. They reward performance, not counterfactual reasoning.

Corsia is the exception. The international aviation scheme has a voluntary carbon market-style additionality requirement because it accepts voluntary-market offset credits alongside lifecycle saff benefits. This makes Corsia a hybrid, simultaneously a compliance scheme and a demand signal for the voluntary market.

The step-function/continuous-function distinction tracks geography and purpose. The U.S. federal RFS is a volume mandate, it wants a certain amount of renewable fuel in the mix. California's L.C.F.S. is an emissions-reduction instrument, it wants the fuel pool to get progressively cleaner. These are different policy goals producing different economic structures.

The regimes in detail.

RFS (Renewable Fuel Standard).

The U.S. RFS mandates that fuel suppliers blend a volume of qualifying biofuels into the transportation fuel pool annually. The tradeable unit is the RIN (Renewable Identification Number), a serial number attached to each gallon-equivalent of qualifying renewable fuel at production and detached at blending.

The RFS is a step function: a fuel either passes the lifecycle greenhouse gas-reduction threshold for its D-code category (D3 cellulosic, D4 biomass-based diesel, D5 advanced, D6 conventional, D7 cellulosic diesel) or it doesn't. Exceeding the threshold earns no additional credit. RIN value is determined entirely by supply-demand between obligated parties (refiners, importers) and renewable-fuel producers. A D4 RIN at 0.70 dollars-1.00 dollars/RIN translates to roughly 70-100 dollars per tonne of CO2-equivalent on a per-tonne basis, but this is coincidental, the RIN price is driven by mandate volume and blending economics, not by emissions-reduction efficiency.

L.C.F.S. (Low Carbon Fuel Standard).

California's L.C.F.S. is the most analytically sophisticated fuel-standard regime in operation. Credits are denominated in tonnes of CO2-equivalent and scale continuously with carbon intensity, the lifecycle greenhouse gas emissions of a fuel in gCO2e per MJ.

The regime sets a declining annual CI benchmark for the transportation fuel pool. Every fuel scoring below the benchmark earns a credit proportional to the gap; every fuel above the benchmark generates a deficit. Obligated parties must retire enough credits to cover their deficits.

The key structural feature: because the benchmark declines annually, credits earned by a fixed-CI fuel shrink over time even with no changes to the fuel itself. This is the "ratchet" mechanism. It drives ongoing innovation, producers who can get their CI lower than competitors earn more credits from the same volume.

Price (2026): the Q1 reference was ~63 dollars per tonne of CO2-equivalent, firming to ~66 dollars-71 by Q2 2026 as the market tightened (the benchmark declines and the credit bank has drawn down from its 2021-2023 peak). The program's Credit Clearance Market sets a maximum price that escalates annually, confirmed at 268.90 dollars per tonne of CO2-equivalent through May 31 2026, with the mid-2026 figure (~275 dollars) set by Carb's annual adjustment.

Some fuels achieve deeply negative CI, dairy biomethane, for instance, can score around −250 gCO2e/MJ because capturing methane that would otherwise escape earns a large avoided-emissions credit. L.C.F.S. pays for this CI performance; being far below the applicable benchmark (~80-87 gCO2e/MJ depending on fuel type) produces a very large credit per unit of fuel. This "negative-CI mechanic" deserves careful reading: it is a counterfactual avoidance claim embedded inside a fuel score, with the same additionality and baseline vulnerabilities as a voluntary carbon market avoidance credit. Chapter 8 develops this point in Part 8.

45Z (Clean Fuel Production Credit).

The U.S. production tax credit for low-CI transport fuels, effective 2025 through 2029. Value scales with CI, up to approximately 1.00 dollars/gallon for saff depending on the emissions profile. A U.S. HEFA saff producer selling into California can simultaneously capture a D4/D5 RIN, an L.C.F.S. credit, and a 45Z credit on the same gallon, a compliance stack that creates some of the most favorable fuel-project economics anywhere in the world.

REDIII and ReFuelEU Aviation.

The E.U.'s Renewable Energy Directive III sets binding renewable-fuel sub-targets for transportation including a dedicated RFNBO e-fuels sub-mandate. REDIII relies primarily on default CI values by feedstock pathway, standardized lifecycle scores published in annexes, not plant-specific measurements. An "actual value" certification pathway exists but requires separate effort. The gap between default and actual values is analytically significant: it is the location of what Chapter 8 calls the "boundary-gap white space" in voluntary fuel credits.

ReFuelEU Aviation mandates escalating saff blending percentages at E.U. airports: 2 percent in 2025 rising to 70 percent by 2050, with a dedicated eSAF sub-mandate. It is a volume obligation, it rewards being a qualifying saff, not continuous improvement. A producer who gets cleaner does not earn additional compliance value once the mandate is met. This distinction has direct consequences for where voluntary credits can and cannot be layered on top.

FuelEU Maritime.

FuelEU Maritime (effective 2025) sets declining greenhouse gas-intensity targets for energy used by ships calling at E.U. ports. Until 2033, renewable fuels of non-biological origin, including green ammonia, count double toward a ship's greenhouse gas-intensity target. FuelEU is a newer and still-maturing regime; the IMO's global greenhouse gas mechanism remains in formation and will interact with FuelEU as both systems develop.

Stacking and its limits.

These regimes can stack on the same physical gallon. A U.S. HEFA saff producer selling into California can legitimately earn a RIN (from the RFS), an L.C.F.S. credit (for CI below the benchmark), and a 45Z tax credit simultaneously. Each instrument targets a different market failure: RINs drive volume; L.C.F.S. drives improvement; 45Z drives investment.

The stacking limit is the attribute. A physical gallon can only be sold once. Its CI attributes can only be counted toward one compliance obligation at a time. The book-and-claim mechanism (discussed in Chapter 8) allows attributes to be separated from the physical molecule, but simultaneous compliance monetization across two regimes for the same attribute is double-counting. This constraint becomes critical when asking whether a voluntary carbon credit can also be layered on top, the central question of Chapter 8 Part 8.

As the L.C.F.S. family expands (Oregon 2016 leads to Washington 2023 leads to Canada CFR 2023 leads to potential Singapore framework 2027+), the stacking question scales proportionally: a producer with multiple market relationships may face competing compliance claims from several CI-based programs on the same gallon of fuel. The attribute-claiming discipline described here applies across all of them.

Stop-and-check 7.H.

The L.C.F.S. and RFS both apply to the same fuel markets but have fundamentally different economic structures. Which rewards continuous improvement, and which rewards qualifying? What are the implications for producer incentives?
Why does California's L.C.F.S. have more "granularity" than the E.U.'s REDIII? What does this mean for which system leaves more room for ongoing improvement to be rewarded, and for which system closes more potential voluntary-credit white space?
A U.S. HEFA saff producer earns a RIN, an L.C.F.S. credit, and a 45Z credit on the same gallon. Can they also sell a voluntary carbon credit on the same tonne of abatement? Why or why not? (Answer developed in Chapter 8.)
Socratic prompt: The E.U. is building out ReFuelEU (volume mandate) and the U.S. maintains L.C.F.S.-style systems (continuous improvement). From an emissions-reduction standpoint, which architecture produces more abatement per dollar of policy cost? What's the trade-off? And which architecture is more likely to expand internationally, and why?

Answers

The L.C.F.S. rewards continuous improvement; the RFS rewards qualifying. The L.C.F.S. is a continuous-function regime: credits scale with the gap between a fuel's carbon intensity and a declining benchmark, so a fuel that is twice as clean earns roughly twice the credit, and getting cleaner always pays. The RFS is a step function: a fuel either passes its D-code lifecycle threshold or it doesn't, and over-performing beyond the threshold earns nothing extra. The implication for producers is that under the L.C.F.S., ongoing CI reduction (feedstock switching, process-energy decarbonization, efficiency) is continuously monetizable, driving innovation; under the RFS, the incentive stops the moment the fuel qualifies, so the system drives volume of qualifying fuel rather than ever-deeper decarbonization.

The L.C.F.S. is more granular because it scores carbon intensity continuously and plant-specifically, feedstock CI, process-energy switching, and efficiency gains all move the credit volume, whereas REDIII relies primarily on default CI values assigned by feedstock pathway, which blunt plant-level gains by averaging them into a standardized score. This means the L.C.F.S. leaves less room for ongoing improvement to go unrewarded: it captures the improvement itself. REDIII's default-value structure leaves real plant-level improvements the methodology cannot score, and that gap between default and actual values is precisely where voluntary-credit "boundary-gap" white space can exist. The L.C.F.S., by capturing improvement comprehensively, closes most of that voluntary white space; REDIII leaves more open.

No, and this is the central point developed in Chapter 8. Earning a RIN, an L.C.F.S. credit, and a 45Z credit means the compliance regimes have already claimed the emissions-reduction attribute of that gallon and provided the revenue that makes the project viable. A voluntary credit requires additionality (the reduction would not have happened without the credit revenue) and a single, unclaimed attribute. A gallon richly paid by the compliance stack is both non-additional (the stack already finances it) and already-claimed (selling a voluntary credit on the same attribute is double-claiming). The richer the compliance stack, the less defensible any voluntary credit on top, the "additionality inversion."

The honest answer is that the two architectures optimize for different things, so "more abatement per policy dollar" depends on the goal. The continuous-improvement architecture extracts deeper decarbonization per unit of fuel because it rewards every increment of CI reduction, plausibly yielding more abatement per dollar where the fuel pool can keep getting cleaner; the volume-mandate (ReFuelEU) architecture guarantees a quantity of qualifying clean fuel regardless of price, which buys deployment certainty but leaves over-performance unrewarded and can overpay for marginal qualifying volume. The trade-off is depth versus certainty of deployment. On international expansion, the L.C.F.S. family is already diffusing (Oregon, Washington, Canada's CFR, potential Singapore framework) because its credit-market mechanics are transplantable and let any producer plug in; volume mandates are more tightly bound to a specific jurisdiction's fuel supply and harder to export. The decisive variables are whether a jurisdiction prioritizes guaranteed volumes or maximal abatement, and whether it has the administrative capacity to run a CI-scoring market.

Closing exercise.

Three things to take away:

1. Cap-and-trade is the dominant operational instrument for major-economy carbon pricing. The E.U. E.T.S. at 75 euros per tonne of CO2-equivalent is the largest, most consequential, and most politically durable carbon-pricing system in the world. California's Cap-and-Invest is the broadest by sectoral coverage. China's national E.T.S. is the largest by emissions volume. Sub-national programs in the U.S. have survived federal headwinds.

2. Free allocation has been the dominant integrity issue. Most systems have given away substantial value to incumbent industry in order to maintain political support. The trajectory has been toward less free allocation, but the rate of phase-out and the design of C.B.A.M.-like border adjustments are central design questions for the next decade.

3. The 2026 E.U. E.T.S. reform and C.B.A.M. phase-in are the next decade's most consequential decisions. What the E.U. does in 2026 about Phase 5 trajectory, MSR design, C.D.R. integration, and C.B.A.M. expansion will set the global template. Other jurisdictions will follow.

The compliance carbon markets are the most stable and durable part of the climate-policy landscape. Voluntary markets, by contrast, covered in Chapter 8, operate under different logic and have had a much more turbulent decade.

What this chapter simplified.

1. The treatment of cap-and-trade governance. I described "the regulator" sets the cap, but in practice cap-setting involves multi-stage political and administrative processes with substantial industry input. The E.U. E.T.S. cap is set by legislative process (E.T.S. Directive amendment) plus implementing acts plus annual administration. California's cap is set by Carb regulation under statutory authority. Each system has distinctive governance dynamics that affect outcomes.

2. The relationship between cap-and-trade and complementary policies. Carbon pricing rarely operates alone. The E.U. has E.T.S. plus renewable energy directives plus energy efficiency directives plus member-state policies. California has Cap-and-Invest plus AB 32 plus SB 32 plus various sector-specific mandates plus complementary policies. The interaction of pricing and standards is itself contested, some argue standards make pricing redundant; some argue pricing without standards is insufficient.

3. The aviation and maritime story. I gave brief coverage. The treatment of international aviation (Corsia, also briefly under E.U. E.T.S. for intra-EEA flights) and shipping (IMO carbon pricing under development) deserves more attention than I gave it. Chapter 12 (hard-to-abate sectors) will provide more depth.

4. The intensity-based versus absolute-cap distinction. I described China's intensity-based system briefly. The intensity-vs-absolute question is significant for India's CCTS, several other emerging systems, and even some debates about expanding existing absolute-cap systems. The mechanics of intensity-based ETSs differ substantially from what I described as cap-and-trade fundamentals.

5. The carbon-tax detail. I gave the carbon-tax discussion much less space than cap-and-trade. The two are sometimes complementary, not just alternatives, and a fuller carbon-tax discussion would cover Pigovian taxation theory, optimal-tax frameworks, the social cost of carbon, and the empirical record of specific systems (Sweden, BC, Canada national, France's failed attempt, et cetera). I'll touch on these in Chapter 9 (carbon pricing more broadly).
