## Why this chapter exists

The previous five chapters have covered carbon pricing, markets, and removal technologies. This chapter asks a different question: **where are the emissions that have to be addressed, and why are they hard?**

The hard-to-abate sectors collectively produce roughly **35–40% of global CO₂ emissions** — steel and cement together about 14%, chemicals about 5%, aviation about 2.5%, shipping about 2%, plus other industrial processes. These sectors share several features:

- **Process emissions** that don't go away when you switch energy sources.
- **High-temperature heat** requirements that resist easy electrification.
- **Long-lived capital stock** (steel mills built today operate for 40–60 years).
- **Globally traded products** that face carbon leakage risk under unilateral policy.
- **Limited substitution options** in many cases (you can't replace cement with renewable electricity).

These sectors are also where **the policy and market instruments from earlier chapters meet physical reality**. Carbon pricing is necessary but insufficient. Subsidies and mandates are necessary but insufficient. CDR is necessary for the truly intractable residuals. The integrated decarbonization of these sectors is the most important practical climate-policy challenge of the next 25 years.

I'll organize this in seven parts: what makes a sector hard-to-abate; **steel** in depth (the largest single industrial emitter); **cement** in depth (the most chemically intractable); **chemicals and refining**; **aviation**; **shipping**; and the policy architecture for hard-to-abate decarbonization (green premiums, sectoral standards, CBAM, demand-side levers).

# Part 1: What makes a sector hard-to-abate

The phrase "hard-to-abate" is doing more work than it appears. Several distinct features can make decarbonization difficult:

## Process emissions

Some emissions come from **chemical reactions inherent to the production process**, not from burning fuel. The classic example: cement manufacture.

Cement is produced primarily from limestone (calcium carbonate, CaCO₃). The chemistry of cement production requires heating limestone to decompose it into lime (CaO) plus CO₂:

$$\text{CaCO}_3 \xrightarrow{\text{heat}} \text{CaO} + \text{CO}_2$$

This reaction releases **~535 kg of CO₂ per ton of CaO produced**. About 60% of cement's CO₂ emissions come from this calcination reaction; only 40% come from the fuel burned to heat the kiln.

You cannot decarbonize cement by switching to clean energy alone. Even with 100% renewable electricity heating the kiln, the 60% process emissions remain. The only options for these emissions are: (a) capture them with CCS, (b) use alternative chemistries that don't release CO₂, or (c) reduce demand for cement.

Other process-emissions sectors include: **ammonia** (CO₂ released when natural gas is reformed to make hydrogen), **steel** (carbon from coke reacts with iron oxide), **lime** (the same calcination as cement), **soda ash, glass, refining**, and others.

## High-temperature heat

Many industrial processes require **temperatures above 1,000°C** that are difficult to achieve with electricity:

- Cement kilns: ~1,450°C
- Steel blast furnaces: ~2,000°C
- Glass furnaces: ~1,500°C
- Aluminum smelting: ~960°C (electrolysis, not heating per se)
- Petrochemical crackers: ~800–900°C

Electric heating at these temperatures is technologically possible but typically far more expensive than fuel combustion, and requires extensive grid upgrades to handle the load. The transition from fuel-fired to electric high-temperature heat is one of the slowest aspects of industrial decarbonization.

## Long-lived capital stock

Industrial assets typically have 30–60 year useful lifetimes:
- Steel mills: 40–60 years
- Cement plants: 40–60 years
- Refineries: 40–60 years
- Aircraft: 20–30 years
- Ships: 20–25 years

Retiring these assets before their useful life ends represents stranded value. Decarbonization strategies either accelerate retirement (politically and financially costly) or replace assets at the end of their natural lives (much slower).

This is why many decarbonization pathways are explicit about "vintage" effects: new capacity built post-2030 should be low-carbon; existing capacity will continue operating for decades.

## Trade exposure and carbon leakage

Steel, cement, aluminum, fertilizers — these are commodity goods traded globally. If one jurisdiction imposes carbon costs but others don't, production shifts to the unrestricted jurisdiction. The carbon doesn't get reduced; it just relocates.

This is the **carbon leakage** problem (Ch. 7). Solutions include:
- **Free allocation** (covers the cost, but undermines the price signal)
- **CBAM** (extends the price to imports)
- **International agreements** (rare — examples include the Steel Climate Standard initiatives)
- **Subsidies for domestic clean production** (the IRA approach)

The trade-exposure dimension makes unilateral policy especially difficult and is a major reason these sectors are slow to decarbonize.

## Limited substitution

For many hard-to-abate sectors, substitutes don't exist or are inadequate:

- **Cement**: alternatives (geopolymer cement, blended cements) exist but cover only fraction of total demand.
- **Steel**: scrap recycling is the lowest-carbon path but is limited by scrap availability.
- **Long-haul aviation**: battery aircraft work for short ranges; not for transatlantic.
- **Long-haul shipping**: similar fundamental energy-density problem.
- **Ammonia**: green hydrogen ammonia is feasible but expensive.

The "decarbonize by replacing the activity with something else" approach has limits. These sectors must decarbonize the activity itself.

## Why these sectors matter

Together, hard-to-abate sectors account for roughly 35–40% of global CO₂ emissions. Their share of total emissions has grown as power sector emissions have fallen — the relative importance of the hard sectors increases as the easier sectors decarbonize.

If the easy parts of the economy reach net-zero by 2050 but the hard sectors remain at current emissions levels, total emissions still won't fall below 30% of current levels. The hard sectors are where the post-2030 climate fight largely lives.

## **Stop-and-check 12.A**

1. The chemistry of cement makes ~60% of its emissions chemically unavoidable absent CCS. Could you electrify the remaining 40% (fuel) emissions? What does this imply about the role of carbon capture in cement?
2. Industrial capital stock turnover is slow (30–60 years). For a steel mill built in 2020, retiring it in 2030 represents a substantial value loss. How does this shape decarbonization policy?
3. CBAM addresses carbon leakage for EU-bound goods but does nothing about leakage from EU-imposed costs on the rest of the world's trade. Is this a meaningful limit on CBAM?
4. **Socratic prompt:** If you were designing decarbonization policy for India's steel and cement industries in 2026, what would you prioritize given the sector's growth trajectory and CBAM exposure?

**Answers**

1. Yes, the ~40% fuel-derived emissions can in principle be electrified or fuel-switched (the kiln heat is replaceable, even at ~1,450°C), but the ~60% process emissions from calcination — CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ — are released by the chemistry itself and remain regardless of energy source. This implies CCS is essentially unavoidable for deep cement decarbonization: short of alternative chemistries that don't calcine limestone, or reducing cement demand, capturing the process CO₂ is the only way to address the majority of cement's emissions. Clean energy alone caps out at roughly a 40% cut.

2. Slow capital turnover (30–60 years) means forcing early retirement of a 2020-vintage mill strands large amounts of capital, which is politically and financially costly. This pushes policy toward "vintage" strategies: require new-build capacity post-2030 to be low-carbon while letting existing assets run out their lives, and favor retrofit options (like BF-BOF + CCS) that decarbonize existing plants rather than scrapping them. It also means the decarbonization timeline is paced by the natural replacement cycle — fast results require either subsidized early retirement or retrofits.

3. It is a real but partial limit. CBAM only equalizes the carbon cost of goods entering the EU; it does nothing about carbon-intensive steel or cement traded among non-EU countries, which is the large majority of global trade. So CBAM cannot by itself drive global decarbonization — it mainly protects EU producers from leakage and creates a low-carbon export incentive for CBAM-exposed sellers. Its broader climate leverage depends on whether it nudges exporters to clean up production wholesale and whether other jurisdictions adopt analogous border measures.

4. The decisive variables are India's growth trajectory (rising steel and cement demand means most relevant capacity is not yet built) and CBAM exposure (only the EU-bound export slice faces the border price). A sensible priority is therefore to ensure new capacity is built low-carbon — leveraging India's solar resources for clinker substitution, SCMs, and eventually H₂-DRI and CCS — rather than retrofitting young plants, while using CBAM-driven export demand to anchor first low-carbon lines. The trade-off is cost and electricity availability against the long-run penalty of locking in high-carbon assets for 40-plus years.

# Part 2: Steel — the largest industrial emitter

Steel production produces approximately **3.6–3.7 GtCO₂e annually** — over 7% of global CO₂ emissions. The path to decarbonization is technologically complex but conceptually clear.

## Why steel is high-carbon

Two main production routes dominate:

**1. Blast Furnace + Basic Oxygen Furnace (BF-BOF)** — produces about 70% of global steel. Uses coal (specifically metallurgical coke) as both an energy source and a chemical reductant. The chemistry: iron oxide + carbon → iron + CO₂. The reaction is direct — carbon physically extracts oxygen from iron ore, producing CO₂ as a byproduct.

Coal serves both as the fuel (providing heat) and as the reductant (removing oxygen from iron oxide). Without a substitute for the reduction reaction, you can't decarbonize a blast furnace by switching to clean energy.

**Emissions intensity:** ~1.8–2.0 tCO₂ per ton of crude steel from BF-BOF.

**2. Electric Arc Furnace (EAF)** — produces about 30% of global steel. Uses electricity to melt steel scrap (or direct-reduced iron, DRI). When the electricity is clean, EAF emissions are very low.

**Emissions intensity:** ~0.2–0.5 tCO₂ per ton of steel from EAF using mostly scrap; substantially higher if using carbon-intensive DRI.

The disparity is large: BF-BOF is roughly 4–8× more carbon-intensive than scrap-based EAF.

## Decarbonization pathways

Four major pathways:

### Pathway 1: Scrap recycling

Scrap-based EAF using clean electricity is the lowest-carbon production route — emissions can be near zero with renewable power.

**Constraints:** Scrap availability is finite (currently ~700 Mt/yr globally vs. ~1,900 Mt/yr total steel production). Recycling rates are already high in developed economies; growth potential is limited. New steel demand from growing economies cannot be met solely from scrap.

**Realistic share:** 30–45% of total steel production by 2050 depending on scrap collection improvements.

### Pathway 2: Hydrogen-DRI + EAF

The leading "green steel" technology. Hydrogen replaces coal as the reductant:

Iron oxide + H₂ → Iron + H₂O

The reduced iron (called DRI — Direct Reduced Iron, or sponge iron) is then melted in an EAF to produce steel. When the hydrogen is green (made from renewable electrolysis) and the EAF runs on clean electricity, total emissions can be **0.05–0.2 tCO₂ per ton of steel** — roughly 95% lower than BF-BOF.

**Key projects:**
- **Stegra** (formerly H₂ Green Steel), Boden, Sweden: 2.5 Mt/yr design capacity (initial phase). 100% green hydrogen-based DRI-EAF, powered by 700+ MW of electrolyzers. Commissioning ramping in 2025–2026.
- **HYBRIT** (Sweden, joint venture of SSAB, LKAB, Vattenfall): Pilot operational since 2020; demonstration scale-up to 2026; commercial scale-up planned 2030.
- **Thyssenkrupp Steel** (Germany): 2.5 Mt/yr H₂-DRI plant under construction at Duisburg. Operational target 2026–2027, though 2025 reports suggest delays.
- **ArcelorMittal**: Multiple H₂-DRI projects across Europe (Spain, Germany, France); 2025 saw multiple **postponements** of European EAF conversions citing economic and energy-cost uncertainty.
- **Salzgitter** (Germany): SALCOS program — DRI-EAF transition over 2025–2033.

**Key challenge — the 2025–2026 retrenchment:** Multiple major European green-steel projects have been delayed or paused in 2024–2025. The economic logic has shifted unfavorably:
- Green hydrogen production costs remained higher than projected.
- The "green premium" for low-carbon steel — the price gap that makes green steel commercially viable — has been smaller than expected, with green-steel premiums of only 20–30% in 2024 falling toward zero in early 2026 as some low-carbon steel price indices declined.
- Energy costs in Europe (industrial electricity prices ~€80–150/MWh) make hydrogen production expensive.
- CBAM revenue redistribution (the proceeds go to the EU budget, not to domestic decarbonization investment) has been politically contested.

The retrenchment is significant. It doesn't mean green steel won't happen — it means the transition timeline is shifting from "2026–2030 substantial deployment" to "2028–2035 substantial deployment."

### Pathway 3: BF-BOF + CCS

Capture CO₂ from existing blast furnace operations.

**Concept:** Keep the BF-BOF infrastructure but add post-combustion capture to handle the CO₂ output. Less disruptive than complete process replacement.

**Costs:** $60–130/tCO₂ for capture. Plus transport and storage.

**Major projects:** Limited so far. Tata Steel Netherlands has explored CCS; ArcelorMittal in some configurations. Various Chinese steel-CCS pilots.

**Critique:** Treats CCS as a permanent solution rather than a transition technology. If you're going to invest in major capital changes, why not move to H₂-DRI instead?

**Defense:** For existing assets with remaining useful life, CCS is faster and cheaper than replacing with new H₂-DRI. Particularly relevant in regions without abundant clean electricity.

### Pathway 4: Emerging alternative chemistries

Several pre-commercial approaches:

- **Molten Oxide Electrolysis (MOE)** — Boston Metal's approach. Direct electrolytic reduction of iron oxide using electricity, no hydrogen needed. Very low theoretical emissions.
- **Aqueous electrolysis** — ElectraSteel and others. Lower-temperature electrochemical reduction.
- **Hydrogen plasma smelting** — uses hydrogen plasma instead of conventional fluxes.

These are pre-commercial in 2025. They may become important after 2030 but are not part of near-term deployment.

## The 2025–2030 outlook

The steel sector's near-term trajectory is more uncertain than CDR's. Two paths:

**The accelerated transition path:** H₂-DRI + EAF deployments succeed in Europe; CBAM creates demand for low-carbon steel; China's steel industry begins serious H₂-DRI experimentation; Stegra, Thyssenkrupp, ArcelorMittal projects ramp up; by 2030, perhaps 10–15% of global steel is produced via H₂-DRI.

**The retrenchment path:** European green-steel projects continue to slip; the green premium fails to materialize; H₂-DRI deployment is delayed to 2030+; existing BF-BOF capacity continues to operate; perhaps 3–5% of global steel from H₂-DRI by 2030.

The retrenchment path looks more probable based on 2025–2026 evidence. Steel decarbonization is shifting from "leading sector for hard-to-abate decarbonization" to "lagging behind the timeline."

This has implications beyond steel: the relative success or failure of green steel will affect whether other H₂-using sectors (chemicals, ammonia, shipping fuels) deploy hydrogen at scale.

# Part 3: Cement — the most chemically intractable

Cement production releases approximately **2.4 GtCO₂e annually** — about 5% of global emissions. The chemistry makes it harder to decarbonize than steel.

## The cement carbon problem

As discussed in Part 1, cement has two emissions sources:
- **Process emissions** (from limestone calcination): ~60% of total. Chemically required.
- **Fuel emissions** (from heating the kiln to ~1,450°C): ~40% of total. Replaceable.

The chemistry: limestone (CaCO₃) becomes lime (CaO) in the kiln, releasing CO₂. The lime is then mixed with other materials (silica, alumina, iron) to form clinker, which is ground into Portland cement.

You can replace the kiln fuel (gas, coal, alternative fuels) with cleaner alternatives, but you can't eliminate the calcination CO₂ without either:
- Using **alternative materials** that don't release CO₂ during processing
- Capturing the CO₂ at the cement plant (CCS)

## Decarbonization pathways

Four main approaches:

### Pathway 1: Clinker substitution and supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs)

Reducing the **clinker-to-cement ratio** — using more SCMs (fly ash, slag, calcined clays, natural pozzolans) and less clinker.

Current global clinker-to-cement ratio: ~0.72. Lowering this to 0.55–0.60 reduces emissions proportionally.

**Constraints:** SCM availability. Fly ash (from coal power plants) is in declining supply as coal phases out. Slag (from blast furnaces) is similarly declining. Calcined clays are more abundant but require process modifications.

**Realistic share of total reductions:** 15–30% of cement emissions reductions achievable this way.

### Pathway 2: Alternative cement chemistries

New cement formulations that use less or no limestone:

- **Calcium sulfoaluminate (CSA) cement** — uses bauxite and calcium sulfate, lower CO₂.
- **Magnesium-based cements** (MgO cements) — different chemistry, lower CO₂ but limited commercial deployment.
- **Geopolymer cement** — uses industrial byproducts; very low CO₂ but performance limitations.
- **Bio-cement** — emerging area using bacterial precipitation.

**Constraints:** Performance limitations, regulatory acceptance, slow market adoption. Conventional Portland cement standards are well-established; alternative cements face structural-engineering qualification challenges.

**Realistic share:** 5–15% of reductions over the next decade.

### Pathway 3: CCS on cement plants

Capture the CO₂ at the kiln exhaust. The technology is mature; deployment is just beginning.

**Major project:** **Brevik Cement** (Heidelberg Materials, Norway) — **operational 2025**. World's first commercial CCS at a cement plant. Captures ~400 ktCO₂/yr (about 50% of plant emissions). CO₂ ships to Northern Lights for storage. Costs in the $80–100/tCO₂ range.

Other CCS-cement projects in pipeline: HeidelbergCement Lengfurt (Germany), CEMEX Hidalgo (Mexico), Holcim Lake Erie (Canada), various Chinese cement-CCS pilots.

**Realistic potential:** CCS could address 40–60% of cement emissions reductions. The biggest of the three pathways quantitatively.

### Pathway 4: Demand reduction

Use less cement: better building design, alternative materials (cross-laminated timber, etc.), longer-lived buildings.

**Realistic share:** 10–20% of cement emissions reductions through demand-side measures.

## The cement decarbonization roadmap

Aggregating these pathways: by 2050, cement emissions could plausibly be reduced 50–80% below current levels through a combination of clinker substitution, alternative chemistries, CCS, and demand reduction. The remaining emissions would require CDR neutralization.

The **Global Cement and Concrete Association (GCCA) Net Zero Roadmap** (2023, updated) targets net-zero concrete by 2050 with substantial reliance on CCS (~40% of cumulative reductions) plus the other pathways.

## The 2025–2030 cement outlook

Cement's near-term trajectory is more positive than steel's, partly because:
- Brevik is operational, demonstrating CCS feasibility.
- CBAM coverage of cement (Q1 2026 enforcement) creates direct economic incentive.
- The technology pathways are more mature than green steel's hydrogen dependence.
- Major producers (Heidelberg Materials, Holcim, CEMEX) have committed to CCS-led decarbonization.

The cement sector is plausibly the first major hard-to-abate sector to begin meaningful decarbonization at commercial scale.

## **Stop-and-check 12.B**

1. Cement's process emissions are chemically unavoidable. If the global cement industry decided to abandon CCS as a pathway, what would the implications be for total cement decarbonization?
2. Brevik's $80–100/tCO₂ capture cost is competitive with current EU ETS prices. Why hasn't every European cement plant moved to CCS in 2025–2026?
3. Cement is largely a regional product (high transport costs limit international trade) — so CBAM has more limited leverage than for steel. Does this matter for European cement decarbonization?
4. **Socratic prompt:** A cement plant in India in 2026 faces the choice of (a) investing in CCS at $80/tCO₂, (b) shifting toward SCMs and clinker substitution at lower cost but slower decarbonization, (c) doing nothing and paying CBAM costs on EU-bound exports. What would you advise?

**Answers**

1. Abandoning CCS would put deep cement decarbonization largely out of reach. CCS is the quantitatively largest pathway — capable of addressing roughly 40–60% of cement emissions reductions and counted on for about 40% of cumulative reductions in the GCCA roadmap. The remaining levers (clinker substitution at 15–30%, alternative chemistries at 5–15%, demand reduction at 10–20%) are individually constrained by SCM supply, performance/regulatory acceptance, and demand limits, and even combined cannot close the gap created by chemically unavoidable calcination emissions. Without CCS, a large residual would be left for costly CDR neutralization.

2. Because the headline capture cost understates the full economics and the barriers are structural, not just price. Brevik's $80–100/tCO₂ excludes transport and permanent storage, which require CO₂ pipelines or shipping and accessible geological sites that most cement plants lack — cement is regional and plants are not sited near storage. Add 20–30 year payback periods needing policy certainty, capital intensity, the bankability problem, and the fact that CBAM only bites on EU-bound volumes, and most plants face a coordination and infrastructure gap rather than a simple cost comparison against the ETS price.

3. It matters considerably. Because cement is heavy and low-value relative to transport cost, little is traded internationally, so CBAM — which only prices imports into the EU — has weak leverage over European cement decarbonization compared with steel, where trade is global. EU cement producers face little import competition, so leakage risk is lower but so is the CBAM-driven demand pull. This means European cement decarbonization must be driven primarily by the domestic ETS price, free-allocation phase-out, subsidies, and procurement standards rather than by the border mechanism.

4. The decisive variables are the plant's exposure to EU exports, its cost of capital, and access to CO₂ storage. For most Indian cement (largely domestic, no nearby storage), a defensible sequence starts with (b) — SCMs and clinker substitution are cheaper, need no transport-and-storage infrastructure, and deliver immediate partial cuts — while building toward (a) CCS for the unavoidable calcination emissions as storage and finance mature. Pure (c) is shortsighted: it forfeits the low-cost reductions and the export incentive. The answer tilts toward (a) sooner only if EU export share and CBAM costs are large.

# Part 4: Chemicals and refining

The chemicals sector — particularly ammonia, ethylene, methanol, and refining — produces approximately **2.5–3 GtCO₂e annually**, about 5–6% of global emissions.

## The chemicals problem

Chemical manufacturing emits CO₂ in three ways:
- **Energy emissions** from process heat (often high-temperature)
- **Process emissions** from chemical reactions (similar to cement/steel)
- **Lifecycle emissions** from the end-use of products (many chemicals end up as CO₂ when their products decompose or burn)

The major chemical commodities by emissions:

**Ammonia (NH₃):** ~500 MtCO₂e/yr. Used primarily for nitrogen fertilizers. Currently produced almost entirely via the Haber-Bosch process using natural gas as both feedstock and energy source. Roughly **2 tCO₂ per ton of ammonia**.

**Ethylene (C₂H₄):** ~280 MtCO₂e/yr. Building block for plastics, antifreeze, many other products. Produced by cracking naphtha or ethane. Energy-intensive; ~1.5–2 tCO₂ per ton of ethylene.

**Methanol (CH₃OH):** ~150 MtCO₂e/yr. Used for chemicals, fuels, increasingly as a maritime fuel (Part 6). Currently produced from natural gas; ~0.5–1 tCO₂ per ton of methanol.

**Refining:** ~1 GtCO₂e/yr from refinery operations (separate from end-use of fuels). Process heat, hydrogen production, various refinery functions.

## Decarbonization pathways

### Green ammonia

Replace natural-gas-based hydrogen with green hydrogen from electrolysis:
- Green hydrogen (electrolytic, from renewables) + Haber-Bosch nitrogen fixation = green ammonia.
- Emissions: near zero if hydrogen is green.
- Cost: ~$700–1,500/ton green ammonia vs. ~$400–600/ton conventional. Premium ~60–250%.

**Major projects:** Yara's Pilbara green ammonia (Australia); Saudi NEOM green ammonia; multiple Indian projects; CF Industries' US deployments.

**Outlook:** Green ammonia is one of the more deployable hydrogen applications because the chemistry is well-established (Haber-Bosch has been operating since 1909). The cost gap is the main barrier; declining electrolyzer costs and CBAM-driven demand for low-carbon ammonia exports could narrow it.

### Blue ammonia (CCS-coupled)

Conventional natural-gas ammonia + CCS to capture and store process CO₂.
- Emissions: 70–90% reduction from conventional.
- Cost: ~$500–700/ton — closer to conventional than green.
- Advantage: Uses existing infrastructure plus CCS retrofit.

**Major projects:** Multiple Saudi, UAE, US blue ammonia projects in development. The Japan-Korea LNG import infrastructure has explored converting to blue ammonia imports as marine fuel.

### Green and blue methanol

Similar logic — replace natural gas as feedstock with green hydrogen + biogenic or captured CO₂ (green methanol), or use natural gas + CCS (blue methanol).

**Major projects:** Various Nordic and German green methanol projects; A.P. Moller-Maersk methanol-fueled container ships demanding green methanol supply.

### Ethylene electrification

Electric crackers: using electric heat instead of fuel combustion to crack naphtha/ethane into ethylene.
- BASF, Linde, SABIC, Dow have announced electric cracker projects.
- BASF/Linde/SABIC Ludwigshafen e-cracker reached FID in 2024 for 6 MW pilot.

**Outlook:** Substantial promise but small commercial scale today. Full deployment requires both technology maturation and very cheap, abundant renewable electricity.

### Bio-based chemicals

Replace petrochemical feedstocks with biomass-derived alternatives. Mature for some products (bioethanol, biodiesel) but limited for most major commodities. Likely contributes 10–20% of long-term decarbonization.

## The chemicals 2025–2030 outlook

Chemicals decarbonization is at an earlier stage than cement or steel. Pilot and demonstration projects are deploying; commercial-scale deployment is mostly post-2027. The sector is highly capital-intensive and globally traded — the same trade-leakage concerns apply.

CBAM coverage of hydrogen products (from Q1 2026) and fertilizers creates direct demand for low-carbon ammonia exports to Europe. This is a meaningful demand pull.

# Part 5: Aviation

Aviation produces approximately **950 MtCO₂/yr** (2024, recovering from pandemic). That's about 2.5% of global CO₂ but ~12% of transportation-sector emissions and ~2.5% of all anthropogenic radiative forcing (including non-CO₂ effects from contrails and NOₓ at altitude).

The 2024 emissions were ~90% of pre-COVID levels; **2025 emissions are expected to surpass 2019 levels** as travel demand continues to recover. The trajectory is upward, not down.

## Why aviation is hard

Two fundamental constraints:
- **Energy density requirement**: Jet fuel has very high energy density (~43 MJ/kg). Batteries have ~0.5 MJ/kg (~85× lower); compressed hydrogen ~7 MJ/kg in usable form (~6× lower). Long-haul aviation requires fuels that store enough energy per unit weight to fly.
- **Asset turnover**: Aircraft lifetimes are 20–30 years. The current fleet largely cannot be substantially modified for alternative fuels. Decarbonization happens primarily as new aircraft are built.

The result: aviation is among the hardest sectors to decarbonize, and the easiest decarbonization pathway is to keep using jet-fuel-like fuels — but make them low-carbon.

## Decarbonization pathways

### Pathway 1: Sustainable aviation fuel (SAF)

SAF is **chemically similar to conventional jet fuel** but produced from non-fossil feedstocks. It can be blended with conventional jet fuel up to 50% (with some pathways limited to 10%) and works in existing aircraft without modification.

**Production pathways:**
- **HEFA (Hydroprocessed Esters and Fatty Acids)** — from used cooking oils, animal fats, plant oils. The dominant current production method.
- **FT (Fischer-Tropsch)** — from biomass via gasification.
- **ATJ (Alcohol-to-jet)** — from ethanol/butanol fermentation.
- **Power-to-Liquid (PtL) e-fuels** — synthesized from green hydrogen + captured CO₂. Very low carbon when both inputs are clean. The most scalable long-term pathway.

**Current state:**
- Global SAF production in 2025: roughly 2–3 billion gallons (~10 Mt) — about 0.5–1% of total jet fuel consumption.
- US: ~240 million gallons domestic SAF registered for RFS in 2025.
- US 45Z Clean Fuel Production Credit: up to $1/gallon for SAF (post-OBBBA, down from $1.75 previously). OBBBA (July 2025) *extended* the credit's sunset from its original end-2027 date to **December 31, 2029**, while restricting eligibility to North American feedstocks and excluding indirect land-use-change (ILUC) emissions from the CI calculation.
- EU ReFuelEU Aviation mandate: 2% SAF in 2025, escalating to 6% in 2030, 20% in 2035, 70% in 2050 (with sub-targets for e-fuels).
- UK SAF Mandate: 2% in 2025, 10% in 2030, 22% in 2040.
- Singapore: announced mandatory SAF levy 2027 with 1% blend target, scaling to 5% by 2030.
- Norway, Sweden, France: existing national mandates.

**Costs:** SAF is currently 2–5× more expensive than conventional jet fuel. The "green premium" is substantial. Power-to-liquid e-fuels are even more expensive ($3–8/liter equivalent).

> **The compliance economics of SAF** — how the RFS, California LCFS, 45Z, REDIII, and ReFuelEU stack on a single gallon, how feedstock and process-energy choices move a fuel's carbon-intensity score (and therefore its credit revenue), and how a producer routes product to the most lucrative market — are treated in **Appendix A (Fuel-Standard Compliance Operations and the Fuel–VCM Boundary)**, building on the markets framework of Ch. 7–8. The short version: a US HEFA gallon sold into California can stack RIN + LCFS + 45Z for some of the most favorable fuel-project economics anywhere — which is also precisely why such a gallon cannot support a voluntary carbon credit on top (Ch. 8 Part 8).

### Pathway 2: Hydrogen aircraft

Hydrogen-fueled aircraft (Airbus' ZEROe concept, others) — pre-commercial. The energy-density problem makes hydrogen aviation challenging for long-haul; potentially feasible for short and medium routes.

**Outlook:** Commercial deployment unlikely before 2035+. The technology and infrastructure (hydrogen production, distribution to airports, fueling) are pre-mature.

### Pathway 3: Electric aircraft

Battery-electric aircraft for short routes (regional, sub-300 km). Commercial deployment beginning with small aircraft (Heart Aerospace ES-30, Eviation Alice). Larger aircraft will require battery breakthroughs.

**Outlook:** 5–10% of aviation emissions plausibly addressable by 2050 via electrification.

### Pathway 4: Demand management

Carbon pricing on aviation, frequent-flyer levies, regulations on short-haul flights (France banned domestic flights where train alternatives exist <2.5 hours), shift to rail for short trips, reduced corporate travel.

**Realistic contribution:** 10–20% of aviation emissions reductions through demand-side measures over 30 years.

### Pathway 5: Operational efficiency

Better routing, fuel-efficient flying, more efficient aircraft (ongoing efficiency gains of ~1–2%/yr historically).

**Contribution:** ~30% cumulative improvement by 2050 through technology and operations.

## The aviation puzzle

The reality: aviation decarbonization is **substantially harder than other sectors**. Even with aggressive SAF deployment, hydrogen aviation development, and operational improvements, aviation will likely still produce **400–600 MtCO₂/yr in 2050** in optimistic scenarios — a substantial residual. CDR (Chapter 11) is the inevitable companion.

## Aviation in carbon markets

**CORSIA** (Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation): ICAO scheme covering international flights (about 60% of aviation emissions). Phase 1 (pilot) 2021–2023; Phase 2 (mandatory for participants) 2024–2026; Phase 3 (full mandatory) 2027 onward. Airlines purchase eligible CORSIA credits to offset emissions above 2019 baseline.

**EU ETS aviation:** Intra-EEA flights covered (~20% of aviation emissions). Free allowances phased out 2024; full auctioning by 2026. SAF use can reduce ETS obligations.

**Singapore SAF levy:** 2027 implementation; charge on tickets calibrated to cover SAF blend mandate cost.

**The CORSIA-EU ETS tension:** CORSIA uses lower-quality credits ($15–25/tCO₂) than what is required under EU ETS or SBTi standards. Airlines complain about cost; environmental groups complain about quality. The 2026–2027 implementation will test whether CORSIA achieves meaningful emission reductions or operates more as a token compliance scheme.

# Part 6: Shipping

International shipping produces approximately **1 GtCO₂/yr** — about 2.5% of global CO₂. The sector emits primarily from heavy fuel oil (HFO) used in ship engines.

## Why shipping is hard

Similar to aviation, but with even longer asset lives:
- **Energy density**: Long-distance ships need high-energy-density fuel.
- **Asset lives**: 20–25 years for ships.
- **Bunker fuel infrastructure**: Globally distributed; switching fuels requires worldwide infrastructure changes.

But shipping also has features that make decarbonization more tractable than aviation:
- **Weight tolerance**: Ships can carry heavier fuels (battery-electric short-haul is feasible).
- **Slower speed potential**: Reducing speed reduces fuel use substantially.
- **Larger fuel storage**: Can use hydrogen carriers (ammonia, methanol) more easily than aircraft.

## Decarbonization pathways

### Pathway 1: Alternative marine fuels

Several candidates:

**Methanol:** Drop-in capability in modified engines. Maersk has ordered ~18 methanol-fueled container ships; first commercial methanol-powered transoceanic vessel began operation 2024. Green methanol or e-methanol provides low-carbon outcomes.

**Ammonia:** Energy-dense, hydrogen carrier, no carbon in fuel. Strong candidate for long-haul. Several pilot vessels; multiple major shipowner orders. Concerns about toxicity and ammonia slip (N₂O emissions).

**Hydrogen:** Limited by storage challenges on ships. Better suited for short routes.

**LNG (transitional):** Lower carbon than HFO but methane slip undermines benefits. LNG is increasingly viewed as transitional, not destination.

**Wind-assist:** Rotor sails, kite sails (e.g., Cargill's WindWings, Berge Bulk projects) — provides 10–30% fuel reduction. Complementary to alternative fuels, not a complete solution.

**Biofuels:** Direct substitution feasible; constrained by feedstock availability.

### Pathway 2: Operational improvements

- **Slow steaming**: Reducing speeds reduces fuel use exponentially. Substantial uptake already; further potential.
- **Hull design**: Optimization, hull coatings, etc.
- **Just-in-time port arrivals**: Avoids unnecessary fuel use waiting outside ports.

### Pathway 3: Demand management

Modal shift from sea freight to other modes (limited possibilities for international shipping). Reduced shipping intensity of goods.

## The IMO framework

The **International Maritime Organization (IMO)** is the global regulatory body for international shipping.

**The 2025 IMO Agreement:** In April 2025, IMO member states reached a landmark agreement on shipping decarbonization. Key provisions:
- **30% reduction in CO₂ emissions by 2035, 65% by 2040** (vs. 2008 baseline), for vessels above 5,000 gross tonnage (covering ~85% of global fleet CO₂).
- **Tiered emissions penalty system**: Vessels exceeding caps pay **$100/tCO₂** if mitigation measures are in place; **$380/tCO₂** with no decarbonization efforts.
- **Entry into force: 2027**, following ratification at follow-up meeting October 2025.
- **US withdrew from negotiations** but the agreement proceeded.

The agreement is significant — the **first global carbon-pricing mechanism for international shipping**. The $100–380/tCO₂ range is comparable to current EU ETS levels and provides strong economic incentive for fuel switching.

**Industry response:** Major shipowners (Maersk, MSC, COSCO, others) accelerated alternative-fuel commitments through 2025. Order books for methanol-, ammonia-, and LNG-fueled ships rose substantially.

## The shipping outlook 2026–2030

The IMO agreement entering force in 2027 creates the strongest near-term decarbonization driver any hard-to-abate sector has. Combined with:
- EU ETS coverage of maritime (in force from 2024)
- Major shipowner fuel-switching commitments
- Methanol-fueled fleet expansion

Shipping is positioned for meaningful 2027–2035 decarbonization — potentially faster than aviation despite similar physical challenges, due to better policy framework.

## **Stop-and-check 12.C**

1. SAF is currently ~0.5–1% of global jet fuel. The EU mandate requires 6% by 2030. What does this scaling challenge imply about the feasibility of the EU mandate?
2. The 2025 IMO agreement creates a $100/tCO₂ "with mitigation" and $380/tCO₂ "without mitigation" tiered penalty. The US withdrew. Why did the agreement still proceed, and what does this say about the political economy of climate negotiations?
3. Shipping is electrifying short routes, switching to methanol/ammonia for longer routes, and adding wind-assist where useful. Aviation has fewer pathways. Why is shipping decarbonization moving faster than aviation despite similar fundamental constraints?
4. **Socratic prompt:** If you were a shipping company in 2026, considering whether to invest in 20-year methanol-fueled ships vs. 20-year conventional ships, what would shift your calculation toward methanol? What toward conventional?

**Answers**

1. The gap is large — moving from ~0.5–1% of jet fuel to a 6% mandate by 2030 is roughly a six- to twelvefold scale-up in five years — and it implies the EU target is aggressive against current supply. Feasibility hinges on feedstock: HEFA, the dominant pathway, is limited by used cooking oil and fats, so meeting the mandate requires rapid expansion of FT, alcohol-to-jet, and especially power-to-liquid e-fuels, which are costlier and earlier-stage. The mandate is plausible only with heavy investment and a tolerated 2–5× green premium; otherwise it risks supply shortfalls or reliance on constrained feedstocks.

2. It proceeded because IMO operates by member-state majority rather than unanimity, so the broad coalition (covering vessels above 5,000 GT, ~85% of fleet CO₂) could adopt it over US withdrawal. Shipping is also genuinely global and competitive, giving most flag and port states a shared interest in one rulebook rather than fragmented national measures. The lesson about climate political economy: durable multilateral action does not require the largest emitters' participation when the activity is internationally governed and a critical mass sees mutual advantage — a contrast to consensus-bound forums where one holdout can stall progress.

3. Shipping moves faster mainly because it has both a stronger policy framework and more tractable physics. The 2025 IMO agreement creates the first global shipping carbon price ($100–380/tCO₂ from 2027), layered on EU ETS maritime coverage, giving a clear economic signal aviation's weaker instruments (CORSIA's low-quality $15–25 credits) lack. Physically, ships tolerate heavier, lower-energy-density fuels, can slow-steam, and can store ammonia or methanol more easily than aircraft, opening multiple near-ready pathways. Aviation's severe energy-density constraint leaves SAF as the only drop-in option, scaling slowly against rising demand.

4. Toward methanol: the IMO penalty regime from 2027 ($100/tCO₂ with mitigation, $380 without) plus EU ETS maritime coverage raises the lifetime carbon cost of a conventional ship over its 20-year life; First Movers / cargo-owner demand for zero-emission shipping; and securing green-methanol supply early (as Maersk has). Toward conventional: lower upfront capital, mature global bunkering infrastructure, and uncertainty about green-methanol availability and price. The decisive variables are expected carbon-cost trajectory, fuel-supply security, and how much of the fleet's life falls under the tightening 2035/2040 IMO caps.

# Part 7: Policy architecture

The hard-to-abate sectors require **integrated policy** — not just carbon pricing, not just subsidies, not just standards, but combinations tailored to each sector.

## Green premiums

The **"green premium"** is the cost difference between low-carbon and conventional production. Current premiums:

- **Green steel**: 20–30% premium currently (down from 50%+ earlier in the decade as costs converge)
- **Green ammonia**: 60–150% premium
- **SAF**: 200–400% premium
- **Green methanol**: 100–300% premium
- **Green cement (alternative chemistry or CCS-coupled)**: 50–100% premium

Closing the green premium is the central economic challenge for hard-to-abate decarbonization. Several mechanisms:

**1. Carbon pricing on conventional production.** EU ETS prices at €75/tCO₂ make conventional steel ~€100–150/ton more expensive — partially closing the green-steel premium. CBAM extends this to imports.

**2. Subsidies for clean production.** The IRA provides ~$10–20 billion of hydrogen and CCS subsidies; the EU Innovation Fund and Hydrogen Bank provide significant support; UK CCS-CfD provides revenue stability.

**3. Mandates and procurement.** ReFuelEU Aviation, the UK SAF Mandate, public procurement requirements (Buy Clean California, EU Green Public Procurement).

**4. Market makers.** Frontier-type advance market commitments for CDR; analogous initiatives for green steel (SteelZero), green concrete (ConcreteZero), green ammonia (First Movers Coalition).

## The First Movers Coalition

A particularly important demand-side mechanism for hard-to-abate sectors. Launched at COP26 (2021), the **First Movers Coalition (FMC)** consists of major industrial buyers (Volvo, Mercedes, Apple, Microsoft, others) committing to procure low-carbon versions of specific commodities:

- **Green steel** purchase commitments
- **Low-carbon aluminum** purchase commitments
- **Sustainable aviation fuel** purchase commitments
- **Zero-emission trucking** commitments
- **Zero-emission shipping** commitments
- **Low-carbon cement and concrete** commitments

The FMC aggregates demand across major buyers, providing visibility to producers about future market for low-carbon products. As of 2025, FMC includes 60+ companies with $12+ billion in committed purchase agreements.

## CBAM as sectoral lever

CBAM applies specifically to hard-to-abate sectors — steel, cement, aluminum, fertilizers, hydrogen, electricity. This is not coincidental: these are the sectors most exposed to carbon leakage, and the sectors where decarbonization is hardest. CBAM provides a sectoral price signal that:
- Equalizes carbon costs across EU and non-EU producers
- Creates direct demand for low-carbon production in CBAM-exposed jurisdictions
- Generates revenue (small but growing) that can fund decarbonization

CBAM is the **most ambitious sectoral climate policy instrument** in the world today. Its success or failure over 2026–2030 will substantially shape global hard-to-abate decarbonization.

## Hub-based deployment

Many hard-to-abate decarbonization strategies are explicitly **hub-based** — clusters of industrial facilities sharing infrastructure for CCS, hydrogen, or clean electricity:

- **Northern Lights cluster** (Norway): cement (Brevik), CCS storage, multiple emitters
- **East Coast Cluster** (UK): industrial CCS network
- **Humber Industrial Cluster** (UK): hydrogen + CCS
- **Porthos/Aramis** (Netherlands): industrial CCS
- **HyNet North West** (UK): hydrogen production + storage + CCS
- **Various US hub awards** under DOE programs

The hub-based approach reduces per-ton costs through shared infrastructure and aligns hard-to-abate sectors around common infrastructure investments.

## The 2026–2030 sectoral outlook

Aggregating across sectors:

- **Cement**: Most positive near-term outlook. Brevik operational, CBAM creating demand, technology pathway clearer. Plausible 15–30% emissions reduction by 2030.
- **Steel**: More uncertain. 2025–2026 retrenchment is real. 5–15% emissions reduction by 2030 likely.
- **Chemicals**: Just beginning. Pilot-scale deployment. Modest impact by 2030, larger 2030–2040.
- **Aviation**: SAF scaling slowly. Emissions growth (from demand) likely outpaces decarbonization in absolute terms. Net emissions still rising in 2030.
- **Shipping**: IMO 2027 creates strong incentive. Methanol-fueled fleet expansion. 10–20% reduction by 2035 plausible.

The hard-to-abate sectors' aggregate contribution to global emissions in 2030 will likely be similar to 2025 levels, with growth in some sectors offset by reductions in others. The substantive decarbonization happens 2030–2050 — but the policy frameworks, infrastructure investments, and demand signals that enable that decarbonization are being established now.

## **Stop-and-check 12.D**

1. Green premiums are typically 20–400% across hard-to-abate sectors. What mix of mechanisms — carbon pricing, subsidies, mandates, procurement — is most effective for each sector?
2. CBAM covers the EU's imports of CBAM-listed goods but doesn't address the rest of the world's trade in the same goods. Is this a meaningful limit on CBAM's climate impact?
3. The First Movers Coalition is a voluntary buyer commitment. SteelZero and ConcreteZero are sector-specific commitments. How do these compare to mandatory procurement standards (Buy Clean California, EU Green Public Procurement)?
4. **Socratic prompt:** If you were a US policymaker in 2027 with the political room to enact one major hard-to-abate policy, would you push for (a) a Foreign Pollution Fee (CBAM analog), (b) Buy Clean for federal procurement, (c) tighter Section 45Q with sectoral specifications, or (d) sectoral standards (e.g., low-carbon cement requirements)? Defend a single choice.

**Answers**

1. No single mechanism fits all sectors; the right mix tracks each sector's premium, trade exposure, and maturity. Cement (regional, ~50–100% premium) responds best to procurement and sectoral standards plus CCS subsidy, since trade measures have little leverage. Steel (globally traded, ~20–30% premium) suits carbon pricing plus CBAM to neutralize leakage, reinforced by buyer commitments like SteelZero. SAF (200–400% premium) needs mandates such as ReFuelEU plus production credits. Green ammonia leans on subsidies and CBAM-driven export demand. The general rule: pricing where trade exposure is high, mandates/procurement where products are domestic, subsidies where premiums are largest.

2. It is a real but partial limit. CBAM only equalizes carbon costs on goods imported into the EU, so it does nothing about carbon-intensive steel, cement, or fertilizer traded among the rest of the world — the bulk of global volume. Its direct climate impact is therefore bounded: it protects EU producers from leakage and creates a low-carbon export incentive for sellers into Europe, but cannot decarbonize global production by itself. Its larger significance is as a demonstration and as a template — its climate weight grows only if it pushes exporters to clean up broadly and if other jurisdictions adopt analogous border measures.

3. They are complementary but operate differently. Voluntary buyer coalitions (FMC, SteelZero, ConcreteZero) aggregate demand and signal a future market to producers, de-risking first low-carbon lines — fast to launch and flexible, but non-binding and dependent on members honoring commitments, so volumes can fall short. Mandatory procurement standards (Buy Clean California, EU GPP) compel public buyers to purchase low-carbon products, creating durable, enforceable demand backed by government purchasing power, but are slower to enact and limited to the public-procurement share. In practice voluntary commitments seed early demand while mandatory standards lock in and scale it.

4. A defensible single choice is (a) a Foreign Pollution Fee. It simultaneously protects domestic producers from leakage, prices the carbon content of the most trade-exposed hard-to-abate imports (steel, aluminum, cement, fertilizers), and — unlike pure subsidy — generates revenue rather than spending it, which broadens its political coalition to include domestic industry. The decisive variables are political durability and trade exposure: a border fee aligns climate and competitiveness interests, whereas Buy Clean reaches only federal purchasing and sectoral standards face industry resistance. The trade-off is administrative complexity and trade-law friction, plus weaker leverage over largely domestic products like cement.

# Closing exercise

Three things to take away:

**1. The hard-to-abate sectors collectively produce roughly 35–40% of global emissions and are growing in relative importance** as power-sector emissions decline. The post-2030 climate fight largely lives here.

**2. Each sector requires a distinct policy mix.** Cement is most amenable to CCS-led approaches. Steel needs H₂-DRI + scrap recycling + CCS interim solutions. Aviation needs SAF + demand management. Shipping has the strongest framework after the 2025 IMO agreement. There is no single "decarbonize hard-to-abate" policy that works across all sectors.

**3. The 2025–2026 evidence is mixed.** Cement is moving (Brevik operational, others in pipeline). Steel is retrenching (European projects delayed). Aviation is scaling SAF but very slowly. Shipping has its framework in place but deployment is just beginning. The pace of hard-to-abate decarbonization will substantially determine whether 1.5–2°C goals remain achievable.

# What this chapter simplified

**1. The chemistry detail of each sector.** I gave the basic chemistry of steel, cement, and chemicals. The actual production processes involve many sub-steps, intermediates, and variations. Detailed metallurgical or chemical-engineering analysis is its own field.

**2. The specific economics of each sector.** I gave green premium ranges. The actual project economics depend on inputs (energy prices, feedstock costs, capital costs, financing terms), outputs (commodity prices, green premiums), policy support (subsidies, tax credits, mandates), and risk premiums. Each project has its own detailed financial model.

**3. The role of demand-side intervention.** I touched on this in aviation (modal shift, demand management) and shipping (slow steaming). The substantive role of demand reduction — using less cement, less steel, less aviation, less freight — is potentially significant and deserves more attention than I gave it.

**4. The labor and equity dimensions.** Hard-to-abate transitions involve workers, communities, and regions whose livelihoods depend on current industries. Just-transition planning, retraining, and regional economic development are essential components of climate policy. I gave brief mention; the full treatment is substantial.

**5. The specific national policy variations.** Different jurisdictions have different policy mixes for hard-to-abate sectors. I gave EU, US, UK, and a few others. The full landscape (China's industrial policy, India's PLI schemes, Japan's GX strategy, Brazil's various initiatives, Indonesia's industrial decarbonization plans, etc.) is far richer than I covered.

**6. The relationship to net-zero accounting.** Hard-to-abate sectors are where SBTi V2's "Ongoing Emissions Responsibility" framework matters most — these are the sectors that need to be retained (under net-zero) but addressed via removal credits. Chapter 13 picks this up.

# Glossary delta (Chapter 12)

- **ATJ (Alcohol-to-jet)** — SAF production pathway from ethanol/butanol fermentation.
- **BF-BOF (Blast Furnace - Basic Oxygen Furnace)** — Conventional steel production route using coal as both fuel and reductant. ~70% of global steel.
- **Black liquor** — Pulp and paper byproduct burned for energy; leading near-term BECCS opportunity.
- **Brevik Cement** — Heidelberg Materials' Norwegian cement plant with operational CCS since 2025; world's first commercial cement CCS.
- **Calcination** — Chemical decomposition of limestone to lime + CO₂. The fundamental process emission in cement.
- **CBAM** — Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (covered in Ch. 7). Applies to steel, cement, aluminum, fertilizers, hydrogen, electricity.
- **Clinker** — The main intermediate product in cement production. Higher clinker content = higher emissions.
- **Clinker-to-cement ratio** — Fraction of clinker in finished cement. Global average ~0.72; reduction lowers emissions.
- **CORSIA** — Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation (covered in Ch. 7, Ch. 8). ICAO compliance scheme.
- **DRI (Direct Reduced Iron / Sponge Iron)** — Iron produced by reducing iron ore with hydrogen or natural gas rather than coal-based blast furnace. Required for low-carbon steel via EAF.
- **EAF (Electric Arc Furnace)** — Steel production route using electricity to melt scrap or DRI. ~30% of global steel; near-zero emissions with clean electricity.
- **e-fuels** — Synthetic fuels produced from green hydrogen + captured CO₂. Power-to-Liquid is the primary production method.
- **First Movers Coalition (FMC)** — Cross-sector industrial buyer commitment to procure low-carbon products. Launched COP26 (2021).
- **FT (Fischer-Tropsch)** — SAF production pathway from biomass via gasification.
- **Green premium** — Cost difference between low-carbon and conventional production. 20–400% across hard-to-abate sectors.
- **Haber-Bosch process** — Industrial process for synthesizing ammonia from hydrogen and nitrogen. Energy- and CO₂-intensive when fed by natural-gas-derived hydrogen.
- **HEFA (Hydroprocessed Esters and Fatty Acids)** — SAF production pathway from used cooking oils, animal fats, plant oils. Currently dominant SAF method.
- **HYBRIT** — Swedish demonstration project (SSAB, LKAB, Vattenfall) for green steel production using green hydrogen.
- **IMO 2025 Agreement** — Landmark global shipping carbon-pricing framework agreed April 2025; entering force 2027. $100/tCO₂ with mitigation, $380/tCO₂ without.
- **Methanol** — Alternative marine fuel; can be produced green (from green H₂ + CO₂) or blue (from natural gas + CCS).
- **MOE (Molten Oxide Electrolysis)** — Pre-commercial alternative steel chemistry; direct electrolytic reduction without hydrogen.
- **ReFuelEU Aviation** — EU mandate requiring increasing SAF blend shares in aviation fuel. 2% by 2025, 6% by 2030, 70% by 2050.
- **SAF (Sustainable Aviation Fuel)** — Drop-in jet fuel from non-fossil feedstocks. Multiple production pathways approved.
- **SCMs (Supplementary Cementitious Materials)** — Materials replacing clinker in cement (fly ash, slag, calcined clay, natural pozzolans).
- **Stegra** (formerly H₂ Green Steel) — Swedish green-steel company; 2.5 Mt/yr capacity ramping in 2025–2026 at Boden facility.
- **SteelZero / ConcreteZero** — Climate Group-led commitments by buyers to procure low-carbon steel and concrete respectively.
