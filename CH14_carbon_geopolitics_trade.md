## Why this chapter exists

Climate policy is usually taught as if it were domestic — a question of what a government does inside its own borders. But carbon is the most globalized problem there is: the atmosphere is a single shared reservoir (Ch. 1), emissions anywhere affect everyone, and the goods whose production emits carbon are traded across borders constantly. The moment one country prices carbon and another doesn't, you have a *trade* problem, a *competitiveness* problem, and a *fairness* problem — all at once.

This chapter is about climate as **geopolitics**: the strategic interaction of the major powers and blocs over who pays, who produces, who leads, and who is protected. It is where the policy instruments of earlier chapters (compliance pricing, CBAM, the voluntary market, disclosure) collide with national interest, industrial strategy, and the brute facts of who controls the physical supply chains of the energy transition.

The framing question is one Chapter 6 set up and never resolved: the Paris Agreement deliberately abandoned the top-down, single-global-price dream in favor of bottom-up national pledges. That made Paris politically durable — but it guaranteed a fragmented world of *different carbon prices in different places*, which is precisely the condition that generates leakage, border adjustments, trade retaliation, and the scramble for the carbon "club." As of 2026 that fragmentation is sharpening, not resolving, with the world's largest historical emitter — the US — in active retreat from the entire architecture.

I'll organize this in seven parts: the fragmentation thesis; CBAM as a geopolitical weapon and the responses to it; the carbon-club idea; the physical-carbon chokepoint (China's dominance and the de-risking response); the petrostates; climate finance and the North–South cleavage; and the US retreat in a multipolar climate order.

# Part 1: The fragmentation thesis

## Why there is no global carbon price

Economists' first-best solution to climate change is conceptually simple: a single, uniform, global price on carbon, rising over time (Ch. 9). It would equalize marginal abatement costs everywhere, eliminate leakage by construction, and let the market find the cheapest reductions on the planet. It is also politically impossible, and understanding *why* is the foundation of carbon geopolitics.

A single global price requires either a world government that can impose it (there isn't one) or universal voluntary agreement on the price and the distribution of revenue (there isn't, and can't easily be). Countries differ enormously in income, historical responsibility, energy mix, development needs, and political tolerance for energy-price increases. A price that is trivial for Norway is regressive and growth-throttling for India. The **"common but differentiated responsibilities" (CBDR)** principle (Ch. 6) is the formal acknowledgment that the same obligation cannot fairly be imposed on all.

So the world got Paris instead: a bottom-up patchwork of nationally determined contributions, with each country setting its own ambition and instrument. The predictable consequence is **price fragmentation** — EU ETS allowances around €70–80, California in the $25–35 range, China's national ETS around $11–14, large swaths of the world at zero (Ch. 7, Ch. 9). The global *average* effective carbon price remains low (single-digit dollars per tonne when you weight all emissions), and roughly three-quarters of global emissions face no meaningful carbon price at all.

## What fragmentation produces

A fragmented price map generates three forces that drive everything in this chapter:

1. **Leakage and competitiveness pressure.** Carbon-intensive production migrates (or threatens to migrate) from high-price to low-price jurisdictions. This is the engine behind free allocation (Ch. 7) and behind border adjustments.
2. **Border adjustments and trade friction.** To defend a domestic price without conceding competitiveness, a jurisdiction extends the price to imports — CBAM (Part 2). That immediately becomes a trade dispute with everyone who exports to it.
3. **Coalition dynamics.** If a uniform global price is impossible, the second-best is a *club* of jurisdictions that price carbon similarly, link their systems, and collectively penalize non-members (Part 3).

The rest of the chapter is, in effect, a tour of how the major powers play these three forces against each other.

## **Stop-and-check 14.A**

1. State the first-best climate policy in one sentence, then give the two distinct reasons it is politically impossible.
2. Roughly three-quarters of global emissions face no meaningful carbon price. Why does that fact, more than any single high price, define the geopolitics of carbon?
3. CBDR was meant as a fairness principle. Explain how it simultaneously functions as a structural cause of price fragmentation.
4. **Socratic prompt:** If you could impose *either* a uniform global price of $50/tCO₂ *or* the current fragmented patchwork with a few high prices and much zero, which produces more total abatement this decade? Which is more politically durable? Are those the same answer?

**Answers**

1. The first-best is a single, uniform, global carbon price rising over time, which equalizes marginal abatement costs everywhere and eliminates leakage by construction. It is politically impossible for two distinct reasons: there is no world government able to impose it, and universal voluntary agreement is unreachable because countries differ so sharply in income, historical responsibility, energy mix, and tolerance for energy-price increases that no single price is simultaneously fair and acceptable to all — a price trivial for Norway is growth-throttling for India.

2. Because the geopolitics of carbon is driven by the *gap* between priced and unpriced production, not by the level of any one price. With roughly three-quarters of emissions facing no meaningful price, carbon-intensive activity can migrate to or stay in unpriced jurisdictions, generating leakage and competitiveness pressure — the forces behind free allocation, border adjustments, and clubs. A high price somewhere matters geopolitically only because so much of the world sits at zero; it is the differential that produces trade friction, not the absolute number.

3. CBDR holds that obligations should differ by historical responsibility and capability, which is a fairness claim. But "differentiated responsibilities" translated into policy means different countries adopt different ambition and different instruments — including, legitimately under CBDR, no carbon price at all for many developing economies. So the very principle meant to make the system fair also licenses and entrenches the divergence in carbon prices across jurisdictions. Fairness and fragmentation are, structurally, two descriptions of the same outcome.

4. They are likely not the same answer, which is the point. A uniform $50 price would almost certainly produce more total abatement this decade — it reaches the three-quarters of emissions now unpriced and finds cheap reductions globally. But the fragmented patchwork is far more politically durable precisely because it asks nothing uniform of anyone and respects CBDR; the uniform price is unenforceable and would fracture on distributional grounds. The honest conclusion: the higher-abatement option is the less durable one, and the durable option underdelivers — which is exactly why the world chose Paris and lives with the gap.

# Part 2: CBAM as a geopolitical instrument

## A recap, then the politics

Chapter 7 covered CBAM's mechanics: the EU charges importers of carbon-intensive goods (iron and steel, aluminum, cement, fertilizers, hydrogen, electricity) the equivalent of the EU ETS price on embedded emissions, phasing in from 2026 as free allocation phases out, reaching full coverage by 2034. The first official certificate price (~€75/tCO₂e, Q1 2026) makes it a live cost. Here the question is different: **what does CBAM do to the rest of the world, and how is the rest of the world responding?**

CBAM is the EU exporting its carbon price by leveraging access to the world's largest single market. It is the most concrete instance yet of a jurisdiction using *trade policy* to extend *climate policy* extraterritorially. That makes it simultaneously a climate instrument, an industrial-protection instrument, and a diplomatic provocation.

## The spectrum of responses

Trading partners have responded along a spectrum, and the response a country chooses reveals its strategic position:

**Adopt your own price (capture the revenue).** The cleanest response: if your exports will be charged the EU carbon price anyway, you may as well levy that price domestically and keep the revenue at home rather than handing it to Brussels. **Turkey, Ukraine, the Western Balkans**, and others — major exporters of CBAM-covered goods to the EU — have moved toward their own carbon-pricing systems substantially *because* of CBAM exposure. This is CBAM working as intended: it incentivizes the spread of carbon pricing.

**Protest as protectionism (the developing-country bloc).** **China, India, Brazil, South Africa** — the "BASIC" group — have attacked CBAM as green protectionism that violates both WTO non-discrimination principles and the UNFCCC's CBDR principle, arguing it imposes equivalent obligations on developing countries that bear little historical responsibility. India has been especially vocal, given its steel and aluminum exports, and has floated retaliatory measures and WTO challenge. The argument has real force: CBAM's compliance and verification burden falls hardest on producers in countries with the least administrative capacity.

**Build domestic systems while objecting (the hedge).** Several countries do both — denounce CBAM rhetorically while quietly building the carbon-pricing and emissions-verification infrastructure that will blunt its impact. India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme and Brazil's emerging ETS (Ch. 7) are partly CBAM hedges.

**The US ambivalence.** The US under Biden was cautiously sympathetic (a US carbon-border mechanism aligns with "buy American" industrial logic and there were bipartisan-ish proposals — the Foreign Pollution Fee, the Clean Competition Act). The US under Trump, having no domestic carbon price to extend and being broadly hostile to climate multilateralism, is a different matter: a US border-carbon measure, if it came, would more likely be framed as an anti-China trade tool than a climate instrument — pricing the carbon-intensity of imports as a tariff lever rather than to extend a (nonexistent) domestic price.

## The WTO question

Is CBAM legal under trade law? Genuinely contested. Proponents argue it is compatible because it is tied to a domestic environmental measure, applies symmetrically to the embedded carbon (not the country of origin), and can be defended under **GATT Article XX** environmental exceptions. Critics argue the practical effect is discriminatory and the verification asymmetry burdens developing-country exporters unfairly. No definitive ruling exists as of writing; a formal WTO challenge would be a landmark case for the entire field of climate-and-trade law.

## CBAM as precedent

Whatever its fate, CBAM has established that a major bloc *can* use border adjustments to project climate policy. The **UK** has its own CBAM in development; **Australia, Canada**, and others have examined the concept. The likely trajectory is a world of *multiple, partially incompatible* border-carbon regimes — which fragments trade further and raises the premium on harmonization, i.e., on clubs.

## **Stop-and-check 14.B**

1. Turkey is building a carbon price largely because of CBAM; India is fighting CBAM at the WTO. Both are large exporters to the EU. What explains the opposite responses?
2. CBAM is simultaneously a climate instrument, an industrial-protection instrument, and a diplomatic provocation. Can it be all three honestly, or does one purpose necessarily undermine the others?
3. A US "Foreign Pollution Fee" with no domestic US carbon price behind it — is that a carbon-border adjustment at all, or just a carbon-flavored tariff? Does the distinction matter?
4. **Socratic prompt:** From a developing country's perspective, CBAM says "price your carbon or we'll price it for you and keep the money." Is that coercion, a legitimate response to leakage, or both? What would a fair version look like?

**Answers**

1. Strategic position. Turkey, Ukraine, and the Western Balkans are export economies tightly integrated with the EU market and lack the leverage to resist, so the rational move is to adopt their own carbon price and keep the revenue at home rather than hand it to Brussels — CBAM working as intended. India is a large economy with strong CBDR-based fairness grievances, significant steel and aluminum exports, and the diplomatic weight to contest; it sees CBAM as green protectionism imposing equivalent obligations on a country with little historical responsibility, so fighting it (including at the WTO) is the calculated response. Different exposure and different leverage yield opposite strategies.

2. It can be all three at once, but the purposes are in tension and the honesty lies in admitting that. As a climate instrument it extends a domestic carbon price to imports and incentivizes carbon pricing abroad; as industrial protection it shields EU producers from competitiveness loss; as a provocation it projects EU policy extraterritorially via market access. The danger is that the protectionist motive can quietly dominate the climate one — the same border charge serves both — which is why critics read it as protectionism wearing a climate label. It is honestly all three only if the design (symmetric treatment of embedded carbon, no excess shielding) keeps the climate logic genuinely binding.

3. The distinction matters and largely turns on whether there is a domestic price to extend. A genuine carbon-border adjustment exists to level the playing field for a domestic carbon price, charging imports the same embedded-carbon cost domestic producers already pay. A US "Foreign Pollution Fee" with no underlying US carbon price has nothing to equalize — it is a tariff calibrated to carbon intensity, functioning as a trade lever (largely against China) rather than as the complement to a domestic climate measure. That difference is decisive for WTO defensibility (GATT Article XX leans on the link to a domestic environmental measure) and for whether the instrument actually drives decarbonization or just redirects trade.

4. It is plausibly both, and the framing depends on the counterfactual. It is a legitimate response to leakage in that the EU cannot maintain a real carbon price while importing uncosted carbon-intensive goods, and it does incentivize the spread of pricing. It is coercive in that it imposes a price set in Brussels on producers in countries that bear little historical responsibility and have the least administrative capacity to comply. A fairer version would recycle CBAM revenue toward decarbonization and capacity-building in affected developing countries, phase in obligations along CBDR lines, recognize equivalent domestic policies generously, and ease the verification burden — converting a unilateral charge into something closer to shared transition finance.

# Part 3: The carbon club

## Nordhaus's idea

If a uniform global price is unreachable through universal consent, the economist **William Nordhaus** proposed a different mechanism: a **"climate club."** Members agree to a common minimum carbon price (or equivalent policy) and impose a uniform tariff on imports from non-members. The tariff does two things: it protects members from leakage, and — crucially — it changes the incentive to *join*. Outside the club you face the tariff on all your exports to members; inside, you don't. Nordhaus showed that with a high enough penalty tariff, a stable, large club can form and sustain a meaningful carbon price where voluntary cooperation fails. The club converts a free-rider problem into a participation incentive.

## The G7 Climate Club and steel arrangements

The idea has migrated from theory toward practice. The **G7 Climate Club**, launched in 2022 and formally established in 2023, is the most prominent attempt — though in practice it has been a soft coordination forum (sharing methodologies, comparing policies, supporting decarbonization in emerging economies) rather than Nordhaus's hard tariff-backed club. The harder-edged manifestation is sector-specific: proposals for a **"green steel club"** or a **Global Arrangement on Sustainable Steel and Aluminium** (a US–EU negotiation) that would create preferential trade among low-carbon-steel producers and common tariffs against high-carbon (read: largely Chinese) steel. These negotiations have repeatedly stalled — partly on the US side's reluctance to accept a carbon-based (rather than overcapacity-based) framing, partly on the EU's CBAM-compatibility concerns.

## Why clubs are hard

The gap between Nordhaus's elegant theory and the messy reality is instructive:

- **Defining membership criteria is contentious.** A common carbon price? A common outcome (emissions intensity)? Equivalent policies broadly construed? Each definition advantages different members and excludes different non-members.
- **The penalty tariff is a trade war.** A meaningful penalty tariff against non-members is, legally and politically, indistinguishable from a trade war — and invites retaliation, WTO challenge, and supply-chain disruption.
- **The biggest emitter is the hardest case.** A club that excludes China excludes ~30% of global emissions and most of the clean-tech supply chain; a club that includes China requires China to accept the club's terms, which it has little incentive to do.
- **CBAM is a unilateral club-of-one.** In practice the EU built CBAM rather than waiting for a negotiated club — which is faster but forfeits the legitimacy and reciprocity that a true club would provide.

The realistic near-term future is therefore not Nordhaus's clean club but a messier ecosystem of **overlapping, partially linked regimes** — EU ETS + CBAM, a possible EU–UK link (Ch. 7), bilateral recognition deals, and sectoral arrangements — with China and much of the Global South outside, and the US idiosyncratically detached.

## **Stop-and-check 14.C**

1. Explain how Nordhaus's penalty tariff converts a free-rider problem into a participation incentive. Why is the *tariff*, not the carbon price itself, the clever part?
2. The G7 Climate Club became a soft coordination forum rather than a hard tariff-backed club. Why did the hard version prove so hard to build?
3. Is CBAM best understood as a step *toward* a carbon club or a substitute that *forecloses* one? Argue it both ways.
4. **Socratic prompt:** Design the membership rule for a carbon club that you think could actually attract India and Brazil without collapsing into meaninglessness. What does your rule reward, and what does it sacrifice?

**Answers**

1. The penalty tariff flips the payoff of staying outside. Without it, every country prefers to free-ride — let others price carbon and bear the cost while enjoying the shared climate benefit. The uniform tariff on imports from non-members means that staying outside now carries a concrete, immediate cost (your exports to club members are taxed), while joining removes it. So the tariff, not the carbon price, is the clever part: the carbon price is the obligation members accept, but the tariff is the mechanism that makes accepting it individually rational, converting a collective-action failure into a self-reinforcing incentive to join.

2. Because the hard version requires a meaningful penalty tariff against non-members, which is legally and politically almost indistinguishable from a trade war — inviting retaliation, WTO challenge, and supply-chain disruption. It also founders on defining membership (common price? common emissions intensity? equivalent policies?), since each definition advantages some members and excludes others. And the biggest emitter is the hardest case: a club excluding China excludes ~30% of emissions and most of the clean-tech supply chain, while including China requires terms China has little incentive to accept. The G7 therefore retreated to soft coordination, which is achievable precisely because it asks for nothing enforceable.

3. Both readings are defensible. Toward a club: CBAM establishes the penalty-tariff machinery and the precedent that a major bloc can charge embedded carbon at the border, and by pushing partners (Turkey, Ukraine) to adopt their own prices it builds the common-pricing base a club needs — a nucleus others can link to. Forecloses one: by acting unilaterally rather than negotiating, the EU forfeited the reciprocity and shared governance that give a true club legitimacy, hardened the BASIC bloc against the whole concept, and created a fait accompli that others must react to rather than co-design — making a negotiated club harder, not easier.

4. A workable rule would reward *direction and equivalent effort* rather than a single common price: membership open to any country implementing climate policies of broadly equivalent ambition (a carbon price, a regulatory standard, or a credible emissions-intensity trajectory), assessed against a development-adjusted benchmark consistent with CBDR, with revenue recycling and capacity support for lower-income members. This rewards genuine decarbonization effort and brings India and Brazil in without demanding a price they reject as unfair. What it sacrifices is rigor and comparability — "equivalent effort" is fuzzy, gameable, and harder to police than a hard price, risking a club broad enough to include everyone but loose enough to mean little.

# Part 4: The physical-carbon chokepoint

## Carbon geopolitics is also materials geopolitics

There is a second, less obvious geopolitical layer to carbon — and it runs through the *physical* carbon and clean-energy supply chains introduced in Chapter 2. The energy transition is extraordinarily materials-intensive: it runs on graphite and anodes (batteries), polysilicon (solar), rare earths (wind turbines, motors), lithium, cobalt, nickel, and copper. And the processing of these materials — not just their mining, but the refining and manufacturing — is concentrated in China to a degree that has no precedent in the fossil era.

The numbers from Chapter 2 bear repeating in this context: China controls a dominant share of natural graphite production and the large majority of **anode-grade graphite processing**, the overwhelming majority of **solar polysilicon, wafers, and cells**, and most rare-earth processing. This is structurally different from oil: oil reserves and production are geographically dispersed and the technology to use it is widely held, whereas the transition's critical-material *processing* is a genuine chokepoint held by a single state.

## The strategic asymmetry

This creates a profound asymmetry in carbon geopolitics. The EU and US can price carbon, build CBAMs, and form clubs — but the hardware of the transition they are trying to accelerate is largely made in, or dependent on inputs from, China. A faster transition in the West means, in the near term, *more* dependence on Chinese supply chains, not less. China's dominance was built through decades of industrial policy, cheap power (much of it coal), and scale — and it gives Beijing a lever that complicates every Western climate-trade move. Penalize Chinese steel via a club, and you may raise the cost of the clean-energy buildout that needs that steel.

## The de-risking response

The Western response since ~2022 has been **"de-risking"** (the EU's preferred term) or **reshoring/friend-shoring** (the US framing) — an explicit industrial-policy push to build non-Chinese supply chains:

- **The US Inflation Reduction Act (IRA, 2022)** tied clean-energy tax credits to domestic content and "foreign entity of concern" exclusions, explicitly to pull battery and solar manufacturing out of China and into the US and allied countries. (Note: the IRA's incentives have themselves been gutted by the 2025 OBBBA — Ch. 13 — which complicates the US de-risking story considerably.)
- **The EU Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA, 2024)** set targets for domestic extraction, processing, and recycling of strategic materials, and supply-diversification benchmarks to cap reliance on any single third country.
- **The EU Net-Zero Industry Act** aimed to onshore clean-tech manufacturing capacity.

The honest assessment: building non-Chinese processing capacity is slow, expensive, and years behind. De-risking reduces *future* dependence at the margin; it does not change the near-term reality that the transition rides on Chinese supply chains. And the 2025 US retreat from IRA incentives undercuts the most significant Western reshoring effort just as it was beginning to attract investment.

## The graphite link

This is where Chapter 2 and Chapter 14 meet most directly. The EV battery requires more carbon — as graphite — by mass than it requires lithium. China's >65% control of natural graphite and ~80%+ of anode processing means that the single most carbon-flavored material in the transition is also its sharpest geopolitical chokepoint. Carbon, the climate liability, and carbon, the strategic material, turn out to be the same story viewed from two angles.

## **Stop-and-check 14.D**

1. Why is the concentration of transition-mineral *processing* (not just mining) in one country structurally different from, and arguably more powerful than, oil-reserve concentration?
2. Explain the asymmetry: how can pricing carbon in the West increase short-term dependence on Chinese supply chains?
3. The IRA was the West's biggest de-risking instrument; OBBBA gutted it in 2025. What does that do to the credibility of US friend-shoring?
4. **Socratic prompt:** China's clean-tech dominance was built partly on cheap coal power. Is China's manufacturing therefore "high-carbon clean tech," and does that change how the West should think about importing it — block it on carbon grounds, or accept higher embedded carbon now to decarbonize faster later?

**Answers**

1. Oil reserves and production are geographically dispersed, and the technology to refine and use oil is widely held, so no single state can durably choke the system. Transition-mineral *processing* — refining and manufacturing graphite anodes, polysilicon, rare earths — is concentrated in China to a degree with no fossil-era precedent. Processing is the harder bottleneck than mining: it embeds decades of accumulated industrial capability, scale, and cheap power, and cannot be relocated quickly. A single state holding the processing chokepoint therefore has a structural lever over the transition that no oil producer ever held over the fossil economy.

2. The transition's hardware — batteries, solar, motors — is overwhelmingly made in or dependent on inputs from China. So when the West prices carbon to accelerate its transition, it accelerates demand for exactly those Chinese-made goods, deepening near-term reliance on Chinese supply chains rather than reducing it. The faster the West pushes, the more clean hardware it must import before domestic capacity exists. Penalizing Chinese steel or components via a club can even raise the cost of the clean-energy buildout that needs them — so the climate ambition and the dependence move in the same direction in the short run.

3. It undercuts US friend-shoring credibility sharply. The IRA's domestic-content and foreign-entity-of-concern rules were the West's most significant attempt to pull battery and solar manufacturing out of China, and they had begun attracting investment. OBBBA's gutting of those incentives in 2025 removes the financial basis just as projects were committing, signaling to investors that US industrial-policy support is politically reversible and therefore unreliable for the long-lived, capital-intensive bets reshoring requires. De-risking depends on policy durability; demonstrating that the central instrument can be repealed within three years makes future commitments harder to trust.

4. There is real force to "high-carbon clean tech": coal-powered manufacturing means Chinese panels and batteries carry meaningful embedded carbon, so importing them is not carbon-free. But blocking them on carbon grounds usually fails the lifecycle test — a coal-made solar panel pays back its embedded carbon many times over by displacing fossil generation across its operating life, so accepting higher embedded carbon now to decarbonize the energy system faster is generally the better climate trade. The legitimate uses of the embedded-carbon argument are at the margin (rewarding cleaner producers via CBAM-style differentiation) and as cover for industrial/strategic motives that are really about dependence, not carbon — which should be argued honestly on their own terms.

# Part 5: The petrostates

## The asset-stranding problem at national scale

Chapter 4 introduced stranded assets and unburnable carbon at the level of firms and reserves. At the level of *states*, the same logic produces an existential strategic problem for the petrostates: their national wealth, fiscal base, and geopolitical weight are built on a commodity the world has formally agreed to wind down. How they respond shapes global oil supply, climate diplomacy, and the speed of the transition.

The strategies diverge sharply:

**The Gulf diversifiers (Saudi Arabia, UAE).** The dominant strategy is to **monetize reserves fast while diversifying the economy** — pump as much as remains profitable before peak demand, and plow the proceeds into sovereign wealth, non-oil industry, tourism, finance, and (increasingly) clean energy, hydrogen, and AI/data-center infrastructure. The UAE hosted COP28 (2023) and positions itself as a transition broker; Saudi Arabia invests in both massive solar and continued oil expansion. The implicit bet: oil demand stays high enough, long enough, to fund the pivot — and a faster transition elsewhere is not in their interest.

**Norway's two-track.** Norway is the paradox: a major oil and gas exporter that is also a climate leader at home (near-fully decarbonized electricity, world-leading EV adoption, the Northern Lights CCS project — Ch. 3, Ch. 10) funded substantially *by* its petroleum wealth. It exports hydrocarbons while decarbonizing domestically and investing oil revenue (via the world's largest sovereign wealth fund) partly into the transition. Critics call it hypocrisy; defenders call it a pragmatic funding model. It is the clearest example of the "produce and transition simultaneously" path.

**Russia.** Sanctions, the Ukraine war, and redirection of energy exports toward China and India have reshaped Russia's position; its long-run transition strategy is largely subordinated to near-term geopolitical survival and revenue.

## The demand-peak question

Everything for the petrostates hinges on *when oil demand peaks* and how steeply it falls after. The IEA's net-zero scenarios imply a near-term peak and sharp decline; the producers' own forecasts (and OPEC's) imply a later peak and a long plateau. This is not a technical disagreement so much as a strategic one — the forecast each party publishes is partly an attempt to shape the investment and policy behavior that will make it true. A widely believed "early peak" discourages investment in new supply (hastening the peak); a widely believed "long plateau" encourages it (delaying the peak). The petrostates have a strong interest in the long-plateau narrative.

## **Stop-and-check 14.E**

1. Why is asset stranding an *existential* problem for a petrostate in a way it is not for a diversified economy with some oil exposure?
2. Norway exports hydrocarbons while leading on domestic decarbonization and funding the transition from oil revenue. Is this coherent or hypocritical? Make the strongest case for "coherent."
3. The Gulf diversifiers are betting oil demand stays high long enough to fund their pivot. What would falsify that bet, and how fast would they know?
4. **Socratic prompt:** Oil-demand forecasts are partly self-fulfilling. If you ran the IEA, knowing your "early peak" forecast discourages new supply investment, would you publish it as a forecast, a target, or a scenario? What's the responsibility of the forecaster when the forecast shapes the outcome?

**Answers**

1. For a petrostate, the entire national wealth, fiscal base, and geopolitical weight rest on a single commodity the world has formally agreed to wind down — so stranding is not a portfolio loss but a threat to the state's revenue, social contract, and power simultaneously, with no diversified base to absorb it. A diversified economy with some oil exposure loses one sector's value among many and can reallocate; the petrostate has, in effect, its whole balance sheet concentrated in the asset being phased out. That concentration is what makes the stranding existential rather than merely costly.

2. The strongest "coherent" case: Norway separates production from consumption responsibility. It decarbonizes what it controls domestically — near-fully clean electricity, world-leading EV adoption, the Northern Lights CCS project — and channels its petroleum wealth, via the world's largest sovereign wealth fund, partly into the transition. As long as global oil demand exists, someone will supply it; Norway argues it may as well be a relatively low-emission-intensity producer that recycles the proceeds into decarbonization rather than ceding the market to less responsible producers. On this view "produce and transition simultaneously" is a pragmatic funding model, not hypocrisy — the oil revenue is financing the exit.

3. The bet is falsified by an early, steep demand peak — the IEA net-zero trajectory rather than the long-plateau OPEC view. They would get warning signals well before the wealth runs out: sustained declines in oil demand growth, accelerating EV penetration eroding transport demand, falling long-dated oil prices and forward curves, and capital fleeing new long-cycle projects. But because demand forecasts are partly self-fulfilling and the producers have an interest in the long-plateau narrative, the danger is recognizing the peak too late — the signals are visible in markets within a few years, but acting on them requires abandoning a narrative the petrostate's whole strategy depends on.

4. The responsible answer is to publish it explicitly as a *scenario*, not a forecast or a target. Labeling it a forecast implies a neutral prediction it cannot be, given that publication shapes the investment behavior that determines the outcome; labeling it a target smuggles in advocacy the IEA is not mandated to do. A scenario honestly frames it as "if these policies and choices hold, this is where supply and demand go," disclosing the assumptions and the feedback. The forecaster's responsibility when the forecast moves the world is transparency about that reflexivity — present the possibility space, name the assumptions, and let actors see that their choices, not fate, close the gap.

# Part 6: Climate finance and the North–South cleavage

## The deepest fault line

Underneath every climate negotiation is a distributional question Chapter 6 named but did not resolve: the countries that caused the bulk of historical emissions (the industrialized North) are not the countries that will suffer the worst impacts or bear the highest adaptation costs (much of the global South). **Climate finance** — the transfer of resources from North to South for mitigation and adaptation — is the mechanism meant to address this, and it is the single most contentious thread in the entire UN process.

## The $100B pledge and its credibility cost

At Copenhagen (2009) developed countries pledged to mobilize **$100 billion per year by 2020** for developing-country climate action. The target was met late (around 2022 by most accounting), was widely seen as inadequate in scale, and — because of how "mobilized" finance is counted (loans vs. grants, re-labeled development aid, leveraged private capital) — became a symbol of broken trust. The credibility damage from the late and contested $100B delivery shadows every subsequent negotiation.

## Loss and damage

A distinct and harder category emerged: **loss and damage** — compensation for climate harms that are *beyond* adaptation (the destroyed island, the unlivable region), which the South had sought for decades and the North long resisted for fear of unbounded liability. A breakthrough came at **COP27 (2022)** with agreement to create a fund, **operationalized at COP28 (2023)** with initial (modest) pledges. The fund exists; whether it will be filled at meaningful scale is unresolved.

## The NCQG: $300 billion, and the $1.3 trillion ambition

The $100B goal expired in 2025 and had to be replaced. At **COP29 in Baku (November 2024)**, after bitter negotiation, countries agreed a **New Collective Quantified Goal (NCQG)**: developed countries to *take the lead in mobilizing* at least **$300 billion per year by 2035** for developing countries — a tripling of the old goal — within a broader call for **$1.3 trillion per year by 2035** from all sources (public, private, multilateral). Developing countries widely regarded the $300B figure as far too low relative to need (estimates of developing-country needs run into the trillions), and the "mobilize" framing reopened all the old accounting disputes. The gap between the $300B floor and the $1.3T aspiration is the defining tension.

## COP30 Belém: the "stabilization" package

The most recent waypoint is **COP30 in Belém, Brazil (concluding 22 November 2025)**, which adopted the **"Belém Package"** — 195 parties, ~29 consensus decisions. Its headline elements: a push to operationalize the **"Baku to Belém Roadmap to 1.3T"** (mobilizing $1.3 trillion/year by 2035), a commitment to **triple adaptation finance by 2035**, a new **just-transition mechanism**, and a set of voluntary indicators for the **Global Goal on Adaptation**. Over 120 countries had submitted new or updated NDCs.

Its most-noted *limitation*: despite the Glasgow (COP26) and Dubai (COP28) language on transitioning away from fossil fuels, Belém produced **no binding commitment to a fossil-fuel phaseout** in the core text. Instead the Brazilian Presidency launched, on its own initiative, two voluntary "roadmaps" — including one toward a fossil-fuel-free economy. Commentators broadly characterized Belém as a **"stabilization"** outcome: it held the architecture together and advanced finance and adaptation, but fell short of the mitigation ambition many sought — and it did so against the backdrop of US absence and rising geopolitical strain.

## Article 6 as a finance channel

One thread connects this to Chapters 7 and 8: the Paris **Article 6** mechanisms (cooperative approaches and the UNFCCC-supervised crediting mechanism) are increasingly a *finance* channel, letting wealthier countries and corporates pay for emissions reductions in developing countries and (for sovereigns) count them toward NDCs. Done well, Article 6 directs capital South and helps meet the $1.3T ambition; done badly, it recreates every integrity problem of the voluntary market at sovereign scale (Ch. 8). Its operationalization is one of the more consequential and least-settled pieces of the finance picture.

## **Stop-and-check 14.F**

1. Why did *how* the $100B was counted (loans vs. grants vs. re-labeled aid) do as much damage to trust as the fact that it was met late?
2. Distinguish adaptation finance from loss-and-damage finance. Why was the North so much more resistant to the latter?
3. The NCQG sets a $300B floor inside a $1.3T aspiration. Whose interests does each number serve, and why was the gap left unresolved at Baku?
4. **Socratic prompt:** COP30 advanced finance and adaptation but produced no binding fossil-fuel phaseout. Is a "stabilization" COP a success (it held the system together) or a failure (it dodged the core question)? What would you need to know about the counterfactual to decide?

**Answers**

1. Because the counting determined whether the pledge was honored in substance, not just in headline. The $100B was met late (around 2022) and largely through loans rather than grants, re-labeled development aid, and leveraged private capital — so even the figure that was "delivered" did not represent the new, additional, grant-based transfer the South understood it to be. That gap between the announced number and its real composition signaled bad faith, doing as much damage to trust as the delay, because it suggested the North was meeting the letter while evading the spirit — a credibility wound that shadows every subsequent negotiation, including the NCQG's contested "mobilize" framing.

2. Adaptation finance funds measures that reduce harm from climate impacts a country can still adapt to — sea walls, drought-resistant crops, resilient infrastructure. Loss-and-damage finance addresses harms *beyond* adaptation: the destroyed island, the region rendered unlivable, losses no defensive investment can prevent. The North resisted loss and damage far harder because it edges toward *compensation* for past emissions, implying open-ended legal liability for harms the North caused — a potentially unbounded obligation. Adaptation framing keeps the money as forward-looking assistance; loss-and-damage framing risks acknowledging responsibility, which is why agreement (COP27) and operationalization (COP28) came so late and at modest scale.

3. The $300B floor serves the developed countries: it is a firm, bounded, politically deliverable commitment they "take the lead in mobilizing," limiting their exposure. The $1.3T aspiration serves the developing countries: it acknowledges that real needs run into the trillions and keeps the larger figure formally in the text as a goal for "all sources." The gap was left unresolved at Baku because it could not be closed — the North would not commit to trillions in public finance, and the South would not accept $300B as adequate, so the compromise was a low binding floor wrapped in a high non-binding aspiration, papering over a genuine distributional standoff rather than settling it.

4. It depends on the counterfactual, which is what you would need to know to judge. If the realistic alternative to Belém was collapse — a fractured process, withdrawn finance, abandoned architecture against the backdrop of US absence — then holding 195 parties together, advancing the $1.3T roadmap, and tripling adaptation finance is a genuine success of keeping the system alive to deliver later. If the alternative was an achievable but missed binding fossil-fuel phaseout, then "stabilization" is a failure that dodged the core mitigation question behind voluntary roadmaps. To decide, you would need to know whether a stronger text was actually attainable given the votes in the room, or whether pushing for it would have broken consensus and produced no agreement at all.

# Part 7: The US retreat and a multipolar climate order

## The American reversal

The defining geopolitical fact of the mid-2020s is the **US federal retreat** from climate policy and multilateralism. The sequence (current as of writing):

- **Paris withdrawal**, again — executive action in January 2025; the withdrawal became **effective 27 January 2026**, removing the world's largest historical emitter and largest economy from the central agreement for the second time in a decade.
- **OBBBA (July 2025)** — repeal or accelerated sunset of most IRA clean-energy tax credits (Ch. 13), gutting the largest climate-investment program in US history barely three years after enactment.
- **The SEC climate rule's collapse** and a broad rollback of federal climate regulation and the methane fee.

The whipsaw — Paris in, Paris out, Paris in, Paris out; IRA enacted, IRA gutted — is itself the story. **Policy instability** is now a defining feature of the largest economy's climate posture, and it imposes a discount on every long-lived investment that depends on US federal policy.

## Who fills the vacuum

The retreat does not stop global climate policy; it redistributes leadership:

- **The EU** remains the standard-setter by regulation (ETS, CBAM, CSRD), though weakened at home by the competitiveness backlash (the CSRD Omnibus, Ch. 13) and by the loss of a transatlantic partner.
- **China** is the paradox of the era: the largest emitter and builder of new coal, *and* the dominant manufacturer and deployer of clean technology, installing more solar and wind than the rest of the world combined and exporting cheap clean-tech globally. Whether China "leads" on climate depends entirely on whether you measure by emissions or by clean-energy buildout — it does both, at scale, simultaneously. With the US absent, China gains diplomatic space to position itself as the more reliable partner to the Global South.
- **US subnational actors** — California (Ch. 7, Ch. 13), the US Climate Alliance of states, and many cities and corporations — continue to pursue climate policy despite the federal retreat, much as state compliance markets survived the federal absence (Ch. 7). The "we're still in" coalition matters, but cannot substitute for federal scale or international standing.
- **Brazil, the COP30 host**, and other middle powers, are increasingly active brokers.

## The multipolar climate order

The net result is a **multipolar, fragmented climate order**: no single hegemon driving the agenda, an EU constrained by its own competitiveness politics, a China ascendant in clean-tech but unwilling to lead on emissions cuts, a US in active retreat, and a Global South demanding finance it is unlikely to receive at the scale it needs. This is the world the rest of the book's instruments — carbon prices, CBAMs, credits, disclosure — actually operate in. None of them assumes this world; all of them must survive it.

The honest synthesis: the *physics* of carbon demands global cooperation, and the *politics* of carbon is fragmenting precisely when cooperation is most needed. That tension — not any single policy — is the defining condition of carbon geopolitics, and the bridge into the book's final chapter on where this all goes.

## **Stop-and-check 14.G**

1. "Policy instability is now a defining feature of US climate posture." Why is instability arguably *worse* for long-lived clean-energy investment than consistent low ambition would be?
2. Is China a climate leader or a climate laggard? Build the strongest case for each using the same set of facts.
3. US subnational actors kept compliance markets and disclosure alive through the federal retreat. What can subnational action substitute for, and what can it never substitute for?
4. **Socratic prompt:** In a multipolar order with no hegemon, what is the most plausible mechanism by which global emissions actually fall — a club, a technology cost-collapse, Chinese clean-tech flooding the world, or something else? Defend your pick.

**Answers**

1. Clean-energy investment is long-lived and capital-intensive — assets paid back over decades — so what investors fear most is uncertainty about the policy environment over that horizon. Consistent low ambition is at least predictable: you can price it in and still build the projects whose economics survive it. Whiplash policy — Paris in, Paris out; IRA enacted, IRA gutted — cannot be priced, because the rules that determine an investment's return might reverse before it pays back. That unpredictability imposes a risk premium (or simply deters capital) on every long-lived clean-energy bet that depends on US federal policy, which is arguably more corrosive than a stable, modest commitment.

2. Leader, by clean-tech buildout: China installs more solar and wind than the rest of the world combined, dominates manufacturing of panels, batteries, and EVs, and floods the world with cheap clean technology that decarbonizes others — without it, the global cost collapse driving the transition would not exist. Laggard, by emissions: it is the largest emitter and the biggest builder of new coal capacity, unwilling to commit to near-term absolute cuts. The same facts support both verdicts because China is doing both at scale simultaneously — the answer depends entirely on whether you measure climate leadership by emissions trajectory or by clean-energy deployment.

3. Subnational action can substitute for federal *policy continuity* — California and the US Climate Alliance kept compliance markets and disclosure mandates (SB 253) alive through the federal retreat, sustaining real regulation, carbon pricing, and a "we're still in" signal much as state markets survived earlier federal absence. What it can never substitute for is federal *scale and international standing*: states cannot deliver national-level emissions cuts across non-participating states, cannot rejoin Paris or negotiate as a party, cannot command federal fiscal and regulatory reach, and cannot restore the diplomatic weight of the United States in the multilateral process. Subnational actors hold the floor; they cannot occupy the seat.

4. The most plausible mechanism is the technology cost-collapse, channeled largely through cheap Chinese clean-tech flooding the world — and the two are really one story. In a multipolar order with no hegemon to enforce cooperation and clubs proving hard to build, emissions are unlikely to fall because countries agree to make them fall; they fall when clean options become cheaper than fossil ones, so self-interest does the work treaties cannot. China's overcapacity in solar, batteries, and EVs is the engine pushing those costs down globally. The defense: this mechanism does not require cooperation, which is exactly the resource the fragmenting order lacks — it runs on economics, which the politics cannot easily reverse. Its limits are hard-to-abate sectors and firm power, where cost collapse has not yet arrived.

# Closing exercise

Three things to take away:

**1. Fragmentation is the master fact.** There is no global carbon price and won't be; the world is a patchwork of different prices, regimes, and ambitions. Every instrument in this book — pricing, CBAM, credits, disclosure — is a response to, or a symptom of, that fragmentation. The geopolitics is the management of fragmentation.

**2. Carbon geopolitics is also materials geopolitics.** China's dominance of the physical-carbon and clean-tech value chain (graphite, anodes, polysilicon) means the West cannot accelerate the transition without, in the near term, deepening dependence on a strategic rival. De-risking is real but slow, and the 2025 US retreat undercut it. The climate liability and the strategic material turn out to be the same carbon.

**3. The physics demands cooperation; the politics is fragmenting.** The defining tension of the mid-2020s is that global cooperation is most needed exactly as it is breaking down — the US in retreat, the EU constrained, China ambivalent, the South under-financed. The COP process holds the architecture together ("stabilization") but cannot, on current trajectory, deliver the ambition the physics requires.

# What this chapter simplified

**1. The detail of trade law.** The WTO compatibility of CBAM, the GATT Article XX exceptions, the subsidy disciplines that constrain IRA-style industrial policy — each is a specialist legal field. I gave the strategic shape, not the case law.

**2. The diversity of the "Global South."** I used "South" and "developing countries" as if they were a bloc. They are not — a large emerging economy (India, Brazil), a Gulf petrostate, a small island state facing existential sea-level rise, and a least-developed country have sharply divergent interests in every negotiation. The BASIC group, AOSIS, the LDC group, and the Arab group often pull in different directions.

**3. The China clean-tech story.** China's simultaneous role as largest emitter, biggest coal builder, and dominant clean-tech manufacturer and deployer deserves a chapter of its own. I compressed it severely.

**4. The military and security dimension.** Climate as a "threat multiplier," climate migration, resource conflict, and the security implications of the transition (and of solar geoengineering, Ch. 15) are a large literature I only gestured at.

**5. The currency and capital-flows dimension.** The role of the dollar in oil trade, the financing of the transition in developing economies, debt-for-climate swaps, and the multilateral development banks' reform agenda are central to climate finance and got only the briefest mention.

# Glossary delta (Chapter 14)

- **BASIC group** — Brazil, South Africa, India, China; a negotiating bloc of large emerging economies, prominent in CBAM and equity disputes.
- **Belém Package** — The COP30 (2025) outcome: ~29 consensus decisions advancing the $1.3T finance roadmap, tripling adaptation finance, a just-transition mechanism, and adaptation indicators — but no binding fossil-fuel phaseout. Widely called a "stabilization" outcome.
- **Carbon club** — Nordhaus's proposal: members adopt a common minimum carbon price and impose a uniform penalty tariff on non-members, converting free-riding into an incentive to join.
- **CBDR (Common But Differentiated Responsibilities)** — UNFCCC principle that climate obligations differ by historical responsibility and capability; a fairness principle that also structurally drives price fragmentation.
- **CRMA (Critical Raw Materials Act)** — EU 2024 law setting extraction/processing/recycling targets and supplier-diversification benchmarks for strategic minerals; a de-risking instrument.
- **De-risking** — The EU/Western strategy of reducing (not eliminating) dependence on Chinese supply chains via industrial policy; US framing: reshoring/friend-shoring.
- **G7 Climate Club** — Forum established 2023 to coordinate climate policy among major economies; in practice soft coordination rather than a tariff-backed Nordhaus club.
- **Loss and damage** — Compensation for climate harms beyond adaptation; a dedicated fund was agreed at COP27 (2022) and operationalized at COP28 (2023).
- **NCQG (New Collective Quantified Goal)** — The COP29 (2024) climate-finance goal: ≥$300 billion/year by 2035 from developed countries, within a broader $1.3 trillion/year aspiration. Replaces the $100B goal.
- **Petrostate** — A state whose fiscal base and geopolitical weight depend heavily on hydrocarbon exports; faces national-scale asset-stranding risk in the transition.
- **$100 billion pledge** — The 2009 Copenhagen commitment by developed countries to mobilize $100B/year by 2020; met late and contested, damaging North–South trust.
